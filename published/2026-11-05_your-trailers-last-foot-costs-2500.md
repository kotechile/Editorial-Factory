<!-- lead -->
A tractor-trailer can cross a scale at the 80,000-pound cap and still fail federal weight law, because the binding limit is a distance between axle groups [1][2]. The Federal Highway Administration (FHWA) publishes a worked example of that failure. In it, a five-axle rig with 34,000 pounds on the drive tandem and 34,000 on the trailer tandem passes the total-weight and tandem ceilings. Its four-axle trailer group then carries 68,000 pounds against a 65,500-pound limit, because the first drive axle and the last trailer axle sit 35 feet apart instead of 36 [3]. Your trailer's last foot of wheelbase costs 2,500 pounds of freight.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Federal law sets three ceilings: 80,000 pounds gross, 34,000 on a tandem, and 20,000 on a single axle [1][2]. A planner who checks all three and finds them clear has still not proved the load legal. Two tandems at their ceiling leave 12,000 pounds for the steering axle, and that figure is a remainder rather than a rule. A trailer is also two capacities at once, floor space and legal weight, and the weight side is where [the same capacity arithmetic that sizes a pick face](/your-golden-zone-costs-10-miles/) meets a tape measure.

I keep coming back to the spacing, because it is the check a load plan leaves out. The fourth limit is the bridge formula. For any group of two or more consecutive axles, it sets a ceiling from the number of axles and the distance between the outermost pair, then rounds to the nearest 500 pounds [3]. Spread the group over a longer span and it may carry more; pack it into a shorter one and the ceiling falls.

The test covers interior groups, not just the whole truck, and FHWA names axles one through three, one through five, and two through five as the critical ones [3]. The last of those is the trailer bridge. At 97 inches of spacing the bridge table allows 38,000 pounds on a pair, above the cap that governs spacing up to 96 inches [3].

In FHWA's example the short group clears at 46,000 pounds against a 51,000 ceiling, and the full span clears at 80,000 against 80,000 [3]. The trailer bridge then breaks: 68,000 pounds against 65,500 at 35 feet. The agency's remedy is blunt, remove some load or increase the spacing. Federal law carries one narrow relief, under which two consecutive sets of tandem axles may each carry 34,000 pounds if the first and last axle sit at least 36 feet apart [3]. Without that relief the formula allows only 66,000 to 67,500 pounds at 36 to 38 feet.

A state may not enforce below these federal figures on the Interstate System, even when a portable scale reads against the carrier [4]. That makes the numbers a floor under enforcement as well as a ceiling on weight. A ticket written slightly over the cap is an argument about method, not a settled debt.

## By the numbers

- **4,000 pounds — The allowance:** five percent of a full legal load is the widest tolerance a portable scale may produce, and such scales are usually within 2 or 3 percent of true weight [2].
- **500 pounds per inch — The tire floor:** a state may not limit tire loading below 500 pounds per inch of tread width, and may not apply that limit to steering-axle tires [4].
- **1,000 pounds — The threshold that is not a tolerance:** a fine schedule with no penalty below a set figure counts as an unauthorized tolerance provision, whatever the figure [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

- **Trailer tandems:** sliding them forward shortens the trailer bridge and lowers the group ceiling; sliding them back raises it and shifts weight off the drives. It is one setting, and it belongs on the routing sheet beside the pickup time.
- **Spec-sheet wheelbase:** a shorter trailer bought for turning radius can leave the first and last axle under the spacing a full load assumes, with no change in the freight.
- **Weight tickets:** a ticket stands on the portable scale's accuracy band, so the printout and the weighing method carry the argument, not the number alone.
- **Oversize permits:** non-divisible loads can be permitted outside the axle and total-weight limits [2]. That conversation starts with the permit, not with a heavier tractor.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that this is a records problem before it is a physics problem. The spacing lives on the trailer's spec sheet and in dispatch, the weight lives on the invoice, and the two rarely meet in one field. A planner holding the tare, the axle ratings and the outer-bridge distance can work out the legal payload before anything is loaded.

Two limits apply. The 12,000-pound steering figure assumes both tandems are full, and a real load almost never sits there, because distribution is the planner's choice. The examples are FHWA's, not a measurement of any reader's fleet, and the tare has to come from the reader's own registration.

The practical version is smaller than a compliance program. One distance, from the first drive axle to the last trailer axle, tells whether a trailer may carry the 34,000 pounds on each tandem that a full load assumes. When it cannot, the plan is wrong before anyone argues about the rate.

## At a glance

- **The shift:** the binding limit on a loaded trailer is a spacing measurement, not the reading on the scale.
- **The number:** one foot between the drive and trailer tandems is worth 2,500 pounds of freight.
- **The risk:** a load that meets every quoted ceiling can still be over the bridge limit and turned back at the ramp.
