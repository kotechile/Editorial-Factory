## The big picture:

Kress runs two price lists for one machine, and the sale page leads with the higher one. That page marks $3,199.98 down to $1,699.99 and calls it a saving of $1,499.99 [3]. The model page for the same mower marks $2,499.99 down to $1,249.99, and its only call to action is a dealer locator [1]. The gap between the two regular prices is $699.99, which is also the value the sale page puts on the real-time kinematic (RTK) mapping cart it throws in [3].

I read the two pages side by side to confirm they sell identical hardware. Neither number comes from a direct marketplace checkout. Both pages belong to Kress, both were live the same afternoon, and the spread dictates where a buyer starts the conversation.

## By the numbers

- **$2,499.98 — Cost per rated acre:** the KR172's half-acre price over its half-acre rating, against $4,203.96 for the quarter-acre KR161 and $4,862.16 for the Luba mini 2 [1][2][4].
- **$199.00 — The half-acre step:** the half-acre Kress lists $199 above the quarter-acre one, so on this sale the bigger machine is the cheaper buy per acre [1][2].
- **3.1 charges — One pass at rated area:** Mammotion claims 0.12 acre of cutting per charge on a 6.1 ampere-hour (Ah) pack, so its 0.37 acre rating needs about three charges, not one [4].
- **40% — The slope ceiling:** the KR172's top slope against Mammotion's claimed 80%, the one row where the smaller machine is the stronger one [1][4].

## What I'd watch:

The acreage numbers measure different things. Kress prints area by cadence, 0.5 acre if the machine cuts every 48 hours and 0.7 acre if it runs every 72 [1]. Mammotion prints area per charge instead, and calls 0.37 acre the lawn it suits [4]. Both read as ceilings, which is the [rating-versus-usable-acreage trap](/your-1-acre-mowers-04-acre-trap/) this desk has written up before: the number assumes the machine gets the weather and daylight it wants.

Steering depends on something the buyer does not own. Kress sells "No antennas. No beacons. No boundary wires", then lists "Dead reckoning for mowing even undershade (weak satellite signal)" in the same feature block, and its box holds an RTK antenna [1]. The brand tells buyers to verify network coverage at a dealer before relying on the signal [1]. Mammotion answers the same problem with onboard sensing and sells "No Wire, No Antenna: 360° LiDAR + Dual Camera AI Vision" [4].

The physical limits are not close. The KR172 is rated to a 40% slope, 59 decibels perceived and 26.48 pounds. The Luba mini 2 is rated to 80% and gives a ceiling below 65 decibels [1][4]. A ceiling is not a measured figure, and a perceived level is not a sound-lab reading, so both stay claims.

The model page's call to action is a dealer locator rather than a checkout, which makes the lower figure a number to argue from. One authorized dealer listed it at the model page's figure and had none in stock, and a second listed it at the sale price [5][6]. The offer also says "while supplies last", and it closes on 31 December 2026 [3].

## The catch

What I find hard to square is the premium. The Luba mini 2 asks $549.01 more than the KR172 and rates 0.13 acre less. That money buys onboard sensing and the steeper slope rating, not more lawn [1][4]. A roundup by another publisher puts the tested end of this class at $1,099 for a 0.3-acre LiDAR unit, so the Kress sale sits mid-market rather than cheap [7].

The one cost a buyer controls is the 36% spread between the sale page and the model page, and closing it takes a written quote rather than a screenshot. I am watching how these brands handle battery replacements next, as neither maker lists a spare pack price on these pages. The year-four cost in any payback sum stays missing on both.

## What we did not test

This review rests on maker spec and price pages, two dealer listings and one roundup. Neither unit was handled here, nothing was installed, and no cutting, noise or steering was measured by this desk. Every acre, slope and sound claim belongs to the maker or to the reviewer named beside it.
