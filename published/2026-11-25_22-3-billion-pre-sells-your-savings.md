# $22.3 Billion Pre-Sells Your Savings

<!-- lead -->

Symbotic closed its fiscal year with $22.3 billion of booked robot work already signed [1]. Four days earlier, Procter & Gamble told investors it will remove 20–30% of its product-supply workforce by 2030 as machines take over the manual jobs [4]. The automation for the next decade is largely sold. The headcount it was priced against is already a budget line. If your payback case still counts on wage inflation, it was built for a market both announcements say is ending.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

This article reads Symbotic's $22.3 billion contracted backlog against Procter & Gamble's plan to cut up to 30% of its supply staff by 2030. It concerns operations leaders signing long robot deals, and it argues the savings those deals assume are now planned cuts.

<!-- tension -->

## Signed before the cuts

The dates landed inside one week. Symbotic filed its annual report on November 23, 2026, covering a fiscal year that ended September 26 [1]. Its backlog — unperformed work under existing contracts, in the company's own words — stood at $22.3 billion, mostly warehouse systems not yet built [1]. About a sixth becomes revenue next fiscal year [1]. The same day, the earnings call added nearly $7 billion more, set to enter the booked orders next quarter through the new Walmart deal [3].

The contracts behind that number go back almost a decade. Walmart signed a master agreement with Symbotic in 2017, widened it in 2021 to 25 distribution centers, then extended it in 2022 to cover all 42 [1]. That one buyer now holds about 47% of the booked orders and 87% of this year's revenue [1]. The pricing is built to protect the seller, too: the company says the backlog is structured to hold its gross profit targets even if inflation or supply-chain prices climb [1].

On November 19, Procter & Gamble put the buyer side on the record. Luc Reynaert, its chief product supply officer, told investors that 20% to 30% of the product-supply workforce will be gone by 2030, contractors included, as manual work gives way to machines [4]. The company already runs autonomous production at more than 15 sites and plans to scale that during the fiscal year ending June 30, 2027 [4]. The same presentation promised to free employees "for higher-value work" [4]. That promise now shares a page with a 20–30% headcount cut. Two descriptions of one workforce, and only one of them is a plan.

I read the two filings as one trade seen from each end. The labor savings behind a decade of robots were priced in the shortage years, when wages kept rising and workers kept leaving. What is being delivered now is different: headcount falling by plan, at a named buyer, on a dated schedule. A payback case built on wage inflation assumes a labor line that keeps costing more. The new reality is a line that shrinks on purpose. Once the cut lands, the savings stop compounding — and the capital is still owed.

## By the numbers

- **$22.3 billion:** Symbotic's booked backlog at fiscal year-end, mostly systems not yet delivered [1].
- **$3.57 billion:** the backlog slice that becomes revenue next fiscal year, by my division of the company's figures [1].
- **47% of backlog:** held by Walmart, which also supplied 87% of this year's revenue [1].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch

The people closest to this are re-reading contracts, not celebrating. One buyer carries nearly half the booked orders [1], so I'd watch whether next year's revenue slips as install crews run out of Walmart sites to convert. The second question is renewal pricing. The seller's margin is protected through inflation [1], which means a buyer whose labor savings fade cannot expect the contract to share the pain. This desk has argued before that [the wage floor you assume sets the robot's payback](/wage-floor-sets-robot-payback/) — and that floor is now falling on the buyer's side of the ledger. I would re-run any payback case on a labor line being cut by plan before signing anything that long. Exits stay hard after signing: an industry survey in late October put 94% of buyers calling a switch prohibitively expensive [5]. The labor pool itself is thinning on the government's count too [6].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My reading, held loosely: neither document proves what the pairing implies. The backlog records demand already signed; it says nothing about whether the promised productivity arrives, and the filing warns that contracts can be adjusted for inflation and revalidation [1]. Procter & Gamble's cut is a plan running to 2030, not a done deal, and the productivity projection is the company's own [4]. What both documents do establish is direction. Capital is contracted forward; headcount is committed down. Whether the savings hold at contract prices, or fade after the first cuts, is a question neither company has to answer. The buyer pays either way.
