The national mechanic's lien index fell to 47 in the third quarter, matching its 2022 low, after remodeling spending dropped 13.0 percent in a single month [4][2]. Fewer liens is supposed to mean the trades are getting paid. The small-business failure count points the other way. Chapter 11 elections for firms of that size ran 46 percent more than in the same stretch of 2025, and the ones filing are the size of the contractors bidding your kitchen [5].

<!-- tension -->

## Why a falling lien count lies

A mechanic's lien is the claim a supplier or subcontractor records against a property when the work it furnished goes unpaid, and the clock that decides how long it survives is [the one form most homeowners never file](/mechanics-lien-clock-payment-release/). The credit agency that compiles the measure counts those recordings across the country and publishes an index where 50 is neutral [4]. The index fell to 47 from a revised 52 in the second quarter and 56 a year earlier, after 58 in the first quarter of 2026 [4].

I read the publisher's own caveat three times before I trusted the headline. The same page that reports the drop says a lower score "may reflect improved payment conditions, fewer projects reaching lien-triggering stages, or both" [4]. That sentence is doing more work than the number above it, because the index cannot tell those two stories apart and the people who compile it say so themselves.

The spending data picks one. Home building ran at $848.8 billion in September, down 5.5 percent from August [1]. Remodeling, the line inside that total that feeds lien claims, dropped 13.0 percent in September [2]. Fewer jobs started means fewer jobs reach the stage where a lien is the last resort, so the count falls for reasons that have nothing to do with on-time payment. The regions with the biggest drops fit that pattern: the West at 38 and the Midwest at 32 sit at multi-year lows, while the South, where building has held up best, is the only region still above neutral at 60 [4].

## By the numbers

- **18.0% — Remodeling's annual drop:** improvement spending in September, on top of the one-month fall, was down 18.0 percent over the year [2].
- **276 and 2,442 — Small-business reorganizations:** Subchapter V elections ran 38 percent above September 2025 for the month, and reached 2,442 in the first nine months of 2026, up from 1,672 [5].
- **47 — Lien index in Q3:** ten percent below the prior quarter and 16 percent below a year ago, the weakest reading since 2022 [4].

## Where the money stops between draws

I look at the payment schedule before I look at the price. A remodel is paid in stages: a deposit, draws against work in place, and a final payment held until sign-off. In a growing market a contractor carries a thin margin between draws because the next job is already signed. In a shrinking market, the deposit on your job becomes the working cash for the last one.

That mechanism sits behind a number that can look unrelated. Subchapter V elections, the debt workout Congress built for small firms inside Chapter 11, are the reorganizations a contractor can use when the cash between draws runs out [5]. The credit agency behind the lien index describes that group as firms that "can include subcontractors and suppliers who depend on getting paid on time" [4]. That is the closest the public data gets to naming the contractors a homeowner is likely to hire.

State enforcement is not the backstop it appears to be. California's licensing board closed 39 stings and sweeps in a 15-state push and produced 73 actions [6]. Those catch people working without a license. They do not recover a deposit from a licensed firm that stops paying its subs.

## The catch

I hold my reading loosely because the same number can mean the opposite. Some of this may be better payment: a firm with a signed backlog has less reason to stretch a deposit, and the index's own keepers leave that door open. The Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies (JCHS) still expects annual remodeling spending near $525 billion through the third quarter of 2027, which is flat rather than fallen, and its projected 1.5 percent growth is positive [3].

The bankruptcy leg is the softer one, too. Epiq counts every small firm that elects Subchapter V, not contractors alone, and it publishes no building share. What it shows is a direction and a size across the economy rather than a count of failed remodelers, and the lien count and the bankruptcy count are two gauges reading the same stress from opposite ends of a job.
