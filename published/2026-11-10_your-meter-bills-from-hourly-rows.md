<!-- lead -->
Your meter bills from hourly rows, and those rows are yours to keep. In 2022 the U.S. had about 119 million advanced electric meters, roughly 72% of the total, and each one logged a house at no finer than hourly steps while sending the record to the utility and to the owner at least once a day [1]. One service yields 8,760 hours of history a year at that detail. The file answers a seasonal cost question for free, and a clamp on the panel answers a different one.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** The hourly record a utility meter already keeps now reaches the owner as a standard file.
- **The number:** 8,760 — the hourly rows one service produces in a year.
- **The risk:** The file stops at the service entrance, so it never names a circuit.

<!-- tension -->

## A meter built to settle bills

Green Button started in January 2012, when more than 50 utilities and power suppliers agreed to publish customer usage in one standard file [2]. Their pledges covered over 60 million homes and businesses [2]. The live count is smaller and far more useful: over 40 million homes and firms can now pull their own record through a channel the program calls Connect My Data [3].

I think the gap between those two counts is the most useful thing on either page. One is a promise a utility made, the other is a working download, so the space between them measures how much of the promise got built.

The coarse detail is not a cost cut. It is what the meter is for: a revenue meter settles a bill, so it is built to be right across a billing period rather than rich inside one. The Energy Information Administration says the advanced class logs use at hourly steps at the least and gives the data to the utility and the owner once a day at the least [1]. Hourly is the floor, a day is the delay, and that pair is a poor fit for anyone who wants to watch a house live.

A bill is one number; the file is the same use cut into steps. That sounds like a small change, and for a monthly total it is. It matters for the questions a total cannot hold: which part of the day the house is busiest, whether a rate with a cheap night window would beat the flat one, and whether the floor of every row — the load that never turns off — rose after a new box went into the rack. None of those needs a permit or a purchase.

## By the numbers

- **72% of U.S. electric meters:** The share that were advanced meters in 2022, when 119 million of them logged interval data [1].
- **40 million customers:** The accounts able to pull their own usage through Connect My Data today [3].
- **60 million homes and businesses:** The reach that the 2012 pledges said the program would serve [2].

## What the free file settles

Season against season is the question most owners carry, and a downloaded year answers it with no new hardware. The same month across two years, set beside a hot or a cold season, tells a house that got dearer apart from a year that got colder. What I find telling is how seldom that step comes before a purchase: the portal shows one monthly total, and the buyer assumes the detail was never kept rather than never pulled.

The gap between the two tools is a number, not a matter of taste. One year of hourly rows is 8,760 lines; one year at a sample a second is 31,536,000, or about 3,600 times as many. Almost no home question needs that second file, and the ones that do are narrow.

Where the free file runs out is inside the panel. An hourly whole-house number cannot say which circuit drew the power, and that is the one question a clamp answers, so [a panel monitor's own limits are worth reading first](/monitor-77-percent-guess-where-it-fails/). The two tools do different jobs rather than compete: the meter for cost and trend, the clamps for the name of the circuit and for anything that happens inside an hour.

That split shows up in the hardware itself. A dryer, an oven and a heat pump sit on 240-volt circuits, and a whole-house row sees their total and nothing else; it cannot tell a dryer from a heat pump running at the same moment. A clamp per branch turns that single line into a column per appliance, which is a different kind of evidence and costs a different kind of money — a panel of clamps, an electrician for an hour, and a place to store the readings that is not a vendor's cloud.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

Two limits stand. The first is time: a record sent once a day cannot raise an alarm inside the day it measures, so a burst pipe or a failing pump turns up the next morning instead of the moment it starts. The second is reach. A program that covers 40 million accounts is not every meter in the country, and an owner outside it has a meter with no file, which no amount of reading changes [3].

I read the export as the lasting half of the local-first case. A standard file is an archive the owner holds, while a portal is a view a utility can retire when it changes vendors. The download is the part that survives that day.
