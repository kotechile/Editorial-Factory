<!-- lead -->
The pitch for a heated snow mat is its running cost, quoted by HeatTrak as about $0.18 an hour [4]. My read is that the hourly figure, and the $1.80 it becomes across a ten-hour snowfall, are the least useful numbers on that page. The mat behind it draws 5.8 amps at 120 volts, which is 696 watts spread across 20 square feet and held on for as long as the pavement has to stay bare [2]. That works out to 34.8 watts for every square foot, and at a list price of $1,075 for the same 20 square feet, to $53.75 a square foot of hardware [2][5]. A 400-square-foot driveway therefore prices out at about $21,500 of mat before anyone touches a wire. Run 200 square feet of mat through a six-hour storm and the electricity alone comes to roughly $7.23 at the 2025 U.S. average residential rate of 17.30 cents a kilowatt-hour [2][6]. A two-stage battery blower's whole charge — two 7.5 amp-hour packs at 56 volts, 840 watt-hours — costs 14.5 cents on that same rate, about 50 times less [1][6].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

My read starts with what each number leaves out. Snow removal is sold with two different denominators, and each vendor picks the one that flatters its product. The mat maker talks about hours, because the mat sips power next to a furnace or a hot tub. The blower maker talks about capacity, because its spec sheet can claim an 18-car driveway cleared through 8 inches of snow on one charge [1]. Neither number is false, and neither settles the purchase.

The denominator that does settle it is square footage, because that is the axis on which the two products stop being comparable. HeatTrak sells its driveway mat as one tire track, 24 inches wide and 10 feet long, and says so plainly: the mats "lay as a track for one tire" [4]. Covering a 400-square-foot driveway the way an embedded system covers it means 20 of those mats. At 13 amps per ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) power unit, you are also planning where nine separate plugs live [2][4].

The blower's denominator is energy, and it is almost embarrassingly small. Melting is latent-heat work: the mat's own wattage and its stated ceiling of 2 inches per hour put the cost of melting one square foot of one-inch snow at about 17.4 watt-hours [2][4]. The blower's full 840-watt-hour charge therefore holds roughly the energy needed to melt 48 square feet of one-inch snow, or about 6 square feet of an 8-inch storm [1]. The same charge clears the maker's claimed driveway, because the machine throws the snow instead of melting it. Moving snow is mechanical work, while melting it is latent-heat work, which is why a mat has to be plugged in for hours to do what one charge does in a pass.

## By the numbers

- **$53.75 a square foot — mat hardware:** the 24-inch by 10-foot driveway mat lists at $1,075 and covers 20 square feet, while the 20-inch by 60-inch walkway mat lists at $229.99 and covers 8.3 square feet, or $27.60 a square foot [2][3].
- **14.5 cents — one full blower charge:** two 7.5 amp-hour packs at 56 volts hold 840 watt-hours, which is 0.84 kilowatt-hours at the 2025 residential average of 17.30 cents [1][6].
- **34.8 watts a square foot — mat load:** 5.8 amps at 120 volts across 20 square feet, against 50 watts a square foot as the design density for an embedded system, which puts a 400-square-foot pour at 20 kilowatts [2][5].
- **$7.23 against 14.5 cents — one storm:** 200 square feet of mat held on for six hours draws about 42 kilowatt-hours, roughly 50 times the cost of the blower's charge for the same snow [2][6].

## What I'd watch:

I have been watching buyers who get this right, and they are not shopping for the cheapest machine. They match the mat to the strip of pavement they refuse to shovel, and let a machine with wheels handle anything larger.

- **Track coverage over full coverage:** the vendors themselves quote tire-track systems as the cheaper option than heating a whole slab, and a portable mat is a track by construction [4][5].
- **Circuit room first:** a power unit caps at 13 amps, which allows about 45 square feet of mat per plug, and an embedded 400-square-foot system draws 20 kilowatts, or roughly 83 amps at 240 volts — the sort of load the [80 percent panel test](/panel-upgrade-80-percent-test/) exists to settle [2][4][5].
- **The replacement clock:** HeatTrak puts mat life at 5 to 6 years, the number that turns a $21,500 hardware bill into an annual cost rather than a one-time one [4].
- **Pack count on the blower side:** the same 24-inch machine is sold bare, with two 7.5 amp-hour packs, with two 10 amp-hour packs, and with four packs, which is the maker's own admission that the single-charge claim depends on which box you bought [1].

## The catch

What strikes me here is that the hourly figure is honest and still misleading. The electricity itself is genuinely small: a six-hour storm on a 100-square-foot walk costs about $3.61 at the 2025 average, and the vendor's own worked example puts an embedded system of that size at $6.00 on a 20-cent rate [4][5]. What the hourly figure hides is the timing, because a mat has to run before the snow arrives, so the clock starts with the forecast rather than the first flake [4].

I would not talk anyone out of a mat where the requirement is a bare, dry threshold. A step, a ramp or a path an elderly parent uses is hard for any shovel to reach cleanly, and $7 of electricity against a fall is not a close call. The arithmetic says the mat is the wrong tool for a driveway, not that it is a bad product.

Three constraints sit on the numbers here. The prices are list prices as of October 24, 2026, and mat vendors discount seasonally. The rate is the 2025 annual U.S. residential average, and the same page shows a 4.4-fold spread across states, from 35.72 cents in Hawaii to 8.20 cents in North Dakota, so the same storm bill runs nearer $14.93 in Hawaii [6]. The melt arithmetic also uses the mat's best case of 2 inches an hour, and a slower melt stretches the energized hours and the bill with them. This desk has no hardware and tested nothing, so every figure above comes from a manufacturer's published page or the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), refigured where it is a ratio.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Heated mats are sold on running cost while their real price is per square foot of hardware and per amp of circuit, which is why a 400-square-foot driveway in portable mat prices at about $21,500 against $4,000 to $10,000 for an embedded system at the vendors' own $10 to $25 a square foot installed [2][5].
- **Why It Matters:** Melting snow takes roughly 17.4 watt-hours per square foot per inch, so a two-stage battery blower's whole charge is worth about 48 square feet of melt — the mat is a threshold tool rather than a driveway tool, and buying it as one is where the money goes.
- **What I'd Watch:** the square footage a mat is actually asked to keep bare, and the circuit it needs.
  - **Track coverage:** heating only the tire tracks instead of the whole slab, which the vendors price as the cheaper choice and which cuts the wattage in proportion.
  - **Amps per plug:** a 13-amp cap per power unit works out at about 45 square feet of mat on one 120-volt circuit.
  - **Melt rate:** the published ceiling of 2 inches an hour is the best case, and slower melts run the meter longer.
- **The Catch:** prices are list prices as of October 24, 2026, the rate is the 2025 U.S. residential average of 17.30 cents with a 4.4-fold state spread, and nothing here was tested on hardware.
