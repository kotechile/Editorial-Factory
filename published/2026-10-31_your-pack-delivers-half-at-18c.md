Your pack delivers about half its rated capacity at -18°C, and the shortfall comes from the cold cells rather than the machine. A pack rated at 100 percent capacity at 27°C (80°F) usually gives only 50 percent at -18°C (0°F) [1]. Cold slows the chemistry inside the cell, which cuts the energy it can hand over. EGO (the outdoor power equipment brand) builds its 24-inch two-stage around two recommended 7.5 amp-hour packs [2]. Together they hold 840 watt-hours in mild air and roughly 420 when the packs are cold. The blower that clears a wide driveway in December is the same machine in January, but it runs on less than half the energy it was sold with.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Makers rate packs at a warm 27°C because the numbers look best there, and the label never says how far the run time falls in winter air [1]. Heat beyond that level costs longevity. A pack run at 40°C loses 40 percent of its cycle life against one kept near room temperature [1]. 

Cold adds a second effect that a fuel gauge cannot show. The extra internal resistance drops voltage when the motor pulls hard, so the tool shuts off early before the charge is gone [1]. Electric vehicles (EVs) show the same thing on a larger scale. Cold cuts their fuel economy about 39 percent in mixed driving and their range by 41 percent [4]. About two-thirds of that extra energy goes to heating the cabin [4]. 

What I keep circling is that the fix here is heat, not capacity. Some advanced chargers refuse to charge a lithium pack below freezing [3], which is the cell setting the limit rather than the motor.

## By the numbers

- **27°C — the rating temperature:** every run-time figure on the box is measured at 80°F, and the label never says how far the number moves in colder air [1].
- **0.02C — the charge rate at -30°C:** the rate research allows at that temperature is so low that one charge would stretch past 50 hours, a wait the source calls impractical [3].
- **12 to 15 years — pack life in a mild climate:** modeling from the National Laboratory of the Rockies puts the same packs at 8 to 12 years in an extreme climate, which covers heat as well as cold [5].
- **40 percent — the cycle-life cost of heat:** a pack run at 40°C instead of room temperature loses 40 percent of its cycle life, so where it is stored matters too [1].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The garage is the failure point, not the driveway. A pack left in an unheated shed or an attached garage sits at air temperature, and a January cold snap holds it below freezing for weeks [3]. Charging it there is the expensive move. Below 0°C a consumer lithium-ion pack cannot take a charge without plating metallic lithium onto the anode [3]. The pack looks like it is charging normally the whole time.

- **The charge window:** the best range is 10°C to 30°C, so a pack that comes in from the cold needs an hour indoors first [3].
- **The pack in the machine:** a warm pack carried out to a cold blower gives back some of what the label promised, then cools toward the air temperature while the machine sits between passes [1].
- **The spare:** rotating two packs through an indoor charge beats running one down and topping it up in a cold garage, and it keeps the spare away from the temperature that shortens its life [1].

What I am watching next is whether winter equipment brands start printing a cold-capacity number beside the run time. The rest of the desk's coverage of these machines sits under [outdoor power and snow equipment](/categories/outdoor-power-equipment/).

## The catch

The half-capacity figure is a lab discharge at one rate on one chemistry, and the source says the momentary capacity decrease differs by chemistry [1]. A reader whose storms run warmer than -18°C loses less, though the source gives no figure for a specific temperature. The arithmetic here is about the pack, not a guarantee for one specific driveway. 

The climate band is extreme climates, which includes heat, and it is predictive modeling rather than a warranty term [5]. A bigger pack does buy more cold run time, and that is not wrong. It just buys that time at the pack's cold efficiency instead of its label rating, a poor trade if an hour indoors solves the same problem. 

My view is that the two things worth keeping apart are the temporary half and the permanent damage. A pack charged below freezing suffers permanent degradation, and nothing on the charger tells you it happened [3].
