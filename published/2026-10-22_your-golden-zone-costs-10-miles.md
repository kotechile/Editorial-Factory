<!-- lead -->
The bill for a full week of stock in a prime pick face gets paid twice: the forklift crew makes one drop a week, and the pickers on that aisle walk an extra 10 miles in the same seven days [1]. That is what your golden zone costs, the waist-to-shoulder shelf space every warehouse treats as free. The number that sets the price is one most slotting lists never carry: how many days of stock the face holds [1].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Slotting gets argued as a sorting problem. Rank items by how often they are picked, pull the fast ones to the front, push the slow ones out [2]. My read is that this ranking hides the bill. A pick face is a box with a fixed size, and size is what turns a rank into a walk.

The spread is brutal. A fifth of the catalogue carries four-fifths of the picks, by the trade press's own rule of thumb [2]. So the good shelves are rationed before anyone plans a move, and every slow item parked on a prime face spends space the fast movers cannot use.

The cost base is big enough to matter. Picking is about half of a consumer-goods warehouse's running cost [4]. Walking is 40% or more of that pick time [2]. That puts walking among the largest lines in the building, long before anyone argues about a rate card.

What changed is the cost of moving stock. Re-slotting once meant rebuilding racking. Our own field notes have a 3PL (third-party logistics provider) re-slotting a high-bay site in days, not months, once its fleet went mobile. Faces can be resized over a weekend now, which kills the old excuse for leaving a face at whatever pallet quantity shows up on the truck.

## By the numbers

- **10 miles — Face-size cost:** A face holding a week of stock needs one pallet drop a week and adds 10 miles of picker walking in that week. A face holding an hour of stock reverses both, and swamps the refill crew [1].
- **40%+ — Walking share:** Walk time runs at 40% or more of total pick time in the order-picking area, the zone carrying the most labour in the building [2].
- **80% of picks — 20% of items:** Four-fifths of pick activity comes from a fifth of the catalogue, which is the whole case for reserving prime faces and resizing everything else [2].
- **$17.95 — Picker-hour:** The median wage for stockers and order fillers in the 2025 release, $37,330 a year. One picker-minute prices at about 30 cents [3].

## What I'd watch:

- **The report that carries the opening size:** The sizing method starts with a pick-frequency report that also lists each slot's opening. That lets a planner compute days of stock per face instead of guessing at it [1]. This is the number the ranking is missing.
- **Quarterly re-reads, not annual ones:** One consultant's advice is to re-check the slotting rules and settings at least quarterly, because the business and the perception of it drift apart [1]. The same piece puts a 30% travel cut on the table for an unoptimised 1,000-item pick line [1].
- **Joint-order frequency beside velocity:** A 2025 capstone model scored weight, pick frequency and how often items are ordered together. It estimated a 24% cut in travel distance in one case-pick area [4]. Two items that leave together belong together, whatever their own ranks say.
- **The forecast gap:** One vendor executive says fewer than 10% of its customers can re-slot ahead of a demand lift [1]. So most of this work lands after the promotion has already hit.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The 24% is a model of one site's case-pick area, not a measured result after a live re-slot [4]. The miles-versus-pallets case is a decade-old field example [1]. What strikes me is that neither number turns into money on its own. Miles become dollars only through your own walking pace, which no source here supplies. The honest version of the rule is a formula: miles saved, divided by pace, times the wage.

There is a second limit. Refill labour does not vanish when a face shrinks. It moves to the lift driver, and it arrives in smaller, more frequent drops, which is its own cost when the aisles are busy. And where the warehouse system allows only one slot per item, the face holds the entire stock, so the plan is bounded by software the slotting exercise did not choose [1].

That is the part I keep circling. A slotting list reads like a plan about where things go. In practice it is a plan about how far people walk and how often trucks stop [1][4].

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Slotting is being run as a ranking exercise when the deciding number is how many days of stock each pick face holds. That number sets walking against refill work.
- **Why It Matters:** Picking is roughly half of warehouse running cost and walking is 40% or more of pick time, so a face sized to a full week of stock moves real labour cost from one crew to another.
- **What I'd watch:** Whether slotting plans start carrying each face's size, and how often the ranking is re-read.
  - **Days of stock:** A face's stock divided by its daily pick rate. This is the one number that prices a slot's walking.
  - **Joint-order frequency:** How often two items ride the same order, which decides whether they belong in neighbouring faces.
  - **Re-slot cadence:** How often the ranking is recomputed. A face sized for last season's velocity keeps charging this season's pickers.
- **The Catch:** The travel cut is modelled at one site and the miles case is a decade old, so the arithmetic finishes with the reader's own pace and wage.
