<!-- lead -->
Walmart held an earnings call on Aug. 20. The chief financial officer (CFO) shared a huge number. The retailer claimed about $2.9 billion in tariff refunds. The Supreme Court struck down these taxes in February. They were International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) tariffs. The refund equals roughly 0.5% of Walmart's annual United States (U.S.) net sales. Most of this cash had already arrived. Calcbench tallied the data on Aug. 28. This massive figure was missing from earnings releases. It did not appear in any investor deck. It was absent from every Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) filing. The check exists only as spoken words on a recording. [3]

The pattern repeats across 101 public companies. These firms disclosed about $18.7 billion in gross refunds by late August. The eleven biggest importers claimed roughly $12 billion of that total. [3]

<!-- tension -->
The Supreme Court ruled on Feb. 20, 2026. The case was Learning Resources v. Trump. A 6-3 majority declared the president cannot use IEEPA to impose tariffs. This voided the "Liberation Day" and fentanyl-related levies. [1] 

A court ruling does not instantly generate cash. The government had collected roughly $166 billion in IEEPA duties. [2] Customs and Border Protection (CBP) built a new system. They call it the Customs Automated Payment Exchange (CAPE). By June 29, CBP accepted about $104 billion for processing. The agency sent about $71 billion to the Treasury for payment. [2] Penn Wharton projects the full clawback could reach $175 billion. [6]

This money is landing unevenly. The biggest importers are cashing checks first. Caterpillar added $392 million to its second-quarter operating profit. This money came from expected IEEPA recoveries. [5] Deere booked $382 million for the year so far. This covered its fiscal third quarter ending Aug. 2. [4] 

Getting paid requires moving fast. Companies must clear Phase I and Phase II of the government refund rollout. Phase I covers shipments not yet processed. Phase II covers shipments undergoing final cost adjustments. These two groups cover roughly $130 billion of the duties. [2]

The remaining money sits in limbo. Phase III covers imports already fully processed. The government has not scheduled this phase yet. CBP held a hearing on June 9. The agency signaled a strict limit on refunds. CBP plans to process only the company named on the import. That company must have also filed a formal legal objection. They had to file it before the Court of International Trade. [2] 

The Department of Justice (DOJ) has appealed the universal-refund order. The DOJ argues the trade court can only refund parties that actually sued. [2] Small importers might get nothing if they never filed an objection.

<!-- tactical-insight -->
Operations leaders should make three moves this quarter.

First, reopen contract talks with your big vendors. You need to discuss passing tariff costs to the buyer. Caterpillar got $392 million back in a quarter. Yet it still paid about $400 million in duties. [5] Deere projects a net tariff exposure of roughly $750 million. This covers fiscal year 2026 (FY26) after refunds. Deere assumes no further refund activity this year. [4] A supplier might book this cash as a windfall. You can claim it back as a price cut. You just have to ask. Frame the refund as shared savings. A vendor might report a slimmer tariff line on its next call. Treat that as your chance to negotiate.

Second, grade your own refund exposure today. Check your status if you are the company named on any IEEPA-paid import. Find out if the shipment was not yet processed. Check if it was a final cost adjustment. Those early shipments are already moving through the system. Your imports might be already fully processed. If you never filed a formal legal objection, you sit outside the current system. Your window to act depends on a two-year legal deadline. The start date for that clock remains unresolved. [2] Call your customs broker today to clarify your status.

Third, watch for hidden numbers. Companies frequently book these recoveries as a one-time accounting boost. This makes the cash easy to miss. The largest single recovery was disclosed only verbally on a call. Apple disclosed a roughly $2.19 billion impact through total sales profit. Several companies tucked the figure into unofficial accounting. They avoided putting it in a headline. [3] A supplier might report record profit margins. Ask what portion comes from one-time tariff refunds. Do this before you project their costs into next quarter.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
A refund is not a strategy. The IEEPA money provides a temporary windfall. It unwinds a tax the Supreme Court invalidated. But two other tariff regimes survived. Separate tariffs on steel, aluminum, and autos remain active. Separate tariffs on Chinese goods were not struck down. [2] 

Deere still expects roughly $1.1 billion in direct tariff expense for FY26. The company says its projected annual cost steps up toward $1 billion in 2027. [4] Big original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) and retailers are pocketing refunds this year. Yet the core tariff rules remain entirely in place. 

The clawback improves your cash position. It does not fix the baseline cost of your supply chain. Treat this as a short-term cash window. Maintain your strict sourcing discipline.
