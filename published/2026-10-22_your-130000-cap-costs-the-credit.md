Pick the foreign earned income exclusion and you kill the tax credit for foreign taxes paid on that exact slice of pay [6]. The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) treats this cap—set at $130,000 for 2025—as a straight trade rather than a free gift [2]. The credit it replaces has no fixed dollar ceiling, moves unused amounts forward for ten years, and can even be claimed on a return filed years later [4][5].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Tax rules sell these two tools as a simple choice, but they work in very different ways. The exclusion drops a specific slice of foreign pay from your return. The credit offers a direct dollar offset, bounded by the US tax on your foreign income [3][4].

My read is that most expats take the exclusion simply because the paperwork looks easier than the credit's hard math. That ease brings a real gain, but it hides a costly trap for high earners.

If your host country taxes that $130,000 at a higher rate than the US would, the exclusion throws away a credit worth more than the tax it saves. The cap decides how much pay sits on the losing side of this math. This makes the exclusion fine for contractors in low-tax countries, but costly for salaried workers in high-tax regions.

The two tools can actually work on different slices of income at the same time. High earners can exclude pay up to the $130,000 limit and then credit the foreign taxes paid on the income above it [6]. Unused credits also survive the year end, rolling back one year and forward ten [3][4].

## By the numbers

- **$130,000 — 2025 exclusion cap:** The top foreign earned income exclusion on the 2025 return rises from the past year because the law ties its $80,000 base to rising prices [1][2].
- **$39,000 — Housing relief limit:** The housing add-on caps out at 30% of the exclusion, yielding $39,000 for a full valid year or $106.85 per day [2].
- **10 years — Credit shelf life:** Unused foreign tax rolls back one year and forward ten, and this fixed span cannot stretch [3][4].
- **$300 — Small-claim forfeit:** Foreign taxes of $300 or less, $600 on a joint return, can be claimed without the full form, but that choice blocks moving that year's tax to any other year [3].

## What I'd watch:

- **The amended-return path:** Publication 514's own case is a filer who deducted 2014 foreign taxes and then, in February 2025, filed an amended 2014 return taking the credit instead, which the publication calls timely because it fell inside the ten-year window from that return's regular due date [5].
- **The stacking pattern:** Tax guidelines clearly show how high earners can exclude pay up to the limit, then credit the foreign tax on the rest of the income [6].
- **The revocation clock:** Revoking an exclusion is simple, but asking for it back inside five years requires a formal ruling request from the tax agency [6].
- **The short-form tail:** The simpler credit form works well for small foreign taxes, but it forever traps that year's tax from moving to any other year [3].

## The catch

The exclusion saves real time and money, and I have noticed filers happily trade potential credits just to avoid the math. The credit requires foreign-source sums, separate categories and apportioned costs before it ever yields a usable number [3].

Some costs stay tied together and never show up in a basic side-by-side view. Picking the exclusion rules out the additional child tax credit for that year [6]. The housing exclusion behaves the same way: electing it forfeits the credit on the housing amounts you exclude [6].

The honest limit is that these numbers rely on moving targets. Rates change, money values shift, and the foreign tax on an excluded slice usually needs splitting rather than reading straight off a payslip. Where the two tools come out close, the tiebreaker is usually the paperwork you feel willing to defend years later.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The foreign earned income exclusion acts as a straight trade, dropping a capped $130,000 slice of pay from the 2025 return while giving up the foreign tax credit on that exact money.
- **Why It Matters:** The tax credit carries no dollar limit and keeps unused amounts for ten years, meaning an exclusion taken in a high-tax country often costs more than it saves.
- **What I'd Watch:** The rules that set the final tax result for an expat already abroad.
  - **The amended-return window:** A ten-year timeline from the normal due date lets filers swap a deduction for a credit on an old return.
  - **The stacking route:** The method of excluding up to the cap and crediting the foreign tax on the rest of the pay keeps both tools active.
  - **The revocation clock:** A five-year wait and a formal ruling request stand between a revoked exclusion and a new choice.
- **The Catch:** The credit needs foreign-source math and separate categories that the exclusion skips entirely, which is why the exclusion is usually the better tool in a low-tax host country.
