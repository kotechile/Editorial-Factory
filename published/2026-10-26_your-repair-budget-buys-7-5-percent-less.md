Spending on home repairs is set to grow 1.5 percent while the goods behind those jobs are up 9.7 percent, so your repair budget's reach shrinks: it buys about 7.5 percent less work this year. That is a quiet squeeze, and neither report prints it — one number comes from Harvard's housing center on October 22 [1], the other from the federal wholesale price report on October 15 [2].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Two reports measured this squeeze from opposite ends this month, and neither one divided its number by the other.

Harvard's housing center sees spending growth of just 1.5 percent by the third quarter of 2027, down from 2.4 percent at the end of this year [1]. Yearly spending moves from $524 billion to $527 billion across those four quarters [1]. The federal price report put goods for home building at 355.2 in September against 323.9 a year earlier, and copper wire and cable at 485.5 against 394.7 [2][3].

One counts dollars spent. The other prices what those dollars buy. What strikes me here is that nobody prints the quotient, and the quotient is the number a family lives inside.

Shipments tell the same story from a third side. Gas warm-air furnaces shipped 287,710 units in August, down 4.3 percent from a year earlier [7]. Home water heaters fell 1.9 percent on gas and 1.6 percent on electric [7]. Cooling units rose 11.4 percent in that month, which is what a summer of fixes looks like beside a year of fewer new units [7].

The reason this lands now is that the fix is winning on price. Retail spending on building goods is flat to negative, and remodel permits have stalled [1]. That is the shape of owners holding old gear together instead of resetting it.

## By the numbers

- **1.5% — Spending growth:** Harvard's forecast for growth by the third quarter of 2027, down from 2.4 percent at the end of 2026 [1].
- **9.7% — Home-building goods:** wholesale prices for the goods in home work, at 355.2 in September against 323.9 a year earlier [2].
- **23.0% — Copper wire and cable:** the metal in panels, pipe and coils, at 485.5 against 394.7 [3].
- **7.5% — Less work per dollar:** spending growth of 1.5 percent divided by goods growth of 9.7 percent, this desk's math on the two rows above [1][2].

## What I'd watch:

The part I keep circling is the spread inside the basket. Steel mill products are up 27.6 percent and softwood lumber 14.2 percent, while plumbing fittings are up 5.1 percent [4][5][6]. A wiring job and a faucet swap do not carry the same risk, and one flat quote about costly goods hides that difference.

- **The trade mix:** copper and steel sit in the trades a panel, a condenser or a service line touches. Those are the jobs with the least room to swap in something cheaper [3][4].
- **The volume tell:** furnace and water-heater shipments fall while cooling units climb, which reads as owners paying to keep a system alive rather than to reset its clock [7].
- **The forecast's own slope:** spending growing 2.4 percent against goods up nearly 10 percent already works out to about 6.6 percent less work per dollar [1][2]. The forecast then eases further.
- **The labor line:** prices stop at the factory gate, so permits, truck rolls, labor and margin sit on top of those input prices [2]. A real quote runs wider.
- **The next print:** the two series to re-read are the January remodel release and the monthly price report. I'm watching whether permits stay flat while spending holds.

## The catch

A wholesale index is not a final quote. It prices what mills and factories received, before a contractor adds labor, permits and margin [2]. The home-building basket also includes new work, which is not the mix inside a retrofit [2].

The forecast is a forecast. Every figure in the October release looks four quarters ahead [1]. Its authors also note the government revised monthly retail sales in September, which moved earlier estimates by less than one percent [1].

My read is that the average is the right shape and the wrong detail. It is a reading across a basket, not a price for one roof.

A family that put in a furnace two years into a fifteen-year life should ignore it, because early replacement throws away real service life. The number bites on an asset near the end of its clock, where the choice is a price this year or a larger one after a failure.

That is why the reserve matters more than the purchase. A replacement date is the one line in a home budget that can be set years ahead. The math behind a [capital sinking fund](/calculators/capital-sinking-fund/) is just that future bill divided by the months between now and then [1].

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Spending on home repairs is set to grow 1.5 percent while the goods those jobs use are up 9.7 percent, so the same money buys about 7.5 percent less work each year.
- **Why It Matters:** The clock on a roof, a furnace or a water heater runs in years, not in budgets. Putting a job off does not make it go away; it lands later at a higher price, often as a crisis.
- **What I'd Watch:** Which part of the basket is moving.
  - **Copper-heavy trades:** wiring, pipe and coils, where the index is up 23 percent and swapping in a cheaper part is hard.
  - **Shipment counts:** falling furnace and water-heater units against rising cooling units show fixes crowding out new units.
  - **The forecast slope:** spending growth easing from 2.4 to 1.5 percent while goods prices hold near double digits.
- **The Catch:** A wholesale index leaves out labor and permits, its basket includes new build, and the average means nothing for a young asset.
