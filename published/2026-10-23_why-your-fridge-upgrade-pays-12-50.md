A new fridge carries a federal label promising about $150 in savings over twelve years, which breaks down to $12.50 a year [1][2]. At the 2025 average home rate of 17.30 cents per kilowatt-hour (kWh), that saving is worth 72 kWh of power [3]. So your fridge upgrade pays $12.50 a year, and that sum settles nothing about whether the old machine is worth keeping.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

My read starts with the label's own math, because the government page lists three different numbers that cloud the choice to repair or replace.

Certified fridges run about 9 percent leaner than models meeting the federal floor, pricing twelve years of that gap at roughly $150 [1][2]. The same page states an older unit draws about 20 percent more power than a certified one [1]. A third line puts fridges older than fifteen years at about $95 a year to run, which is a cost rather than a saving [1].

The baseline floor sits in the federal code, which keeps the real savings gap narrow. Current rules cap a standard top-mount unit with automatic defrost at 8.07 times its adjusted volume plus 233.7 kWh a year [5].

For a unit whose adjusted volume comes to 24.5 cubic feet, that cap works out to 431 kWh, costing $74.63 a year at the national home rate [3][5]. Every certified fridge must clear this tight legal limit, so the badge offsets about a sixth of the running cost rather than a large share of it.

The math shifts sharply when labor enters the frame. The median wage for a home appliance repairer stood at $24.51 an hour in 2025, tracking a yearly median of $50,990 [4].

One year of an old unit's power at $95 equals 3.9 hours of that median wage, while the entire twelve-year saving covers just 6.1 hours [2][4]. The repair quote and the power savings weigh against each other, leaving the heavy purchase price on a separate scale.

## By the numbers

- **$12.50 a year — Badge value:** Twelve years of the certified power drop totals roughly $150, making the yearly benefit about $12.50 [1][2].
- **72 kWh — What it buys:** At the 2025 average home rate of 17.30 cents per kWh, that $12.50 covers 72 kilowatt-hours of power [3].
- **$24.51 an hour — Repairer's wage:** The 2025 median wage for home appliance repairers sits against a $50,990 yearly median [4].
- **3.9 hours — One power year:** A $95 annual running cost translates to 3.9 hours of work at that median repair wage [1][4].

## What I'd watch:

I watch three outside factors, because each one changes the total cost more than the label does.

- **The tight deadlines:** The rules in force today apply to units made on or after September 15, 2014, while the next step arrives at the 2029 and 2030 deadlines, depending on product class [5]. A fridge bought this year sits at the older cap, meaning its eventual replacement will face a much tighter limit.
- **The haul-away:** Many appliance stores pick up and recycle the old unit when they drop off a new one [1]. Disposal often rides inside the deal, which makes a replacement look slightly cheaper at the till than on the tag.
- **The rebate channel:** The program's own page points owners toward partner-sponsored rebates on certified products [1]. A rebate beats the badge by design, since a single $150 payment equals the entire twelve-year power saving [1][2].

## The catch

The part I keep circling is the $95 running cost, because reading it as a strict premium over a new machine breaks the math.

Reading $95 as total power use fits the rest of the data. That sum buys 549 kWh, a unit using 20 percent less needs 457, and the 92 kWh gap saves $15.83 a year [1][3]. If $95 represented only the extra cost over a new machine, the old fridge would need to draw 2,745 kWh, which no modern home unit does.

The local power rate acts as a second soft spot, since the same 72 kWh hold vastly different values across the map. The 2025 averages ran from 35.72 cents per kWh in Hawaii down to 8.20 cents in North Dakota [3]. That means the exact same power drop saves $25.81 a year in the first state and just $5.93 in the second.

Two smaller caveats shade the labor math. The $24.51 figure tracks a wage, not a customer bill. A real service visit carries truck and overhead costs, so $95 buys far fewer than 3.9 hours of a tech's day [4].

Also, the program's page asks owners to hold a fridge at an ambient temperature of 35 to 38 degrees Fahrenheit, which is the inside setting it means [1].

None of these government pages supply an actual repair price, leaving the most critical number blank. That final repair quote belongs entirely to you, and it ultimately decides the question.

- **The Big Shift:** The financial case for replacing a working fridge rests on a published power saving of just $12.50 a year, while the labor to keep an old unit alive costs a median $24.51 an hour.
- **Why It Matters:** A buyer replacing a working machine for the label gets a discount of roughly $150 spread across twelve years, which barely dents the heavy upfront purchase price.
- **What I'd Watch:** Three main factors alter the total cost of ownership more than the power rating.

  - **The rebate channel:** Partner-sponsored rebates can match the entire twelve-year power saving in a single upfront payment.
