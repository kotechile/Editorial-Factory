The engine maker prints the bill most buyers never add up. Briggs & Stratton, the small-engine manufacturer, says to change the oil after the first 5 hours of use, then every 50 hours or yearly, whichever comes first [1]. A Honda dealer's storage guide adds a fuel rule: gas left in the tank has to be treated inside 30 days and drained within 90 [2]. The battery mower next to it in the aisle has no oil and no tank. Consumer Reports measured its class average at 45 to 50 minutes of cut per charge, enough for a quarter acre [3]. So the trade is a 45-minute ceiling against a service list that never hits zero.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

For homeowners choosing a gas or a battery mower, this piece compares the gas side's printed upkeep — oil at 5 hours, then every 50 hours or yearly, plus fuel that decays in storage — with a cordless that finishes a quarter acre on one charge and carries none.

<!-- tension -->

## The schedule you inherited

The 50-hour rule is not advice. It is a condition the maker attaches to the engine. Briggs & Stratton wants the first oil change after the first 5 hours, then a change every 50 hours or once a year, whichever comes first [1]. A small suburban lot runs a mower 20 to 30 hours a season. The calendar triggers the work, not the hour meter. Every spring brings oil, a spark plug, an air filter, and an engine that must start or be diagnosed.

The fuel rule is the part owners skip until spring exposes it. Nichols Equipment, a Honda outdoor-power dealer, splits storage into windows. Gas in the tank for 30 to 90 days needs a stabilizer mixed into fresh fuel, then a 10-minute run to push the treated gas through the system [2]. Past 90 days, the whole fuel system must be emptied, tank included [2]. The same page is blunt about one thing: a stabilizer cannot fix fuel that has already gone stale [2]. I have watched enough neighbors curse a mower in April to believe this is the single most common failure in the class. The fuel aged on a clock nobody saw, and the carburetor kept score.

## By the numbers

- Briggs & Stratton prints an oil change every 50 hours of use or yearly, whichever comes first, after a first change at 5 hours [1].
- Gas may sit in a stored mower for no more than 90 days before the entire fuel system must be drained [2].
- Consumer Reports measured the average battery walk-behind at 45 to 50 minutes of cut per charge [3].

## What the cordless actually removes

Buyers price the wrong side of this comparison, and my reading, held loosely, is that the sticker gap hides it. The usual worry is what the cordless gives up: run time, peak torque, the freedom to refill and keep going. But the gas mower charges its price after the sale. It asks for oil on a 50-hour or yearly clock, a new filter and a plug [1]. It also asks for fuel rotated inside a 90-day window [2]. The battery mower asks for planning instead. Consumer Reports found two-thirds of the electric walk-behinds it rated could finish a quarter acre on one charge [3]. A 7.5 amp-hour pack holds 420 watt-hours of that work [5]. If your lawn fits inside one charge, the upkeep line goes away. No oil to change, no tank to drain. Nothing on the gas side's list has an equivalent on the cordless side, and that absence — not the cut quality — is what you are paying for.

The running cost is easy to state. A charge or two a season draws from the wall, at a national grid intensity of 0.81 pounds of carbon dioxide per kilowatt-hour [4]. No jerry can, no mixed fuel, no oil bottle in the garage. The same trade runs through the rest of our [outdoor power and snow equipment](/categories/outdoor-power-equipment/) coverage, because every machine in this class carries either a service list or a charge cycle.

## The catch

The upkeep tax is small in dollars, and honesty about that matters. A season's oil, plug and filter run a few tens of dollars and one working hour. A battery mower often costs hundreds more at the till. The schedule alone does not close that gap in year one. What it changes is the character of owning the machine. The gas mower degrades when it is ignored, and it starts with the fuel first, per the 90-day rule [2]. The cordless wears through use and years of charge cycles, a slower and kinder clock [5]. Power still belongs to gas: Consumer Reports' testers call it unbeatable there [3]. A cordless that bogs in tall, wet grass has not lost a service race. It has hit the limit it was bought around.

What I would tell a neighbor is the honest rule. If your lawn is a quarter acre or less and one charge finishes it, the cordless deletes a recurring chore, not just a price. If your lot takes several charges or your grass is heavy, the gas machine's upkeep is the salary you pay for its torque — and the service list is the least of what you owe it.
