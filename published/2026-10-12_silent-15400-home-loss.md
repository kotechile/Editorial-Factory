<!-- lead -->

The average water damage claim on a US home runs $15,400, and about 1.50% of insured homes file one in a typical year [1]. That is roughly one in 67.

The parts that feed that loss have listed lifespans. A standard water heater carries six to 12 years, a central air system seven to 15 [3].

Both the failure and the bill sit on a schedule. Most homes hold no budget line for either.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Insurers price a house by what breaks, not by how it looks. The Insurance Information Institute (III) tracks each cause of loss twice. It counts how often owners file, and how much gets paid [1].

Water damage and freezing sits in the awkward middle of that list. It made up 22.6% of home claims in 2023, behind only wind and hail [1]. The category even covers mold, when the policy does [1].

It needs no storm. It needs a supply line or a water heater at the end of its listed life [3].

Some losses ignore upkeep entirely. Fire and lightning reaches 0.23% of homes at $88,170 a claim.

Wind and hail reaches 2.80% at $14,747 [1]. Neither one responds to a service visit.

My read: this is why skipped repairs feel like savings and behave like debt. A household is not choosing between spending and not spending. It is choosing between a visit priced off a $23.84-an-hour wage [4] and the loss table's own average for the failure [1].

## By the numbers

- **1.50% — Water-damage claim rate:** the share of insured homes filing a water damage and freezing claim in a typical year from 2019 to 2023. About one in 67 [1].
- **$15,400 — Average water-damage claim:** the average paid for that cause of loss across the same five years. All causes averaged $17,059 [1].
- **$231 — Expected annual loss:** our math of 1.50% times $15,400. That is the water damage the average insured home adds to the pool each year [1].
- **6 to 12 years — Water heater life:** the listed life of a standard unit. Central air runs 7 to 15 years, and a ceiling vent fan 5 to 10 [3].

## What I'd watch:

I have been reading these loss tables the way an owner reads a service history.

- **The pool math:** insurers paid 5.33 claims per 100 homes at $20,062 each in 2023. That is $1,069 of insured loss per home, before a dollar of premium [1]. Every owner pays into that pool, whether or not they file.
- **The uninsured half:** the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) counts household leaks at nearly 1 trillion gallons a year [2]. One faucet dripping once a second wastes more than 3,000 gallons. Fixing the easy leaks saves about 10% of a water bill. Most of that waste never becomes a claim.
- **The labor price:** the US Department of Labor puts maintenance and repair workers at a $23.84 median hourly wage in 2025, or $49,590 a year [4]. That wage, not the part, is most of what an upkeep visit buys.
- **The aging stock:** Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies counts 145 million US homes [5]. The remodeling market sits above $600 billion, still 50% above pre-pandemic levels. Its own read is that far more of that money needs to go into energy efficiency and weather hardening.

The part I keep circling: every component on that list is a fixture the home already owns.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

That $231 is not a household's bill, and reading it that way is the easy error. It is what the pool pays out per insured home, spread across a large group. Most of those people never file.

One owner's real cash risk is the deductible and the future premium, not the $15,400 average [1]. It also includes whatever the policy leaves out, plus the mess.

The table covers a five-year window, 2019 to 2023. Its water damage line mixes in winter weather that no checkup can stop. Nothing in it measures whether a service visit prevented a loss.

I could be wrong to read a broad claims table as a strict maintenance rule. The field notes this desk keeps for home capital tell a simpler story: a skipped $150 attic drain clear that ended as a $16,800 claim. That is one case, not a rate, and I would not price a reserve off it.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Skipped home repairs carry a published price. Insurers' own loss tables show water damage and freezing hitting 1.50% of insured homes a year, at an average $15,400 a claim.
- **Why It Matters:** The parts that cause these losses have known failure dates. That puts the repair bill on a schedule and turns upkeep into a financial choice, not a chore.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The expected-loss line:** 1.50% times $15,400 gives $231 a year per insured home, the figure a reserve would be built on.
  - **The service-life clock:** water heaters, air conditioners and vent fans carry set lifespans, so replacements can be planned rather than absorbed.
  - **The uninsured leaks:** small drips waste far more water than owners expect and rarely appear in a claim.
- **The Catch:** those averages are what the pool pays, not what one owner pays. The data also carries weather that no service visit can stop.
