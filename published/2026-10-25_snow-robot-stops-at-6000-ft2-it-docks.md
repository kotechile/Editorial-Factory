The Yarbo Y40 Core with the Snow Blower Module Gen 2 is listed at $5,599 as of 2026-10-25, and the number that decides the purchase sits one line under the price: the maker claims 6,000 ft² of clearing per charge, based on exactly one inch of snow [1]. Hit that limit and the robot stops and heads for its charging dock. Buy it only if the same Core will also mow your lawn, because the bundle with the Lawn Mower Pro is $7,399 and that second job is what pays for the platform [1].

| Product | Price (2026-10-25) | What the listing says |
|---|---|---|
| Yarbo Y40 Core with Snow Blower Module Gen 2 | $5,599 | $6,199 regular; two-stage, 12-inch intake, 24-inch width [1] |

## The big picture:

Yarbo sells one base unit and a rack of add-ons, so the first price tag is an entry fee rather than a total. The mower bundle runs $7,399, which puts the mowing job at $1,800 over the snow-only setup, and the snow module on its own lists at $2,000, or $1,299 refurbished [1][2]. My read: the price list is the most honest page on the site, and it says clearing snow alone does not carry the base unit.

## By the numbers

- **$1,800 — The second job:** the Y40 with the blower and the Lawn Mower Pro lists at $7,399, which is $1,800 over the snow-only setup, so the summer job is the one that has to justify the hardware [1].
- **$2,000 — The wear part:** no auger, scraper or pin price appears in the store. The blower is sold as a single unit at $2,000, or $1,299 refurbished, so a February failure is a part price [2].
- **1.5 hours — The dock return:** at a low battery the machine returns to base and needs roughly 90 minutes to charge back to about 80% [1].

## What I'd watch:

I am watching the owner reports on Yarbo's forum, which are the closest thing to a field record, while Consumer Reports notes it has not lab-tested the product [6][8]. The spec sheet also needs reading against that outside record.

| Feature | Maker claim | What the outside record says |
|---|---|---|
| Snow throw distance | Up to 40 feet [1] | 7 to 8 meters, or 23 to 26 feet, on the earlier module [5] |
| Water resistance | IPX5 [1] | IPX4 in the reviewer's spec table [5] |
| Base machine weight | Not published on the page | 61 kilograms (134 pounds) without the blower [5] |

The front intake takes a 12-inch bite, but storms run deeper than the one-inch baseline. Clearing 1,000 square feet at 10 inches takes 167% of a single charge, which is one dock return before the driveway is clear [1].

- **Wet snow, not deep snow:** owners report nine inches of dry powder clearing easily and heavy slush clogging the auger, so some finish the job with a $159 plow blade [8][3].
- **Cold weather charging:** freezing weather forces the battery to warm for 20 to 30 minutes before it takes a charge, stretching every mid-storm return [5][1].
- **Curb piles:** plowed-in snow at the street usually needs a person, which is the exact job most owners want to avoid [1].
- **Wiring faults:** at least one owner had a whole module swapped out for a wiring failure [8].

## The catch

Both ends of the thermometer are hard stops. The maker rates the machine to a 36% slope (20°) and stops the working range at -13°F [1], so a steeper driveway or a colder snap sits outside the promise. My view: in a snowbelt town those two lines matter more than anything in the marketing.

The hidden cost is the module, not a blade. The 38.4-amp-hour battery is $1,199 with no published lifespan, and track grease runs $49 a tub [4][3].

A mainstream 24-inch two-stage claims an 18-car driveway at 8 inches on one charge, in its own words [7]. That is a different unit with no published area, so it cannot be set against the square footage above, but it shows the trade: less money, more of your Saturday. An owner with a two-car driveway and a walk-behind already in the garage should keep pushing it.

## Verdict

Buy the Yarbo Y40 Core with Snow Blower Module Gen 2 only if the Core will also mow. At this price, snow alone buys a platform you will not use, and the mower module is what turns an expensive snow blower into a machine that works most of the year. Snow-only buyers on a normal driveway should spend less and push.

## What we did not test

This review is built from Yarbo's own spec and price pages, one independent measured review of the previous generation of the snow module, Consumer Reports' preview, which states plainly that it has not lab-tested the product, and owner reports on Yarbo's forum. The unit was not handled here, nothing was run in snow, and every capacity, throw and charge figure belongs to the maker or to the reviewer named beside it. If you do buy, run your own numbers through the [true cost of ownership calculator](/calculators/true-cost-of-ownership/) and read the [yard robotics coverage](/categories/yard-robotics/) before the first storm.

- **Why It Matters:** the snow job alone does not make the cost work, so the mower module is what spreads the price across a season.
