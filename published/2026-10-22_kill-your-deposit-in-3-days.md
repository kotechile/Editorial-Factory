<!-- lead -->
Sign a remodel contract at your kitchen table, and federal law gives you three days to cancel and take your deposit back. Sign that exact same paper inside a contractor's showroom after a prior visit, and that window vanishes. The difference is not the price, the crew, or the paperwork. It is simply the physical address where the sale happened.

That gap matters because the deposit is the largest check a homeowner writes before seeing a single day of work. Three states write a hard ceiling into law. California caps a down payment at the lesser of $1,000 or 10 percent of the total contract amount [1]. Maryland caps it at one third [2]. Massachusetts allows one third, or more when special-order materials cost more [4]. Where no statute caps it, the printed contract decides.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The deposit exists because a builder needs cash for materials and a locked slot on the calendar. It is also the exact moment a bad actor takes the money and disappears. That is why some states cap the upfront cash while leaving the rest of the payment schedule alone.

What strikes me is that the cap and the cancellation right guard two entirely different halves of one decision. The cap limits how much cash can leave your bank account. The cancellation window decides whether you can pull that cash back.

Federal law sets that window, and it sets it strictly by location. The rule covers a sale agreed away from the seller's fixed place of business, at $25 or more if it happens at your house, or $130 or more anywhere else [5]. The rule carves out a sale settled after earlier talks at a permanent retail location [5]. So the contractor who meets you at home sells you a contract you can undo. The one who walks you through a showroom and then hands you the paper does not.

The federal rule is old and quiet. Its text has not changed since January 2017 [5]. The door-to-door salesmen it was written to catch are gone. Yet the location test they left behind still decides the fate of a kitchen remodel.

## By the numbers

- **$1,000 or 10% — California ceiling:** A down payment cannot exceed the lesser of the two on a home improvement contract between an owner and a contractor [1].
- **One third — Maryland ceiling:** The contract cannot demand more than one third of the total price at signing, and no payment is allowed before the signature [2][3].
- **5 business days — Maryland door-to-door window:** The required state contract notice gives buyers until midnight of the fifth business day to cancel, or the seventh day if the buyer is at least 65 [3].
- **10 business days — Federal refund clock:** Once the seller receives your cancellation notice, they must return any payments inside ten business days [5].

## What I'd watch:

The mechanics around these rules say more than the actual ceilings do. I've been reading the legal notices these contracts are meant to carry, and the tells are highly specific.

- **Who picks the room:** The contractor sets the meeting location. One showroom visit before the paperwork is the single fact that can remove the federal cancellation window, and nothing in the contract has to mention it [5].
- **The notice block:** A covered sale must carry a cancellation notice in bold type of at least 10 points, right next to the signature line [5]. A contract missing this block is not the sale the federal rule describes.
- **The payment ladder:** California bars any payment past the value of work performed or material delivered, once the down payment is paid [1]. A payment schedule tied to calendar dates rather than work stages does not sit well against that section.
- **The special-order carve-out:** In Massachusetts, the deposit ceiling rises to cover the real cost of custom or special-order materials that must be ordered early [4]. A kitchen remodel with a custom cabinet run is where this clause earns its keep.
- **The financed version:** In Maryland, a contract secured by an interest in the house must state a three-business-day right to rescind in writing [3]. That notice is a legal rule, not a courtesy.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that both legal guards are narrower than they look, and neither one makes a large deposit perfectly safe.

The caps are strictly state law. California, Maryland, and Massachusetts write hard ceilings down. The federal pages I read say nothing about the other states, and I found no primary source that counts them. In a state with no legal ceiling, the homeowner relies entirely on a contract, not a statute.

The federal window is narrower still. It turns on exactly where the sale was agreed, and its carve-outs are ordinary events: an earlier visit to a fixed retail location, or a repair visit you asked for [5]. It also starts ticking on the date of the deal, not the date you finally read the fine print.

A cap is not a promise. It limits what can leave your account before work starts. It does not get the money back, does not put the owner ahead of an unpaid supplier, and does not turn a bad builder into a good one. The refund duty sits with the seller, and those ten business days are the seller's clock, not yours.

That is why the physical room, the signature line, and the payment ladder carry more weight than the deposit cap does. The cap limits the loss. The other three decide whether that loss can be undone.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** State statutes cap home remodel deposits at the lesser of $1,000 or 10 percent in California, and at one third in Maryland and Massachusetts. Meanwhile, the federal cancellation window depends entirely on where the contract was signed.
- **Why It Matters:** The deposit is the biggest payment a homeowner makes before any physical work exists. A cap limits how much cash is exposed, and a three-business-day window is the only reversal the federal rule offers.
- **What I'd Watch:** Where the sale is agreed, what sits beside the signature line, and how future payments are tied to actual work.
  - **The room:** A sale agreed at the house carries the federal window. One agreed after a showroom visit may not.
  - **The notice block:** The federal rule asks for a cancellation statement in bold type of at least 10 points beside the signature.
  - **The payment ladder:** California strictly bars payment past the value of work performed or material delivered.
- **The Catch:** A cap is not a guarantee. Three states write a ceiling down, the pages say nothing about the rest, and no statute gets a paid deposit back for you.
