<!-- lead -->
The battery snow aisle carries three prices for roughly one 24-inch promise, and the gap between the cheapest and the dearest is $1,650.99. The Ryobi 40V HP (the brand's high-performance line) 21-inch kit (RY40HPSN02K2) lists at $849.00 [1]. The Toro 24-inch Power Max e24 60V kit (model 39925) sits $750 above it, at $1,599.00 [2]. The Greenworks 80V MAXIMUS (the brand's flagship line) 24-inch kit closes the set at $2,499.99 [3] — three prices as of 2026-11-22, and a $1,650.99 spread between the ends. Width does not explain that spread, because all three machines cut within three inches of each other. Intake depth does, and it is the number every box leads away from.

## At a glance

This comparison prices the three battery snow blowers a large-driveway buyer meets first and finds the deciding spec is intake depth, not width — with the mid-tier Toro delivering the class promise for $900 less than the flagship.

## The big picture: width is the decoy spec

All prices here are the makers' listed prices, and the widths are close. Ryobi cuts 21 inches [1]. The Toro and Greenworks machines both cut 24 — three inches more for $750 and $1,650 more respectively [2]. At $849.00 for the first machine and $2,499.99 for the third, the arithmetic runs the wrong way for the premium: $40.43 of price per inch of cut against $104.17. I did that division twice before accepting it, because it inverts the usual story that the top of a class buys scale.

The intake depths are not close, and that is where the money goes. Ryobi takes 11.5 inches per pass [1]. Greenworks takes 18 [3]. The Toro housing is the deepest of the three, at 20 [2]. That is the number a February storm meets. In a 10-inch fall the Ryobi takes one pass and a sweep for the remainder. In a 16-inch fall it needs two full passes where either two-stage takes one. Passes are the winter's actual labor, and depth sets them.

Throw is the second gate. Ryobi rates 35 feet [1]. Both two-stage machines rate 50 [2]. A 21-inch path throwing 35 feet ends near the driveway edge on a wide lot, and the machine can only clear what it can throw within its own arc. The two-stage pair throws a whole strip farther.

## What I'd watch: where each machine wins

What I am watching in this set is which driveways the two-stage premium actually pays on, and the answer is wherever snow arrives in bulk — plow windrows, end-of-driveway piles, anything deeper than a foot. The Toro's 20-inch intake swallows a plow's leavings in one pass [2]. So does the Greenworks' 18 [3]. The Ryobi's 11.5 requires the operator to break the pile first or take it twice [1]. The class's own reference point is the EGO (the battery outdoor-tool brand) 56V two-stage, which Pro Tool Reviews priced at "$799.99 bare, $1299.99 kit" at review time and measured clearing the same driveways on 20 to 30 percent of its battery charge in light snow [5]. That is the two-stage pitch working as designed, and it is what the $849 single-stage is measured against — not a machine this desk handled.

The Ryobi wins on the axis nobody quotes: weight. It carries 59.4 pounds [1]. The Greenworks carries 176.4 [3] — a difference you feel every time the blower turns. A single-stage at that weight is a machine a person walks at a pace; a 176-pound two-stage is equipment the driveway pushes back on. Both two-stages are self-propelled, which converts mass into traction. The maker's own copy for the Toro reads "Self-Propelled" [2]. Pro Tool Reviews lists the Greenworks as self-propelled with power steering [5]. The machine still has to be turned at the end of every pass.

The warranty ladder is quiet but real. Greenworks carries the battery 4 years and the tool 5 [3]. On a platform where the pack is the consumable that costs $329.99 [4], that fourth year is a line item, not a footnote. The battery configurations differ too: Ryobi runs two 8Ah 40V packs [1]. Toro runs one 10.0Ah with two empty slots [2]. Greenworks runs two 4.0Ah [3] — three answers to how much capacity rides in the box.

## The catch

Coverage claims are not measured on a shared yardstick, and the class's own pages prove it. Toro speaks in car spaces — "clear up to 20 car spaces on one charge" [2]. Ryobi speaks in car spaces too — "Clears up to 25 Car Spaces Using Included (2) 40V 8Ah Batteries" [1]. The Greenworks publisher row speaks in inches of snowfall and feet of throw [5]. And the 24-car EGO claim Family Handyman retells is a publisher's phrasing of the maker's claim, not a shared measurement [6]. A car space is a shape, not a measurement. Nothing here can rank the three machines on a single coverage number, so the comparison above rests on intake depth and pass counts instead.

My reading, held loosely: most of the price argument rests on figures the makers rate and nobody measures per machine. Every throw distance and car-space count in this piece is a vendor rating under vendor conditions, except where a publisher is named. The pass arithmetic assumes straight full-width passes on a flat apron — the best case no driveway quite is. The Ryobi single-stage and the two-stage pair also sell different jobs; a buyer whose heaviest storm is six inches is overserved by all three, and the honest cheapest answer to that winter is the machine the box actually prices, not the class its aisle sits in.

## What we did not test

One more thing the reader should hold: this desk did not run, push or measure any of these machines. The EGO figures belong to Pro Tool Reviews and Family Handyman, cited as the class reference rather than a current buy, because the EGO kit had no fetchable live price on the day this comparison was built. And the Toro page lists $1,599.00 but routes the buyer to retailers, so a dealer may price it differently on the day you shop.
