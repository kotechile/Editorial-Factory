The vent screen a California homeowner is told to install has 1/8-inch openings. That number sits on the state's insurance-discount list, which describes the measure as "1/16 to 1/8 inch noncombustible, corrosion-resistant metal mesh screens over exterior vents" [1]. The same range is what Fire Safe Marin's retrofit guidance reads into Chapter 7A of the building code [2]. A laboratory study published in 2009, cited by the Lake County Fire Safe Council, found that embers passing through 1/8-inch screening still carried enough energy to ignite fine fuel on the far side [3]. The vent screen sold as the compliant one leaks embers — the small ones, the ones the study found still burning. The finer 1/16-inch screen closes more of that gap and takes air with it, which is the part of the trade no hardening list prices.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** The mesh size the code and the discount list accept still lets embers through.
- **The number:** 71% open area for a 1/16-inch screen, against 75% at 1/8 inch [2].
- **The risk:** Finer mesh cuts airflow, so a fully screened attic can go under-ventilated.

<!-- tension -->

## The screen was a rodent guard

The mesh in a vent was never a fire device. It kept rodents and nesting birds out of the attic, and a quarter-inch opening did that job. The code then moved to 1/8 inch for ember resistance, and the retrofit guidance now calls the quarter-inch screen ineffective [2].

That history explains why the number moved one step and stopped. A mesh size is the one thing an inspector, a contractor, and a homeowner can all check from the ground, so the prescriptive path in the code is a mesh size. The performance path measures the whole vent instead: no flaming ignition during the ember test, no flaming ignition during the flame test's integrity portion, and an unexposed face that stays under 662°F (350°C) [4]. That path needs a listed product, a test report, and a model number.

I read the two paths side by side and keep coming back to how differently they treat the same opening. The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety calls wind-blown embers the principal cause of building ignitions during wildfires, and vents one of the most vulnerable points in a structure [5]. The discount list reduces all of it to a mesh range [1]. The screens differ by a few points of open area — 81% for the quarter-inch, 75% for the 1/8-inch [2] — and nobody in that chain is lying. Each document answers a narrower question than the one the house asks.

## By the numbers

- **$40 a vent:** The council's estimate for a listed assembly where flames can reach the opening [2].
- **662°F:** The ceiling the flame test puts on the unexposed face of a vent [4].
- **144 square inches:** The largest single vent opening the code allows, which a finer screen still has to feed with air [4].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## Where the money goes

Exposure is not evenly spread across a house. The gable-end vent, set in the triangular wall under the roof peak, faces the wind head-on, and Fire Safe Marin advises against it or wants a listed assembly where it cannot be avoided [2]. Open-eave soffit inlets under the roof edge sit where burning material collects, and the guidance prefers a soffited eave for that reason [2]. Ridge and off-ridge vents, along and near the roof's top edge, take less, though the same guidance warns against the unresistant versions of both [2]. Foundation and crawlspace openings are still openings into the building's underside.

The council's own rule for spending is blunt. Where flame impingement is plausible, a listed assembly is a good investment, and where only embers are plausible, mesh alone is adequate [2]. My reading is that this ranks the job better than any "ember-resistant vents" line item does, because it puts the money on the two faces that can see fire and leaves the rest as screening.

The airflow arithmetic is where a cheap retrofit goes wrong. Backing an existing quarter-inch screen with 1/16-inch mesh takes that opening from 81% open to 71% [2], and a vent sized at the code minimum has no spare capacity for a tenth of its face. Debris no longer falls through, so the finer mesh needs cleaning more often [2]. Neither cost appears on a discount form. Block-level hardening is somebody else's bill — [the state's own estimate](/your-blocks-30b-hardening-bill/) covers every house on the street, not the vent on yours.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## What the study does not say

The 2009 finding is narrower than "mesh does not work." It says embers small enough to pass a 1/8-inch screen still carry ignition energy, and that energy needs fine fuel packed close behind the opening before it becomes a fire. Cardboard, dried leaves, and a bird's nest all do that job. An attic with nothing combustible against the vent is a different risk from one used for storage, and the council's advice treats the interior as part of the assembly. What I find telling is that the cheapest fix in the chain is not a product: clearing what sits behind the vent removes the fuel that passing embers need.

The honest limit on the finer mesh is maintenance and airflow, not protection. A screen that clogs is worse than a clean one with wider openings, and a vent swap that ignores the attic's ventilation arithmetic trades a fire risk for a moisture one. The flame test's temperature ceiling is a limit on a test specimen, not a promise about a house. It says nothing about the radiant heat that arrives before any ember does.
