A 5% hurricane deductible on a $400,000 home hits $20,000 before your insurer pays a dime [1]. The average home claim in 2023 landed at just $20,062 [2]. On an average storm, the policy covers the last $62.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The deductible stopped being a flat fee. It became a share of the house.

Florida law sets a clear ladder. Insurers must offer hurricane deductibles of $500, or 2%, 5% or 10% of the policy's dwelling limit [1]. California uses the same math for earthquakes. It caps the deductible at 15% of the home's coverage [3].

The part I keep circling: the percentage grows with the asset, not the loss. A house worth more today carries a bigger deductible for the same storm.

Florida tries to make this math clear. The state makes carriers print the exact dollar figure on the declarations page [1]. A policy with a separate hurricane deductible must also show a large, bold warning about high out-of-pocket costs [1].

## By the numbers

- **$8,000 — Base 2% risk:** the cash a carrier holds back on a $400,000 policy before a hurricane claim pays anything [1].
- **$20,062 — Average claim paid:** the typical size of a home property loss in 2023 [2].
- **2.8% — Wind and hail rate:** the share of insured homes hit by wind or hail in a typical year of 2018-2022 [2].
- **15% — Quake ceiling:** the maximum share of a home's coverage limit a California earthquake policy can charge [3].

## What I'd watch:

I look closely at two numbers on the declarations page now, and neither is the premium.

- **The printed dollar figure:** Florida makes carriers print the exact dollar amount [1]. This turns a vague percentage into the hard hurdle a claim must clear.
- **The buy-down credit:** Moving from a 2% to a 5% deductible on a $400,000 house adds $12,000 in personal risk. At a 2.8% claim rate, that extra risk costs an owner roughly $336 a year in expected losses [1][2].
- **The California million line:** The 5% and 10% quake options vanish above a $1 million coverage limit. A pre-1980 home on a non-slab foundation with no verified retrofit starts at 15%. That is $150,000 on a $1 million policy [3][4].
- **The specific peril:** The Florida ladder only applies to hurricanes. A burst pipe still meets the policy's other deductible [1].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that this is honest math, but the framing hides the real trade.

The $336 break-even figure is just a bench test. It uses the broad wind and hail rate. A named-storm deductible only fires during a named storm [2]. The real cost is lower. That means the premium discount an owner needs to break even is lower too.

A total loss blows past any deductible. It costs the owner the same maximum amount either way. But a $9,000 repair costs nothing extra under a 2% deductible. Under a 5% deductible, the owner pays every penny of it.

Public records do not show what a carrier charges to buy down that percentage. That exact price lives inside the filed rate plan and the quote [1][3]. Without seeing that credit, an owner is guessing at the price of their own risk.

State rules also vary. Florida's ladder applies in Florida, and its ceiling on homes under $500,000 has its own exit: an owner can sign a statement turning down cover for the first dollars of hurricane damage, with the lender's approval, and go higher [1]. California's 15% limit caps the deductible an insurer must offer at a minimum. It does not set the policy cost [3][4].

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Hurricane and earthquake deductibles now function as a percentage of the home's total coverage limit. The cash an owner owes scales with the value of the house, not the size of the damage.
- **Why It Matters:** At standard coverage limits, a percentage deductible often exceeds the average claim. A 5% deductible on a $400,000 home forces the owner to pay the first $20,000. That means a typical $20,062 loss lands almost entirely on the homeowner.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The printed dollar value:** Florida forces insurers to calculate and print the exact dollar amount on the policy summary [1].
  - **The buy-down credit:** whether the premium discount for choosing a smaller deductible beats the expected cost of carrying that extra risk [1][2].
  - **The specific peril:** a percentage deductible usually isolates one threat, leaving the policy's other deductible in place for fires or water leaks [1].
- **The Catch:** The break-even math relies on averages. Public sources never reveal the exact cost to buy down a deductible, and state rules vary wildly by peril.
