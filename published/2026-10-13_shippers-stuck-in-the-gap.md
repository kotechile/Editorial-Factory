<!-- lead -->
Half of the world's container ships missed their August schedules, dropping the global on-time rate to 49.9% [1]. At the exact same time, rail-bound containers at the ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach sat for 6.75 days — the longest wait in a year [2]. Shippers are now stuck between two clocks, and neither one belongs to the control towers they bought.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

I keep coming back to what those two numbers measure. 

An on-time rate tracks ships, while dwell time tracks boxes sitting on the ground. Both moved the wrong way in August, and neither publisher compares them directly.

The gap comes down to simple math. A port complex that just handled more than 2.9 million twenty-foot equivalent units (TEU) over the summer takes time to clear [2]. Trains take time to build, and a terminal can only stack so much steel before it runs out of ground.

The Pacific Merchant Shipping Association (PMSA) tracks this dwell data. Cargo volumes stayed high while rail dwell took an upward turn, it said [3].

The slowdown landed on one side. Truck-bound boxes moved faster, easing to 2.95 days [3]. Rail went the other direction, climbing 0.41 days in a month to 6.75 days from 6.34 in July [2].

The port itself held up fine. Drayage trucks, gates, and yard crews kept boxes moving. The stretch happened at the seam: the handoff where an import leaves the dock to become a rail load bound for the Midwest.

The load port explains the pressure. Inside Asia, the on-time rate fell to 32.3%, with late arrivals stretching past seven days [4]. China, the world's biggest exporter, dropped to 23.9% [4]. Delayed ships bunch up behind the ones running to plan, and Sea-Intelligence counted 12 double-sailings in two weeks [5].

## By the numbers

- **49.9% — Global on-time rate:** The share of vessels that hit schedule in August, down 5.9 points on the month and the lowest since September 2022. None of the 13 largest carriers improved on the month before [1].
- **6.81 days — Late-arrival delay:** How far behind the stragglers ran in August, up 0.60 days on the month, the worst since March 2022 and 1.92 days worse than a year earlier [1].
- **32.3% — Asia's on-time rate:** The regional August reading, with Shanghai dropping to 12.2% and Ningbo to 16.9% [4].
- **6.75 vs 2.95 days — The dwell split:** Rail-bound boxes waited 6.75 days at the twin ports, up from 6.34 days in July and the longest in 12 months, while truck-bound boxes cleared in 2.95 days, down from 3.03 [2][3].

## What I'd watch:

What strikes me here is how carriers respond to a backed-up network. 

They pull space out of it instead of adding the buffer room the system needs.

- **Backlogged extra sailings:** Shipping lines pushed 1.50 million TEU into Asia-to-North Europe routes for Golden Week, 27% more than last year and 60% above the pre-pandemic norm [5]. Sea-Intelligence warned shippers to treat mid-to-late October schedules as "highly volatile placeholders" [5].
- **Targeted cancellations:** Drewry tracked 39 blank sailings across the main East-West trades between October 5 and November 8 [6]. That cuts 5% of planned voyages, half of them on the transpacific route [6].
- **Suspended port bookings:** Hapag-Lloyd stopped selling space to Manila, Batangas, and Subic Bay through December 31 [7]. A work stoppage by customs brokers and truckers there left only about 10% of cargo moving out [7].
- **The next dwell reading:** The National Retail Federation (NRF) expects a late peak-season bump that could make September the busiest month for import volume [3].

My read: The number worth tracking is no longer the vessel on-time rate. It is the days a box spends waiting between the dock and the train.

Smaller events show how little slack remains. Hurricane Isaias shut the Port of Mobile for about two days in early October, and vessel and terminal work resumed on 11 October [8]. That is a true exception with a clear start and end, unlike a global network running at half speed.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The honest limit is that these are two sets of numbers from two publishers. 

Neither one proves the ocean delay caused the rail backup. The busiest three-month stretch in the history of the Port of Los Angeles is a demand story as much as a jam story [2].

I want to be fair to what worked. Truck dwell fell. Terminals, local drivers, and rail partners kept local cargo moving at a pace near three days [3]. A gateway is not failing when one of its two exits slows down. It is simply being asked to hold more than its inland link can take.

The on-time rate has run below par for most of 2026, so another monthly drop is a trend line, not a sudden alarm. Carriers can add sailings and ports can add rail slots.

What I take from this is narrow. Tracking every leg of a shipment still matters, and the alert queues these systems generate require real work. The question I would put to any architect now is where the next dollar goes: another data feed, or physical buffer room where two delays meet.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The global vessel on-time rate fell to 49.9% in August, while rail-bound cargo dwell at Los Angeles and Long Beach climbed to a one-year high of 6.75 days.
- **Why It Matters:** Both readings hit in August and sit at opposite ends of the same box's trip. Because truck dwell fell at the same ports, the slowdown lives entirely in the handoff between the ship and the train.
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether the October dwell figure climbs again as peak season runs late, and how carriers manage capacity.
  - **The seam:** The exact step where a box leaves a marine terminal and starts moving inland by rail.
  - **Buffer room:** Physical space to hold cargo at that handoff, like on-dock rail slots or near-port transloading yards.
  - **Volatile placeholders:** Published schedules that carriers do not expect to honor, which Sea-Intelligence flagged for mid-to-late October.
- **The Catch:** Two datasets from two publishers show a link, but not a proven cause. The case for spending money at the rail handoff rests on where the marginal delay lands, not on a claim that supply chain software failed.
