<!-- lead -->
The two slowest classes of stock-keeping units (SKUs) are 67.76% of the items on file in one warehouse study. They hold 60.7% of the units on hand and produce just 49.8% of the picking work [1]. Slow stock quietly eats your floor space and your cash. It also hides behind a healthy gross margin, which is the sale price minus the buy price. Your daily report shows these items earning a profit. That screen cannot see what the tail costs to keep.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Every yearly stock review runs the same flawed scorecard. It cuts the bottom of the list on sales, margin and turn rates.

Gross margin answers one question. Does this item sell for more than it cost to buy? It says nothing about what the item cost to store. What strikes me here is that the tail pays three costs that sit outside that math.

First is capital-days: the units on hand multiplied by how long they sit. Second is floor space. A slow mover holds its slot for a full year. A fast mover turns a whole season in that same space.

Third is the dead-stock write-off. The average maker carries 20% to 30% of its stock as dead goods. On a $3 million inventory, that is $600,000 to $900,000 in write-offs later [3].

Load those three costs against a margin figure and the ranking flips. In one consumer-goods case, 44% of SKUs earned just 8% of sales. The bottom 100 items lost money once fully loaded costs were counted [2]. They had read positive on margin reports.

## By the numbers

- **67.76% — Catalogue tail share:** The C and D speed classes were two-thirds of the SKUs on file, against 16.54% for the fast A and B movers [1].
- **60.7% — Units on hand:** The tail held most of the stock for just 49.8% of the picking work [1].
- **$600,000 to $900,000 — Dead-stock cost:** What the average maker's 20% to 30% share of dead goods costs later, on a $3 million inventory [3].
- **50% — Aging reserve hit:** One published reserve policy books a 50% reserve at 181 to 365 days, and 100% past a year [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

I have been reading the pruning case studies this week. The operators who fixed this did not cut harder. They changed what they measured.

- **Stock held per unit of work:** The metric that exposes the tail is units on hand against units shipped, not margin. In the speed study the tail carried 60.7% of the units for 49.8% of the work [1]. The fast movers carried 28.66% of the units for 50.2% of the work [1]. My read: a stock-to-work ratio above 1 flags the cut list with no margin input at all.
- **The reserve, booked first:** Write-offs in the consumer-goods case rose 340% over five years [2]. Forecast accuracy outside the top 50 SKUs averaged 42% [2]. The tail was also the least predictable stock in the building. The reserve policy that answers it is an aging ladder: 25% at 91 to 180 days, 50% at 181 to 365 days, and 100% past a year [5]. Book the reserve before the review and cheap items stop looking cheap.
- **The floor, priced in feet:** One storage vendor notes the long tail takes about 80% of warehouse space for 20% of sales [6]. That is a vendor claim, but it points the same way as the speed table.
- **The write-off line, watched quarterly:** Most firms set a health target for dead stock below 3% of total stock value [4]. A quarterly trend line against that number is the cheapest early warning there is.

The field note I keep circling: a multi-brand clothing retailer. Its bottom 30% of slow movers read positive on margin. They ran at about −18% net once pick travel, pallet fees and markdowns were tallied. Cutting those bottom 2,400 items lifted output 14% and freed 38,000 square feet.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

A slow mover is sometimes cheap insurance, and this math will not tell you which ones. A spare part that keeps a line running is worth more than its holding cost. An item that anchors a customer's whole order is a different asset from one that anchors nothing.

The evidence is also thinner than the headline suggests. The speed shares come from one vendor's own client study, not a broad survey [1]. The 44% share and the loss-making line come from a single case study written up secondhand [2].

What survives is the direction, not the precision. Margin does not price capital-days, floor space or the write-off. I could be wrong that a stock-to-work ratio alone is enough to cut on. I am fairly confident it beats gross margin as a first cut.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The slow tail of a catalogue is most of the stock and most of the floor. In one warehouse study the C and D movers were 67.76% of SKUs and held 60.7% of on-hand units for 49.8% of the picking work [1].
- **Why It Matters:** A cull scored on margin keeps the items that eat cash, floor space and the write-off reserve. The average maker holds 20% to 30% of its stock as dead goods [3].
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether operators start ranking cull lists on stock per unit of work and booked reserves instead of margin.
  - **Stock-to-work ratio:** Units on hand divided by units shipped for the same item. It flags the tail with no margin input [1].
  - **Aging reserve ladder:** The reserve booked by age — 25% at 91 to 180 days, 50% at 181 to 365 days, and 100% past a year [5].
  - **The 3% ceiling:** The dead-stock target most healthy firms set as a share of stock value [4].
- **The Catch:** One vendor's client study and one case study carry these numbers, and service-critical slow movers such as spare parts are never blind cut candidates.
