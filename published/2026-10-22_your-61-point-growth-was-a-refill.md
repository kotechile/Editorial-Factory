<!-- lead -->
Constellation Brands shipped 123.9 million cases of beer last quarter, a 5.5% jump [1]. The volume distributors actually sold to stores fell 0.6%. That 6.1-point gap isn't demand growth. It is a stock refill, and the company says so in its own release.

A survey of 150 small and midsize businesses (SMBs) shows the same error at the other end of the chain [2].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

A distributor's order bundles two things: shopper pull and the change in a warehouse buffer.

A supply plan that reads raw orders counts that buffer twice. It tracks the extra cases in the supplier's shipments, and again in the buyer's forecast.

I keep circling the way Constellation reports this. The brewer prints both numbers. Shipments track what leaves the brewery. Depletions track what distributors send to stores [4].

The gap is public every quarter for anyone checking a demand plan.

This year's gap is wide because of last year's cuts. The brewer and its distributors slashed stock in fiscal 2026 as premium beer sales slowed.

Chief executive Nicholas Fink told analysts on Oct. 7 that the firm entered fiscal 2027 "too light." It pumped up shipments to clear backlogs and fill shelves.

Distributor stock is now in a "great position," Fink said, though still below its usual days on hand.

Then comes the crucial line for planners. Fink said the company expects "ships and depletes to track within 99% of each other" [3].

My read: if the year ends near parity, the back half has to run the other way. After a quarter in which shipments led stores by 6.1 points, the refill looks like a one-time bump, not a revenue gift.

A Netstock software survey shows the buyer's side of this trade. Polling SMB users, the report found 53% are ordering earlier or in larger lots this year. Another 39% lean more on forecasting tools [2].

The early-order group is struggling. Only 44% of firms ordering earlier report service levels above 90%, compared to 62% of everyone else.

The report's own reading is careful: early buying "often reflects existing availability pressure more than it reflects a proven strategy." Moving an order up just changes when the goods arrive. It adds no news to the forecast.

## By the numbers

- **6.1 points — Shipment spread:** Beer shipments rose 5.5% to 123.9 million cases while depletions fell 0.6%. The spread is the difference between two stated figures, not a number the company prints [1].
- **99% — Full-year parity guide:** The firm expects shipments and depletions within 99% of each other for fiscal 2027 [3]. They split in the quarter just reported.
- **44% against 62% — Service gap:** Only 44% of firms ordering earlier report service above 90%, against 62% of everyone else [2]. The report ties that gap to strain, not to the tactic.
- **$3.91 million — Typical stock value:** Flat inside a $3.8 million to $4.0 million band for three years [2]. That is the report's own evidence that early orders re-time goods instead of adding to them.

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

- **The next gap:** The brewer reports again next quarter. A shipment line that trails store sales would confirm the give-back the full-year math implies.
- **Survey repeats:** A second pass at the same group would show if the 44% is a fixed cost of hedging or just a bad year.
- **Dead stock over turns:** Nearly a quarter (24%) of SMBs report more than 10% of their stock is dead, up from 12% in 2024. Faster inventory turns and more waste can rise at the same time.
- **Blind spots:** Brewers and distributors report shipments and depletions as two distinct metrics. Direct sellers only see one line and must rebuild the second from store data.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that the refill was still the right call.

A channel that cuts too deep and leaves shelves bare needs stock. Constellation flagged this exact swing a year out.

Its September 2025 outlook told investors to expect shipments to trail depletions by 6.0 to 7.0 points in the second quarter of fiscal 2026. It then expected the two to align for the second half. This year's 6.1-point lead is the mirror of that guide. For anyone reading closely, the move was disclosed, not discovered.

The survey data carries its own limits. Netstock sells the software its report promotes, and 150 replies cannot definitively prove that early orders cause poor service. The report admits the link may run backward: tight supply forces early orders.

Finally, a refill is never free.

Its beer margin fell 160 basis points to 39.0%, even as volume jumped 5.5%. Marketing and selling costs more than offset the savings from lower tariffs. Volume from a channel righting itself carries the exact same cost base as volume from a thirsty shopper.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Constellation shipped 5.5% more beer than a year earlier, but its distributors sold 0.6% less to stores. The 6.1-point gap is a channel stock refill. A separate survey finds small firms that order early to shield themselves report worse service.
- **Why It Matters:** A distributor order combines shopper pull with warehouse stock changes. A plan that reads raw orders as demand counts a buffer twice, booking a one-quarter refill as permanent growth.
- **What I'd Watch:** The next shipments-against-depletions gap, the survey's next read on its early-order group, and the dead-stock share that rose to 24%.
  - **Ships against depletes:** Two volume lines a supplier reports, one to distributors and one from them to stores. The gap between them is the channel's stock change.
  - **Parity guide:** A full-year target that puts the two lines within 99% of each other. It tells a planner the split is meant to close.
  - **Early-order group:** The share of buyers who moved orders up, tracked against their own service rate rather than against the firms that did not.
- **The Catch:** The refill was the right call after a real shortage, and Constellation disclosed the swing a year early. The survey is a vendor comparison that cannot prove causation, and the extra volume still cost Constellation a 160-basis-point drop in margin.
