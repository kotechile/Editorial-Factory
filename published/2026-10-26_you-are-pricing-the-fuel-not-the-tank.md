Your tank's class sets the bill more than its fuel does. A 50-gallon gas water heater sold today can meet its federal energy floor at a score of 0.56, and that one number turns 2025's average home gas price of $15.34 per thousand cubic feet into about $26.26 for every million Btu of hot water the tank makes. The same heat costs about $22.04 from a heat pump tank at the floor that starts on May 6, 2029, and the standard electric tank at today's floor costs $55 for the same million Btu, or $55.07 on the exact average price.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Water heating runs all year, and it is the load where the equipment's own efficiency gap is widest. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) puts water heating, lighting and cooling together at 25% of home energy use in 2020, against 52% for heating and air conditioning [9]. The tank is a smaller slice of the bill than most owners assume, and a much larger slice of the loss they can fix.

The federal floors are where the gap shows. A mid-draw gas tank of 20 to 55 gallons, made before May 6, 2029, must score 0.6483 minus 0.0017 times its rated gallons, which is 0.5633 at 50 gallons [3]. A standard electric tank in the same band must score 0.9307 minus 0.0002 times gallons, or 0.9207 at 50 gallons [4]. From May 6, 2029 that electric floor jumps to 2.30 at every draw pattern, a level that plain heating elements cannot reach [5]. The rule is dated on manufacture, not on sale or setup.

| 50-gallon, mid-draw floor | Score | Cost per million Btu |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Standard electric, today | 0.9207 | $55.07 |
| Gas-fired, today | 0.5633 | $26.26 |
| Electric from May 2029 | 2.30 | $22.04 |

My read is that this is the rare energy choice where the physics is better than the pitch suggests, and the pitch is where the money leaks. A tank replacement is also the moment [the big gas units' venting trap](/your-75000-btu-tank-venting-trap/) already covered, and it arrives once.

## By the numbers

- **$26.26 — Gas tank, per million Btu:** 2025 gas at $15.34 a thousand cubic feet is $14.79 a million Btu of fuel, and dividing by the 0.5633 floor gives $26.26 of hot water [1][3][7]. That division is the desk's own.
- **$22.04 — Heat pump tank, per million Btu:** 17.30 cents a kilowatt hour is $50.70 a million Btu of power, and dividing by the 2.30 floor for units made from May 2029 gives $22.04 [2][5][6].
- **$55.07 — Standard electric tank, per million Btu:** the same power divided by today's 0.9207 floor for a 50-gallon unit, which is 2.10 times the gas tank's cost [2][4][6].
- **20.6 cents — The break-even price:** the heat pump tank and the gas tank cost the same when home power reaches about 20.6 cents a kilowatt hour at $15.34 gas, and gas wins below about $12.88 a thousand cubic feet [1][2][3][5][7].

## What I'd watch:

The quote is where the class gets chosen, and its wording shows which comparison the seller is making.

- **The score line:** a quote that reads "50-gallon gas, same as yours" is selling a class, and the class has a number on it. What I would want to see is that score printed beside the model, because the legal floor and the shelf unit are rarely the same figure [3].
- **The room a heat pump needs:** 1,000 cubic feet of air in a space that stays between 40 and 90 degrees all year, with a program warning that colder spots cut efficiency and can force a standard electric backup [8]. A closet or a cold garage corner is the usual block, and that is a fact about the house, not the box.
- **The local power rate:** the 20.6-cent break-even moves with a household's own rate, so a home well above the national average gets a different answer from the same two floors [2][5].
- **The 2029 repair clock:** a standard electric tank fitted in 2026 is the last of its class made new, which matters when the same owner weighs a repair against a replacement before that date [5].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The honest limit is that the gap is narrow. The heat pump tank at the 2029 floor beats the gas tank by about 16%, and cheap gas takes that lead back on any day when it is priced close to power [1][2][3][5].

I could be wrong about how heavily a home should weigh that margin, because no figure here includes a fitted price. Without a fitted price there is no payback in dollars to quote.

What the numbers do settle is the class to refuse. A standard electric tank at 0.9207 costs 2.10 times the gas tank per million Btu of heat, and nothing in this price set closes that gap [2][4][6]. The part I keep circling is that the same tank is the cheapest box on the shelf, which is why it keeps going into houses that will carry the bill for a decade.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The energy class a water heater must meet sets the bill far more than the fuel it burns. That gap widens on May 6, 2029, when new electric tanks must reach a score of 2.30, a heat pump level.
- **Why It Matters:** At 2025 prices the same million Btu of hot water costs about $26.26 from a 50-gallon gas tank, $22.04 from a heat pump tank at the 2029 floor and $55.07 from a standard electric tank, so the class chosen at replacement sets a decade of bills.
- **What I'd Watch:** Two published prices and two legal floors now decide the answer, and the state spread is where it moves.
  - **The break-even price:** the power rate where heat pump and gas tanks cost the same, about 20.6 cents a kilowatt hour at 2025 gas prices.
  - **The room it needs:** the 1,000 cubic feet of air and the 40-to-90-degree range a heat pump needs to run at its rating.
  - **The May 2029 floor:** the date the electric class must reach 2.30, which ends the standard electric tank as a new option.
- **The Catch:** The heat pump's edge is roughly 16% per million Btu of heat, not the halving that sales talk implies, and no fitted price appears in these figures, so there is no dollar payback to promise.
