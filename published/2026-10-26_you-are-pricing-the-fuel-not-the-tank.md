Your tank's class sets the bill more than its fuel does, because what decides the running cost is the efficiency standard the unit must meet. Take a 50-gallon gas tank sold today: federal rules let it meet its floor at a score of 0.56, and that one number turns 2025's average home gas price of $15.34 per thousand cubic feet into about $26.26 for every million Btu of hot water it makes. A standard electric tank at the floor it must meet today costs $55 for that same million Btu, or $55.07 at the exact average price. A heat pump tank meeting the floor that starts May 6, 2029 does the same job for about $22.04. A Btu is a unit of heat.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Water heating runs all year. That makes it the load where the gap between a good unit and a cheap one is widest. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) groups water heating, lighting and cooling at 25% of home energy use in 2020. Heating and air conditioning take the other 52% [9].

So the tank is a smaller slice of the bill than most owners assume. It also holds a much larger share of the waste an owner can fix, because a cheap unit wastes fuel for its whole service life while the purchase price is paid once.

The federal floors are where that gap shows up. A gas tank of 20 to 55 gallons, built before May 6, 2029, must score 0.6483 minus 0.0017 times its rated gallons. At 50 gallons that is 0.5633 [3]. A standard electric tank in the same range must score 0.9307 minus 0.0002 times gallons, or 0.9207 at 50 gallons [4]. Those scores measure how much heat a unit gets from the fuel it takes in, so a higher number is better.

From May 6, 2029 the electric floor jumps to 2.30 for every usage pattern. Plain heating elements cannot reach that [5]. The date that matters is when the unit is built, not when it is sold or installed.

| 50-gallon, mid-draw floor | Score | Cost per million Btu |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Standard electric, today | 0.9207 | $55.07 |
| Gas-fired, today | 0.5633 | $26.26 |
| Electric from May 2029 | 2.30 | $22.04 |

The three rows are the classes a homeowner will be offered at a 50-gallon size. Mid-draw means a medium amount of daily use, which is how most households run a tank. My read is that this is the rare energy choice where the physics beats the sales pitch. The pitch is also where the money leaks. A tank replacement comes once a decade, and it is the same moment [the big gas units' venting trap](/your-75000-btu-tank-venting-trap/) already described.

## By the numbers

- **$26.26 — Gas tank, per million Btu:** 2025 gas at $15.34 a thousand cubic feet comes to $14.79 a million Btu of fuel. Divide that by the 0.5633 floor and the hot water costs $26.26 [1][3][7]. That division is the desk's own.
- **$22.04 — Heat pump tank, per million Btu:** power at 17.30 cents a kilowatt hour comes to $50.70 a million Btu. Divide that by the 2.30 floor that applies to units built from May 2029 and the hot water costs $22.04 [2][5][6]. A heat pump moves heat rather than burning fuel to make it.
- **$55.07 — Standard electric tank, per million Btu:** the same power divided by today's 0.9207 floor for a 50-gallon unit. That is 2.10 times the gas tank's cost [2][4][6].
- **20.6 cents — The break-even price:** heat pump and gas cost the same when home power reaches about 20.6 cents a kilowatt hour at $15.34 gas. Below about $12.88 a thousand cubic feet, gas wins [1][2][3][5][7].

## What I'd watch:

The quote is where the class gets chosen. Its wording shows which comparison the seller is making.

- **The score line:** a quote that reads "50-gallon gas, same as yours" is selling a class, and every class has a number on it. What I would want to see is that score printed beside the model. The legal floor and the unit on the shelf are rarely the same figure [3].
- **The room a heat pump needs:** it wants 1,000 cubic feet of air in a space that stays between 40 and 90 degrees all year. The program warns that colder spots cut efficiency and can force a standard electric backup [8]. A closet or a cold garage corner is the usual block. That is a fact about the house rather than about the box.
- **The local power rate:** the 20.6-cent break-even moves with a household's own rate. A home well above the national average gets a different answer from the same two floors [2][5].
- **The 2029 repair clock:** a standard electric tank fitted in 2026 is the last of its class to be built new. That matters when the same owner weighs a repair against a new unit before that date [5].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The honest limit is that the gap is narrow. The heat pump tank at the 2029 floor beats the gas tank by about 16%. Cheap gas takes that lead back on any day when it is priced close to power [1][2][3][5]. I could be wrong about how heavily a home should weigh that margin. No figure here includes a fitted price, and without one there is no payback in dollars to quote.

What the numbers do settle is the class to refuse. A standard electric tank at 0.9207 costs 2.10 times the gas tank for every million Btu of heat [2][4][6]. Nothing in this price set closes that gap. The part I keep circling is that the same tank is the cheapest box on the shelf, which is why it keeps going into houses that will carry the bill for ten years.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The class a water heater must meet sets the bill far more than the fuel it burns. That gap widens on May 6, 2029, when new electric tanks must reach a score of 2.30, which is heat pump territory.
- **Why It Matters:** At 2025 prices the same million Btu of hot water costs about $26.26 from a 50-gallon gas tank, $22.04 from a heat pump tank at the 2029 floor and $55.07 from a standard electric tank. The class chosen at replacement sets ten years of bills.
- **What I'd Watch:** Two published prices and two legal floors now decide the answer. The state spread is where it moves.
  - **The break-even price:** the power rate where heat pump and gas cost the same, about 20.6 cents a kilowatt hour at 2025 gas prices.
  - **The room it needs:** the 1,000 cubic feet of air and the 40-to-90-degree range a heat pump needs to run at its rating.
  - **The May 2029 floor:** the date the electric class must reach 2.30, which ends the standard electric tank as a new option.
- **The Catch:** The heat pump's edge is roughly 16% per million Btu of heat, not the halving that sales talk implies. No fitted price appears in these figures, so there is no dollar payback to promise.
