<!-- lead -->
Walmart's supplier deduction bill starts at $200 per defect, and that SQEP (Supplier Quality Excellence Program) fine punishes the cheapest work a supplier already controls. The 3% OTIF (on-time, in-full) penalty — the cost-of-goods fine billed on missed deliveries — gets the attention. It should not. Walmart's schedule is a three-class cost curve: the cheap parts of the bill are the easy fixes, and the part that bills most is the one that costs real inventory money. Read the fee schedule as a spending order and the first budget line picks itself.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
Walmart suppliers face two overlapping deduction programs: OTIF, which fines 3% of the cost of goods when scores fall short, and SQEP, which fines per data and packaging defect. This piece prices each class and shows which fixes pay first.

<!-- tension -->
## The bill arrives in three classes
Walmart launched OTIF in 2017 and phased the SQEP defect schedule in between 2021 and 2024. In February 2024, OTIF billing moved to a quarterly cadence [4]. The split between the programs is deliberate: Walmart priced each failure mode by what it costs its own receiving floor. I read the fee schedule three times before the shape of it landed, because it is not one fine with tiers — it is three separate cost curves wearing one deduction statement. The three classes are:

1. **Data defects.** A missing ASN (the advance ship notice that tells a distribution centre what is on the truck) forces manual handling, so it bills as a flat fee per defective purchase order.
2. **Packaging defects.** A mislabelled pallet forces rework, so it bills per pallet and per load.
3. **Fill-rate failures.** A short delivery costs the retailer a lost sale, so it bills as a share of the goods themselves.

## By the numbers
- **$200 per defect:** The admin fee that opens every document-accuracy fine, before per-case charges stack on top [1].
- **3% of the cost of goods:** The OTIF penalty on the non-compliant cases of a purchase order, billed when a scorecard falls below target [3].
- **$20 per load:** The load-quality charge that rides on the same fee when a full truckload fails inspection [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## Where the fee classes split control
The classes do not cost the same to remove, and that gap is the whole decision. The data class bills $25 flat per defective purchase order for an ASN that never downloaded. Other document errors bill $200 per defect plus $1 per case [1]. Killing this class is master-data and EDI (electronic data interchange) hygiene, which lives inside your own four walls. Harvest Group, a supplier-services firm, reports purchase order accuracy and ASN problems as recurring fine sources [2].

The packaging class closes with pallet specs and load inspection. It bills $4 per pallet against its own admin fee [2]. Neither project touches inventory, freight or network design — that is what makes them first.

The fill-rate class is the expensive one. Walmart prices it as 3% of goods because a short delivery costs the retailer a sale, and the current targets are 90% on-time prepaid, 98% collect-ready and 95% in-full [4]. Closing it means safety stock, and your inventory plan already prices that trade. Suppliers are treating the classes separately for that reason, reporting per-program scores rather than one blended compliance number [1].

What I keep coming back to is how many ops leaders budget against "the Walmart fine" as one line. The data class can be retired cheaply, and each fee it removes stays removed. The cheap parts of the bill are also the cheap parts to buy. That is the same gap as your miss costing 9x the [chargeback](/your-miss-costs-9x-the-chargeback/) downstream — the deduction a supplier sees is the small end of its own miss.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch
This schedule is Walmart's published programme terms as reported by supplier-services firms, not audited deduction data. The exact thresholds for your account live in your own Retail Link scorecard, not in any guide [3]. The arithmetic still holds as a spending order, and one worked example shows how fast the cheap class pays. Vendormint's own worked example — one rework defect and one inaccurate ASN on a 12-case purchase order — bills $412: two admin fees plus $1 per case across 12 cases [1]. My reading, held loosely, is that the caveat does not touch the order of spend. No fill-rate project in the building pays for itself that fast.
