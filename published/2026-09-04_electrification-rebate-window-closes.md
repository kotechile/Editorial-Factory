August 31, 2026, at 5:30 p.m. Central time was a hard stop. It was the last chance for a Wisconsin homeowner to file a specific rebate. They needed this cash to replace a gas appliance with an electric one [2]. The next morning brought a quiet end. On September 1, the federal High-Efficiency Electric Home Rebate (HEEHR) program stopped these payments. The program ended gas-to-electric swap payouts in every state that runs it [1][3]. Many people wanted to ditch a gas furnace, water heater, or stove. They planned to buy electric gear and claim a rebate. That window is now shut. The government barely announced the change.

<!-- tension -->
This is the second quiet cut in under a year. The 25C Energy Efficient Home Improvement tax credit expired on December 31, 2025 [4]. That credit paid up to $2,000 toward a heat pump. It capped out at $3,200 a year [4].

Then the Department of Energy (DOE) issued a new rule. The agency released Program Notice 26-2 on May 29, 2026. This notice stripped the HEEHR appliance rebate of its main goal [1]. The program no longer pays households to remove gas or oil equipment [1].

The new rule is strict. Rebates now only cover upgrades from older electric gear to better electric gear [1]. New home builds also qualify [1]. A dead gas furnace replaced by a heat pump gets nothing. A gas water heater swapped for a heat pump version fails to qualify. Pulling a gas stove for an induction cooktop misses out.

The program even changed its name. It shifted from "Home Electrification and Appliance Rebates" to "High-Efficiency Electric Home Rebates" [1]. This rebrand shows where the money is going. Funding moved away from gas-to-electric conversions. It shifted toward efficiency upgrades for homes already using electricity [1].

Stack these dates together. The story sharpens. The tax credit died at the end of 2025. The conversion rebate died in September 2026. Homeowners now face thinner, stricter deals. Yet many people still think the old deal exists.

This shift hurts households using rebate math. It hurts families waiting for an aging gas furnace to break. The new rules favor already-electrified homeowners doing efficiency upgrades. They also help the insulation and air-sealing trades. The new rules put the home's outer shell first [1][5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
The money is not entirely gone. It just moved. It also gained new rules. Here is what remains and how to claim it.

First, the whole-home path stays open. The Home Owner Managing Energy Savings (HOMES) program offers a separate rebate [5]. This payout grows based on proven or modeled energy savings [5]. The fuel-switching ban did not hit this program. Your project might be a deep retrofit. This means adding heavy air sealing and insulation. It also means sizing a heat pump to fit the tighter home. If so, the whole-house rebate still pays [5]. It rewards the exact work that makes a heat pump cheap to run.

Second, electric-to-electric upgrades still qualify under HEEHR. You can replace an old electric resistance furnace. You can swap an aging electric water heater. Moving to a heat pump still earns a rebate. You can claim up to $8,000 for an ENERGY STAR space heating heat pump. A heat pump water heater can earn up to $1,750 [2][6]. The total project cap sits at $14,000 [6].

Income limits set the payout. The area median income (AMI) is the middle-point income for your specific region. If your household earns below 80% of this AMI, the rebate covers 100% of the project cost [6]. Households earning between 80% and 150% of the median get 50% coverage [6].

Third, the home's outer shell comes first. Notice 26-2 requires homes to use rebates for insulation and air sealing first [1]. You must do this before claiming heating and cooling upgrades. The only exception is if the home is already properly sealed [1]. Check your attic insulation before buying a heat pump. The program demands structural work first. This work actually cuts your utility bill.

Fourth, geothermal systems still have a federal lifeline. The 25D Residential Clean Energy Credit remains untouched [4]. This tax credit covers 30% of a ground-source heat pump's cost through 2032 [4]. It offers a bigger check than the 25C credit ever did. But it requires burying an underground pipe loop. This is not a simple above-ground equipment swap. It means a completely different job and a much larger budget.

Stop pricing your project on old numbers. Pull your state's current program page today. Check your project category. Are you doing an electric-to-electric swap or a whole-home upgrade? Run the payback math on the actual surviving rebate. Do not rely on numbers from 2024.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
This shift does not make electrification a bad choice. It is also not a ban on gas appliances. The change simply dictates who receives federal money. It does not alter the basic math. A heat pump still beats a gas furnace on running costs in most climates. The equipment itself became better and cheaper over time.

The government killed the specific subsidy for the switch itself. Your gas furnace might be genuinely broken. You might want a rebate to soften the cost of a direct swap. That specific financial help is gone.

You may be better served waiting to do a whole-home retrofit. You should also check your state and utility incentives. Several states still run conversion programs independent of the federal government [2]. The rebate landscape shifted by a pen stroke in May. It shifted again by a calendar date in September. The payback math changed right alongside it.

<!-- tldr -->
- As of September 1, 2026, the federal HEEHR rebate no longer pays for gas-to-electric appliance swaps. The 25C tax credit for this exact job expired on December 31, 2025.
- Surviving incentives include the HOMES whole-home rebate and the 30% geothermal 25D tax credit. Upgrades from old electric gear to new electric gear also still qualify.
- Verify your state's current program and inspect your attic insulation before buying a heat pump. New federal rules require homes to seal their outer shell first.
