<!-- lead -->

Every guide to a move abroad quotes 183 days, as if the count came with the passport. Three states publish three different counts, and 183 is neither the loosest nor the strictest of them. California's Revenue and Taxation Code keeps a domiciliary a resident until an absence of 546 consecutive days, and then only if the absence runs under an employment-related contract [1]. Oregon lets a domiciliary out at 30 days away and pulls a visitor back in at 200 [2]. Virginia draws its line at 183 [3]. A reader who left last spring is still a resident of the state that issued the licence until that state's own count is met, and the count is not the one in the guide.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** the 183-day rule is one state's count, not a national one.
- **The number:** 183 days — Virginia's line, and not the strictest of the three [3].
- **The risk:** a kept house or a long summer visit reopens the residency a move was meant to close.

<!-- tension -->

## Three states, three counts

A state asks three things. It asks where the reader is domiciled: the place treated as home, and the place they mean to return to. It asks whether a permanent place of abode is still available to them, meaning a house or flat they can use. It asks how many days they spent inside the state. California, Oregon and Virginia answer those questions differently, and the answers run from 30 days to 546.

1. **California: 546 days.** A person domiciled there stays a resident until 546 consecutive days away under an employment-related contract, and the rule sets aside 45 visiting days a year [1].
2. **Oregon: 30 days, or 200.** The same reader escapes with no abode in the state, an abode elsewhere and 30 days or fewer; a visitor who keeps an abode there and stays past 200 days is caught [2].
3. **Virginia: 183 days.** Domicile at any point in the year makes a resident, and so does a place of abode kept past 183 days [3].

Domicile outlasts a departure, because it is about intent as much as distance. Virginia's code calls it "the permanent place of residence of a taxpayer and the place to which he intends to return even though he may actually reside elsewhere" [3]. A reader who keeps the storage unit, the voter card and the return ticket in their head has told the state where home is.

The abode question is separate, and a house defeats it. Virginia taxes anyone who kept a place of abode there past half the year, "whether domiciled in Virginia or not" [3]. No intent is required, and no permanent move. Oregon runs the other way: its escape needs three things at once — no abode in the state, an abode somewhere else, and a month or less at home [2]. Fail any one and the reader is a full-year resident of a state they left.

I find it telling that the strictest count belongs to a state whose shelter is written for employees rather than for the self-employed. California's rule asks for absence "under an employment-related contract" [1]. A founder billing clients from Lisbon has no such contract. Their absence is judged on purpose alone — temporary or transitory — and that question has no day count at all.

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What the statutes reward

The statutes reward a sequence rather than a single act, and each step leaves a document trail. A leaver undoes all three with the things people most often keep: the flat, the licence, the summer.

1. **Sever the abode.** Oregon will not release a domiciliary who keeps a permanent place of abode in the state, however few days they spend there [2]. A rented-out house still counts in most readings, because the statutes ask whether one is maintained, not whether it sits empty.

2. **Move the domicile.** Domicile moves when intent and conduct move together. Virginia's list of what a state may weigh is long: employment, income sources, vehicle registration, the residence of a spouse and children, voting registration [3]. No single item decides it; the pattern does.

3. **Keep the days under the allowance.** California sets aside up to 45 visiting days in a tax year, and only inside its contract rule [1]. A reader outside that rule has no allowance to spend.

## By the numbers

- **45 visiting days a year:** the home time California disregards inside its contract-absence rule, about six weeks [1].
- **$200,000 of intangible income:** past that in a contract year, the California shelter is closed whatever the days show [1].
- **78 weeks away:** the length of the California absence — a year and a half, not one tax year [1].

Two more edges sit in that section, and a summary will drop both. The shelter closes to anyone whose stocks, bonds, notes or other intangible personal property produced income above that line in a contract year [1]. It is also closed where the main purpose of the absence is to avoid California tax [1]. A founder living off a portfolio, or one who moved after a liquidity event, loses the shelter on income or on purpose before the calendar is read.

What I notice is how differently the three documents fail. Oregon's test looks easy, and the house defeats it. Virginia's needs no intent at all. California's needs a contract the self-employed do not have. Three doors, and a leaver can be shut out of the one they counted on by a fact they never weighed.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The move is not the event that ends residency; the paper trail is. A reader who leaves in March and files a part-year return in April has told the state they were a resident until March. The state will use that next year.

The exit is a dated set of acts, done close together. Surrender the licence for a foreign one, register to vote at the new address, move the lease or deed, close the accounts that assume residence, and hold the visits under the allowance that applies. For a founder, the honest reading of California's statute is that no allowance exists, so the calendar of visits matters more than the count in the guide. A [calculator for the income side of a move](/calculators/expat-evaluator/) will price two tax systems; the residency test is the input it cannot guess.

I read the guide's count as surviving because it is short and easy to sell, not because it is right. None of the three statutes above carries a rate, a rebate or a saving. They carry a definition of who still belongs to the state, which is the part of a move that no flight confirms.
