<!-- lead -->
On Sept. 18, California's main grid battery at Moss Landing caught fire for the fourth time in seven years after roughly 1,500 units sparked on their own [1][2]. Twelve days later, Gov. Gavin Newsom signed two laws to pay homeowners for the power stored in their garage batteries, electric vehicles (EVs) and smart thermostats [4].

<!-- tension -->
**Why it matters:** California spent years betting on giant lithium plants to save daytime solar for evening peaks. Now, the state's main site is a fire risk built with a closed design the industry no longer uses [1]. San Jose State professor Dustin Mulvaney puts it simply: "If we had only known the worst-case scenarios of how not to build batteries, we wouldn't have built Moss Landing." [1]

My read: The failure of the big battery just made the case for the small one. California runs the nation's largest battery fleet [1], yet Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E) has kept its nearby Elkhorn plant dark since a January 2025 fire [3]. Senate Bill (SB) 913 steps into that gap, ordering the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) to price the power customer-owned batteries send back to a stressed grid [5].

**By the numbers:**
- **1,500 units burned:** Roughly 1,500 still-charged, hard-to-reach units caught fire while crews tried to take them apart [1].
- **4th fire since 2019:** The Sept. 18 blaze marks the site's fourth safety failure in seven years [1].
- **2 new laws:** SB 905 and SB 913 order the state to pay customers for their battery power [4].
- **July 2027 restart:** PG&E's nearby Elkhorn plant stays offline until mid-2027 [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
**What I'd watch:** Industry experts view this fire as a chemistry lesson. Moss Landing used nickel-manganese-cobalt (NMC) in a tightly packed layout, which is less stable than newer options [3]. Today's home batteries rely on lithium iron phosphate (LFP), a mix that avoids the same intense fires when damaged [1]. 

What I keep circling is whether homeowners know to ask for LFP by name, or if they just buy "a battery."

Three signals I am watching next:

- **The CPUC's math:** The bill's author was "shocked" Newsom signed it, and trade groups warn the next governor's CPUC will decide if customer bills actually drop [5].
- **Who stays outside:** Newsom vetoed Assembly Bill (AB) 1813, a shared solar program, the exact same day he signed the virtual power plant (VPP) bills [4]. Renters who cannot buy an EV or home battery miss out.
- **The chemistry message:** The fire points to aging NMC designs, not modern LFP setups [1][3]. I want to see if sales teams explain this difference to homeowners buying backup systems today.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
**The catch:** My read: this is not a clean passing of the torch. Moss Landing relies on an older design the industry has moved past, and experts stress this latest fire reveals nothing new about modern battery safety [3]. Also, the VPP payout remains just an idea until the CPUC writes the actual pricing rules, especially since this governor vetoed three VPP bills last year [5].
