USTR (the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative) just opened a public docket on whether the trade rules behind your Mexico sourcing hedge will hold. Comments are due January 12, 2027. That deadline makes 2027 the first renewal risk in a deal now reviewed every year.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

I keep coming back to one thing: both escape routes from China exposure shifted their legal footing this month, in opposite directions.

The Mexico case rests on the trade deal, not on a duty rate. The Federal Register notice published on October 5 records what happened at the July 1 meeting of the Free Trade Commission, the body that runs the USMCA (the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement). "The United States did not agree to extend the Agreement for a new 16-year term," it says [1]. Absent an extension, the text ends on July 1, 2036. Until then, the Commission meets to review it every year [1].

A rule reviewed each year can be reopened each year. The review also brings enforcement. On October 8, USTR invoked the deal's Rapid Response Labor Mechanism at an auto-parts plant in Puebla, Mexico. It asked Mexico to review whether workers there can organize. It also told Treasury to hold that plant's imports open instead of closing them out [3]. That freezes cash at one plant. No new duty rate is needed to hurt.

The onshore option moved too, and not toward independence. On October 14, GlobalFoundries said it will run two new chip plants in Malta, New York [2]. Japan pays, up to $21 billion. GlobalFoundries brings the process tech and runs the plants. In a separate deal, the Commerce Department takes about four percent of the company [2]. It also moved $955 million of an existing federal chip award into advanced packaging and other work at its New York and Vermont sites [2].

**By the numbers:**

- **Up to $21 billion — Japan's fab funding:** about $10 billion would speed a first 300mm plant and about $10 billion would fund a second. The sum is a ceiling, not a promise [2].
- **900,000 wafers — Yearly capacity per fab:** each plant should add about 900,000 300mm wafers a year at full output. The first is due in 2029 [2].
- **Four percent — U.S. equity stake:** the stake sits in a separate deal, not in the plant funding [2].
- **January 12, 2027 — Comment deadline:** written comments and hearing requests for the 2027 review are due at 11:59 p.m. Eastern [1].

## What I'd watch:

The people closest to this are arguing about how long each option lasts, not about rates. I'd watch four signals next.

- **Review docket filings:** the notice asks for comment on compliance and on the North American investment climate. Filings will show if the yearly review becomes routine or a real fight.
- **Spread of import freezes:** the Puebla action covers one plant. I'm watching to see if buyers start pricing Mexico risk at the factory instead of the border.
- **The 2029 gap:** the first New York wafer is due in 2029. Until then, onshore capacity is a promise, and a promise does not cover a line that ships in 2027.
- **The memory squeeze:** Supply Chain Dive, citing Omdia research, reports memory prices up to 400% higher over two years [4]. That cost lands on the same buyers who pick the next line.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

A yearly review is not an exit. The deal and its duty-free terms stand today. The 2036 date is a decade away. What changed is the cost of assuming the ground stays put, not the rate paid now. I could be wrong, but I doubt a comment docket reopens a sourcing plan this quarter.

The same restraint applies onshore. Four percent is not control. The $21 billion is a cap for two plants that make nothing yet. The state does not set GlobalFoundries' prices.

My read: the two options now run on different clocks. Mexico's preference is reviewed each year. The onshore line shows up in 2029, and its money has a government at both ends. A landed-cost model that holds both fixed is comparing a license to a building site.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The U.S. did not agree in July to extend the North American trade deal for another 16 years. The deal is now reviewed every year, and the first comment docket is open until January 12, 2027. In the same fortnight, the biggest onshore chip news of the month came with Japanese money and a U.S. stake in the operator.
- **Why It Matters:** Both hedges against China exposure now carry political terms. Nearshoring rests on a preference that renews each year. Reshoring rests on capacity that arrives in 2029 and is paid for by governments.
- **What I'd Watch:** I'm watching the review docket, import freezes at Mexican plants, and the gap between the 2029 plant date and schedules that will not wait.
  - **Review filings:** comments under the 2027 review show if importers treat the yearly cycle as routine or as a fight.
  - **Held entries:** an import held open is not final, so what is owed on it stays open while a labor case runs.
  - **Fab dates:** a first wafer in 2029 is the earliest onshore output from this deal, so it cannot cover near-term volume.
- **The Catch:** A yearly review is not an exit, and four percent is not control. The deal and its rates stand today, and the onshore capacity is still a plan.
