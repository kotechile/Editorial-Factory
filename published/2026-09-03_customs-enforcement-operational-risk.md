<!-- lead -->
On September 18, U.S. Customs and Border Protection claims a new enforcement tool. CBP is actively reviewing every importer-of-record Form 5106 on file. Where officials find inaccurate or incomplete information, they will void the IOR number, "rendering it invalid for any purpose, including entering imported merchandise into the United States" [1]. A stale physical address is now a critical defect. Listing a broker's office or a P.O. box will trigger a void [4]. CBP offers no grace period and no advance warning. Your cargo simply stops at the port.

<!-- tension -->
The Federal Register published this rule on August 19 [1]. It represents the sharpest edge of a much larger enforcement wave. A stack of dated obligations just landed on supply chain operators at once. Tariffs of up to 100% on imported drones took effect today, September 3 [5]. Canada's retaliatory counter-tariffs on $27.6 billion of U.S. goods hit September 8 [2]. On December 4, a minimum-import-price regime kicks in for polysilicon and its derivatives, layered on top of a new 15% tariff [3].

Look at the timeline. The machinery of trade enforcement is fully operational. Authorities are aiming directly at importers' paperwork and port-level clearance, not just their bills of materials. The IOR rule is the clearest tell. It adds no duty at all. Instead, it weaponizes a form every importer has already filed.

The backstory is concrete. The U.S. imposed a 50% tariff on $27.6 billion of Canadian goods effective August 22. Canada immediately suspended negotiations and is now matching those penalties dollar-for-dollar [2]. The drone tariffs followed a Commerce Department national-security investigation opened last year, landing mostly without a transition window [5]. Washington has finished arguing. The government is now collecting.

Mid-market importers and third-party logistics providers face the highest exposure. Companies that run compliance through a broker and ignore their own IOR data are exactly the ones whose 5106s still list a registered agent's address [4]. The beneficiaries are the entities selling the fix—customs brokers, compliance software vendors, and the domestic manufacturers these duties protect [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
Operations teams face an immediate compliance checklist.

The primary task is pulling Form 5106 to verify the physical address. CBP instructions explicitly require the location associated with the actual business [1]. Listing a registered agent, customs broker, freight forwarder, or P.O. box is now a critical defect [4]. If CBP voids your number, agents will notify you at the most recent email on file and copy the broker that last filed an entry for you. Reestablishing that number runs through a dedicated CBP program. The fix is not instant [1].

Cost mapping requires equal urgency. Drones and thermal-imaging components face tariff spikes up to 100% [5]. Canadian-sourced steel and aluminum that previously carried 25% duties now carry 50%, alongside furniture and apparel. Appliances, dairy, and certain derivative products carry 25% [2]. Polysilicon-adjacent solar inputs face minimum import prices—$21 per kilogram for raw polysilicon, $100 per kilogram for ingots and wafers. Ingots and downstream derivatives also absorb an additional 15% tariff [3].

Every deadline requires a single named owner on a centralized calendar. The timeline leaves no margin for error: September 8 for Canada, September 18 for the IOR rule, and December 4 for polysilicon. If a container sits at the port when CBP voids a number, the cargo does not wait for the paperwork to catch up [1].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
Two caveats keep this enforcement wave from becoming apocalyptic. First, an importer can reestablish a voided IOR number. The CBP notice process is designed to force a paperwork cleanup rather than ambush an operator, as the agency writes to you first at your provided address [1]. Second, the tariff wave contains specific carve-outs. Drones receive reduced rates if sourced from allied countries including the EU, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland, Taiwan, and the U.K. [5]. Polysilicon faces country-specific treatment, and the 15% tariff applies to ingots and downstream derivatives rather than the raw material itself [3]. The risk is immediate and real. But relying on a broker to handle everything is no longer a defensible strategy when a single form error can stop your containers.

- Operators must verify the physical address on their 5106, map duty exposure to landed costs, and assign every effective date to a named owner.
