<!-- lead -->
On September 18, U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) gets a new tool. The agency is checking every Form 5106 on file. This form registers the Importer of Record (IOR). The IOR is the company officially named on the import paperwork. CBP is reviewing old forms, not just new ones. If the agency finds bad data, it will void the company's IOR number. This makes the number "invalid for any purpose, including entering imported merchandise into the United States" [1]. A stale physical address is now a major defect. A broker's office or a P.O. box will trigger a rejection [4]. There is no grace period. The cargo simply stops at the port.

<!-- tension -->
The government published this rule on August 19 [1]. It belongs to a massive enforcement push. Multiple trade barriers landed at once this month. Tariffs up to 100% on imported drones took effect today, September 3 [5]. Canada launches counter-tariffs on September 8. These border taxes hit $27.6 billion of U.S. goods [2]. Then, a mandatory price floor starts on December 4. This minimum import price limits cheap polysilicon and related solar parts. It layers on top of a new 15% tariff [3].

Look at what these dates share. The trade debate has shifted. Officials spent two years debating these duties. Now the machinery is running. Regulators are targeting routine paperwork and port clearance. They are not just taxing raw materials. The IOR rule is the clearest signal. It adds no new tax. Instead, it targets a form every importer has already filed.

The backstory behind these dates is clear. The U.S. placed a 50% tariff on Canadian goods on August 22. Canada stopped talks in response. The country is now matching those duties dollar-for-dollar [2]. The drone tariffs followed a national security probe last year. The Commerce Department led that review. Most drone taxes landed without a transition window [5]. Each policy has finished the debate phase. Trade enforcement is now in the collection phase.

The people this hurts are easy to name. Mid-market importers face immediate risk. Third-party logistics (3PL) providers do too. Many run their compliance through a broker. They have not checked their own IOR data in years. These companies still list a registered agent or a P.O. box [4]. The people this helps sell the fix. Customs brokers, software vendors, and domestic makers will benefit [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
Make three moves before September 18.

First, pull your Form 5106 and check the physical address. CBP requires the actual location of the business [1]. Listing a registered agent, customs broker, freight forwarder, or P.O. box is a defect [4]. If CBP voids your number, it sends a written notice. This goes to your most recent email on file. The agency also copies the broker who last helped you. Getting the number back requires a special CBP program. The fix is not instant [1].

Second, map where these duties hit your total delivery costs. Drones and thermal cameras face tariffs up to 100% [5]. Canadian steel and aluminum previously carried a 25% duty. They now carry a 50% rate. Furniture and apparel also face this rate. Appliances, dairy, and some steel parts carry a 25% tax [2]. Polysilicon solar parts now face minimum import prices. Raw polysilicon hits a floor of $21 per kilogram. Ingots and wafers hit $100 per kilogram. Meanwhile, finished solar products face an extra 15% tariff [3].

Third, put these deadlines on a calendar. Assign a named owner to each date. September 8 marks the Canada tariffs. September 18 brings the IOR rule. December 4 triggers the polysilicon duties. Keep one owner, one list, and one central place. A voided number traps a container at the port. The cargo does not wait for the paperwork to catch up [1].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
Two details keep this situation from being a disaster. A company can restore a voided IOR number. The notice process aims to force a data cleanup. It is not an ambush. CBP writes to you first using your listed contact info [1].

The tariff wave also includes multiple exemptions. Drones receive lower rates if they come from allied nations. These include the European Union (EU), Japan, South Korea, Switzerland, Taiwan, and the United Kingdom (UK) [5]. Polysilicon imports get country-specific rules. The 15% tariff applies to ingots and finished goods rather than raw polysilicon [3].

This operational risk is real and immediate. It is not the end of global trade. Relying entirely on a broker is risky. It is no longer a safe answer for a form that can stop your cargo.
