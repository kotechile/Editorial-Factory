<!-- lead -->
Heating oil sold for $6.19 a gallon in the week of October 19, up from $3.53 a year earlier [1]. The federal answer to that price is 315,000 barrels lent from the Northeast emergency reserve, about one day of what oil-heated homes burn in a winter [3]. The trap sits on the other side: the way out of the fuel, a heat pump and the work around it, also got more expensive this month [4].

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

- **The shift:** Heating oil reached $6.19 a gallon as Washington lent fuel companies 315,000 reserve barrels.
- **The number:** $2,488, the season's cost at that price on the government's 402-gallon estimate.
- **The risk:** Install materials rose 3% to 15% while the reserve barrels go to suppliers.

<!-- tension -->
## Why the cheap answer got dearer

The Energy Information Administration (EIA), the federal energy statistics agency, published its winter outlook on October 6 [2]. It put heating oil at $5.26 a gallon for the season, or $2,115 for a household burning 402 gallons [2]. The market did not wait for it. Weekly readings ran $6.05, $6.14 and $6.19 through October, and the last one is above even the $5.79 the agency expected for November [1]. I read that weekly table twice before I trusted the gap. Federal help arrived in the third week of October [3].

The reason sits in the fuel, and it is not a household decision: heating oil is the same refined product as diesel, distillate has been the tightest part of the crude barrel all year, and the Northeast has no spare pipeline capacity to bring in anything cheaper [2]. Refining slowed, imports cost more, stockpiles are thin [2]. That leaves a local price set by the world market and moved through limited delivery capacity.

The Department of Energy offered fuel companies the barrels from sites in South Portland, Maine; Boston, Massachusetts; and New Haven, Connecticut [3]. Deliveries must finish by November 13, and each borrower returns the same volume plus a fee before the winter of 2027–28 [3]. That is a loan to suppliers, not a price cap for households.

The other option is to stop buying the fuel. A heat pump conversion needs materials: duct, gas piping, dampers, filters and a tankless water heater. Those parts rose 3% to 15% at twelve manufacturers this month [4]. Most disclosed increases sit between 3% and 8%, and two more take effect later in November [4]. The break-even between the two systems has drifted in both directions for a year, and [that arithmetic is what a homeowner is really being asked to sign](/heat-pumps-cheaper-to-run-pricier-to-buy/).

## By the numbers

- **$6.19 a gallon:** The national residential price in mid-October, up from $3.53 a year earlier [1].
- **39.6 million barrels:** What oil-heated homes burn over five winter months, against 315,000 lent from the reserve [2].
- **3,052 oil furnaces:** August shipments of the appliance that burns it, up 27.1% on a small base [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## What I'd watch

The first test is the weekly price. A loan of 315,000 barrels against 262,000 barrels burned each day is a small test of a large price, and the weekly survey should settle it within a month [1]. If the price holds near $6, the release changed the mood rather than the supply [1].

Two things sit behind that delivery date. Borrowers owe the fuel back with a fee before the next winter, which leaves the reserve thinner for 2027 [3]. And the agency publishes a new outlook on November 10, which puts a fresh set of prices next to the October ones [2].

What I keep circling is how little the replacement market has moved. Oil warm-air furnace shipments rose 27.1% in August to 3,052 units, a six-month streak, while gas warm-air furnaces fell 4.3% [5]. The trade press cautions that a base that small says little about the country [5]. The detail fits a narrower story that I find more convincing. Households are replacing the box they already own, because the alternative needs an electrical panel and a contractor they cannot book in October.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch

The price can fall as fast as it rose. Heating oil tracks diesel, and the agency expects a wholesale move to reach the retail price within four to six weeks, in either direction [2]. It also warns that switching to a cheaper fuel "would not necessarily result in lower expenditures for their household", because houses and equipment differ from any average [2].

The reserve carries a return obligation, not a price cap. What I would want before comparing anything is the number no agency prints: the installed cost of a heat pump in a given house.
