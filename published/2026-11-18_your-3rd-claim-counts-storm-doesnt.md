The claim that can cost you the policy is the water one, not the wind one. Texas lets a carrier count claims that are not weather losses, three of them inside three years on the same house [1]. A storm loss doesn't count at all, because a policy cannot be refused on losses caused by natural causes [5]. So the roof that fails in hail leaves the ladder untouched, and the pipe that bursts in a wall climbs it.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
This piece covers how a Texas homeowners policy is repriced: carriers count non-weather claims — water damage most of all — and a third counted claim in three years lets the carrier refuse the next year's policy, while storm losses never count.

<!-- tension -->
## Where the count begins

A policy year starts the clock. A water loss is reported, adjusted and paid, and the carrier's file records a claim rather than a dollar amount [1]. The count then runs on a rolling three-year window. What the carrier counts is how many claims it paid, not how large they were. A small repair that clears the deductible counts the same as a gutted kitchen.

Two levers sit inside that rule, and both are paper. After a second counted claim, the company must tell the owner in writing that a third one puts the policy in danger [2]. That letter is a notice of possible nonrenewal, which means the carrier may decline to write another year. A company that never sent it cannot refuse to renew because of the third claim [2].

The second lever is a forgiveness clause. The first two water claims caused by an appliance do not count at all, but only if the damage was fixed and the repair passed an inspection by a qualified inspector [3]. Two documents do the work there, and neither is a form the owner has to invent.

I went looking for this rule in the policy forms and found it in the state's consumer guide instead [1]. That placement says something about the product. The form sets out what is covered, and the state sets out how many covered losses a carrier may hold against the house.

## By the numbers

- **$17,240 average water claim:** the mean cost of a water damage and freezing loss, 2020 through 2024 [7].
- **22.2 percent of claim costs:** water damage and freezing's share of what carriers paid out in 2024 [6].
- **Two refusals:** the number of companies that must turn an owner down before a state plan will insure him [4].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## The letter that decides

At renewal the carrier prices a file, and what that file holds is a count of claims. The warning letter is the paper that matters most, because it carries a date [2]. If it never arrived, the owner holds proof that the third claim was not the reason his policy ended.

The inspection report is the other document. Once the work is done and a qualified inspector signs off, an appliance water claim stops counting [3]. That makes an invoice and a signed report the cheapest paper in the file, and the only paper that can take a claim back off the count.

Where the ladder ends has a price too. A refusal to renew sends the owner to a market that needs at least two licensed companies to have said no first [4]. That plan writes a basic policy, and it costs more than a standard company charges. The gap lands on the mortgage as much as on the premium.

What I'm watching next is whether carriers actually send the letter that says a repair passed, because the forgiveness is worth nothing if it reaches the owner after the renewal date.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## Where the count stops

The honest limit is the state line. The three-in-three count and the warning duty are Texas rules, set out by the Texas Department of Insurance as of its 1 June 2026 revision [1]. Every state writes its own version of the ladder, so a reader in Florida or California is counting against a different number of steps. No federal agency publishes a national count, and any figure sold as one is a state figure with a bigger label.

The second limit is that the count is not the money. A deductible of $2,500, which the reader swaps for his own number, leaves $14,740 of an average water claim [7]. That makes the average loss worth filing on the cash and still a step on the count. A loss below that deductible closes without payment, and those claims are not counted at all [5]. The [deductible arithmetic](/your-5-deductible-risks-your-claim/) settles that half of the decision before the ladder is ever consulted.

I'd want to see a second state's ladder before treating three as the national number, and no source in this file draws one.
