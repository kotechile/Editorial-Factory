<!-- lead -->
Your misclassified entry costs up to 4x the duty you underpaid, and the bill does not stop at the border. Duty is the tax charged when goods enter the country, and federal law lets Customs and Border Protection (CBP) add a penalty of twice that tax for a careless error, four times for a reckless one, and the full value of the goods for a planned cheat [1]. The law offers one discount, called a prior disclosure. You lose this discount by missing a strict deadline, not by getting the code wrong [1].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The unpaid tax is never up for debate. CBP must collect the lost funds whether it issues a fine or not [1]. The only real question is the multiplier stacked on top.

That multiplier depends on how CBP grades the error. A simple typing slip that is not part of a larger pattern breaks no rules at all [1].

Careless errors can carry a fine of up to twice the tax. Reckless errors reach four times the tax, and fraud reaches the full value of the goods [1].

A prior disclosure shrinks all three fines, but only if you file it fast. If you report the error before a formal probe begins, CBP caps the fine and leaves the goods alone [1].

For careless and reckless errors, the fine drops to just the interest on the unpaid tax. That interest runs from the day the import was fully processed [1]. For fraud, it drops to 100 percent of the tax, or 10 percent of the value if no tax was affected [1].

None of those caps applies until the company pays the money it owes.

The hard part is the clock. CBP starts a formal probe based on its own notes. It begins the day an officer first sees signs of a broken rule [1].

The company has to prove it did not know about that probe [1]. That puts the burden of proof on the team that just found its own mistake.

My read: This is a filing problem wearing a code costume. The companies that get hurt are rarely the ones with the most wrong codes. They are the ones who found an error in March and were still doing paperwork in June.

The overall scale is small. CBP's own paperwork estimate for the prior-disclosure process covers 762 responses a year, at roughly three hours each [4]. Most wrong entries never take this path.

## By the numbers

- **4x — Reckless-error ceiling:** A reckless mistake sparks a fine of up to four times the unpaid tax and fees, or the domestic value of the goods, whichever is lower [1].
- **100 percent — Fraud disclosure cap:** Reporting a fraud error before a probe caps the fine at 100 percent of the tax, rather than the total value of the goods [1].
- **10 days — Oral disclosure deadline:** A spoken warning only counts if a written record reaches the local fines officer within 10 days [2].
- **762 — CBP's paperwork estimate:** The prior-disclosure collection is estimated at 762 total annual responses, roughly three hours each and 2,286 total burden hours [4].

## What I'd watch:

What I am watching next is the paper trail a company builds in the first two weeks after it spots a bad entry. That trail becomes the entire defense.

- **The written record:** A phone call to a CBP officer starts the process. But the officer can deny the claim if a written record never arrives within 10 days [2]. A chat without a follow-up letter offers zero help.
- **The four parts:** A valid disclosure names the goods, the entry numbers or ports, the false claim, and the correct data [2]. The company must promise any unknown facts inside 30 days [2]. I would want to know who inside a team can pull those four pieces together on a tight clock.
- **The tender:** CBP rejects the claim outright if the company never pays the final calculated loss [2]. Teams that file the forms but argue over the math without paying lose their shield.
- **The five-year file:** Importers must keep entry records for five years from the entry date [3]. That matches the exact window an audit can reach back into. The defense relies on those same boxes.
- **The escalation price:** For a calculated loss above $100,000, a company can ask headquarters to review CBP's number, as long as it deposits the money and more than a year is left before CBP's deadline to act [2]. Taking that review gives up the right to fight the number afterwards.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The fine cap is not zero. A lowered fine for careless errors still includes the interest on the unpaid tax. That interest runs from the date the import was fully processed [1].

A company that files late pays for every day of delay.

The disclosure is also not automatic. CBP decides whether the filing was truly "prior." The company carries the heavy burden of proving it acted before a probe started [1]. That is a messy fact fight about internal emails, not a simple form.

Some errors do not even trigger this law. Simple typing mistakes fall outside the rules entirely unless they form a clear pattern [1]. A panicked disclosure can sometimes cause more trouble than the first mistake.

The part I keep circling: The fine is a direct multiple of the tax. The cheapest disclosure is the one filed on a small error early. The same steps taken after a broker audit has covered years of entries cost much more to do.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A wrong customs code exposes an importer to a fine of two to four times the unpaid tax, and up to the full value of the goods for planned fraud, under 19 U.S.C. § 1592 [1].
- **Why It Matters:** The government collects the missing tax either way [1]. A prior disclosure is the only legal tool that shrinks the fine down to simple interest, and it relies on strict deadlines rather than legal arguments [1][2].
- **What I'd Watch:** How fast a company turns the discovery of an error into a written, dated filing.
  - **The 10-day record:** The required written note that keeps a spoken disclosure alive [2].
  - **The four parts:** The strict checklist covering the goods, entries, false claim, and correct data [2].
  - **The tender:** The upfront payment that decides if CBP accepts the disclosure at all [2].
- **The Catch:** Reporting an error does not erase the tax or the interest, the company must prove it acted before a probe began, and basic typing errors do not require this process at all [1].
