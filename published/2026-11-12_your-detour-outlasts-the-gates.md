<!-- lead -->

The detour outlasts the gates the port added to absorb it. The Vincent Thomas Bridge is the road from San Pedro onto Terminal Island, where most of the Port of Los Angeles' container terminals sit. It closed on 7 November for a deck replacement Caltrans expects to run 16 months, to March 2028 [1]. Long Beach's answer was extra evening and Saturday gate hours for six weeks [2]. The tariff deadline now falls on 9 January, about four weeks after those gates close [3].

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** A 16-month road closure is being absorbed by six weeks of extra gate hours.
- **The number:** 8% of the port's drayage trucks used the bridge that is now shut.
- **The risk:** The next tariff date lands about four weeks after the extra gates stop.

<!-- tension -->

## The 70 weeks and the six

Caltrans announced the closure on 22 October and shut the bridge at 12:01 a.m. that Saturday, sending traffic onto Interstates 710 and 110, State Route 103 and Harry Bridges Boulevard [4]. Long Beach began its extra gates two weeks before that, a program of six weeks that skips Thanksgiving week and was built with the terminal operators, the dockworkers' union and the harbour trucking association [2]. On 10 November, the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative (USTR) published two notices. Together they moved the 178 exclusions from Section 301, the statute behind the tariffs on Chinese goods, and the suspended fee on China-linked ships into January [3].

None of that is a mistake: the two agencies each answered their own question. The port measures what leaves the terminal, and it states that every Los Angeles terminal stays open with no expected impact on "port operations, cargo terminal productivity, flow or capacity" [1]. A trucking company measures something the port does not print, which is how many round trips a driver finishes in a shift. Drayage, the short hop that moves a container between a terminal and a warehouse, is paid per move. A slower route therefore costs the port nothing and costs the fleet the next move.

I have been reading the two statements side by side, and they do not describe the same cost. The bridge's share of that fleet is small. The port puts it at 8% of port-complex drayage truck traffic, and that is the number behind the "no expected impact" sentence [1]. The trucking side is an estimate published by a forwarder: 45 to 60 extra minutes per trip to the Terminal Island terminals at peak [5]. At least one Southern California drayage provider has posted a congestion surcharge, and no published figure prices it. The only quantity on the table is time.

## By the numbers

- **8% of the complex's drayage traffic:** The share of port truck moves that used the closed bridge [1].
- **997,177 containers at Long Beach:** September's record month, up 25% on the year before [2].
- **45 to 60 minutes:** The added time truckers estimate for a Terminal Island trip at peak [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## Where the December booking lands

The complex was running at a record when the bridge shut. Long Beach moved 997,177 twenty-foot equivalent units (TEU, the standard container measure) in September, its busiest month in 115 years [2]. Los Angeles moved 1,042,652, up 18.07% [8]. Between them the two ports handled just over 2.04 million TEU in one month, and that is the volume the detour now works through.

What I keep returning to is the ordering of the clocks. Six weeks from the program's late-October start puts its end in mid-December. The tariff date is in January. If the deadline pulls cargo forward the way the November one did, the extra gates will be shut before the boxes land, and the truck that has to retrieve them is the same truck losing 45 minutes a turn. Rail is the exception: the port says rail cargo is unaffected, which is worth knowing because [the rail leg was already the slower half of this gateway](/your-port-clears-35688-boxes-dont/).

Two things are worth tracking before the deadline. USTR took about 60 unique comments on its proposal to pause the fees again, and this is the second two-month extension of a pause first written for a year [6]. Short renewals are not a settlement, and each one resets the date a shipper plans against.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The collision only bites if the deadline still pulls cargo. September's record was itself partly a deadline effect, and the pause has already been extended more than once, so another extension would soften the case without shortening the 70 weeks of road closure. What I find hard to settle is how much of the delay the port can absorb. The extra gates were announced to cover the closure's start. The port's "no expected impact" sentence is a claim about terminal throughput, and a truck's day is not what it measures.

The 8% figure is the port's own, written to explain why the closure should not worry shippers, and no independent count of drayage turns has been published. What that leaves is narrower than the headline. A road carrying a tenth of the truck traffic is closed for almost two years, the only buffer announced covers six weeks of it, and the next tariff date falls after the buffer ends.
