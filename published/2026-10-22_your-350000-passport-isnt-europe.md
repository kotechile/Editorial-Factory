<!-- lead -->
Argentina will now sell you a passport for $350,000. That cash buys a new nationality, but it does not buy you a home in Europe. Portugal's own migration report, published twenty days earlier, prices that second thing in years.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Two government documents, published twenty days apart, show a market splitting in two.

On October 2, Argentina announced a straight cash-for-citizenship programme [1][2]. The price is a $350,000 payment to the state treasury that is never returned, or an $800,000 government bond. A spouse or a child aged 18 to 25 adds $100,000. Each younger child adds $25,000, so a family of four reaches $500,000. The doors open in the fourth quarter of 2026 [1][3].

Portugal tells a different story. The country's migration agency shared its 2025 report on September 22. It counted 1,585,854 foreign residents at the end of the year [4].

The agency handed out 337,880 residence titles in 2025. That is up 55% on the year before, and the report says the jump came largely from finishing files that had sat for years. Permits for investment, the route brokers sell as a golden ticket, came to 2,685. Permits for professional activity were 71.1% of the year's grants [4].

What strikes me here is that tiny fraction. Investment permits are less than eight-tenths of one percent of Portugal's grants in the year. Europe's door is a queue. The queue runs on paperwork.

About one in six of the residents counted, 261,443 people, hold no valid residence card at all [4]. They are waiting on a ruling, a renewal slot, or a temporary-protection status that is not a permit.

The last price on that road is time. Since May 19, 2026, most applicants from outside the EU and the Portuguese-speaking bloc need ten years of legal residence. Only then can they ask for citizenship.

Europeans and nationals of Portuguese-speaking countries need seven. The old rule was five for everyone [5][6].

## By the numbers

- **$350,000 — Argentina's cash route:** a payment to the treasury that is never returned, or $800,000 in a bond; a family of four pays $500,000 [1][3].
- **0.79% — Portugal's investment share:** 2,685 investment permits out of 337,880 titles granted in 2025 [4].
- **261,443 — residents without a valid card:** one in six of the 1,585,854 people the report counts [4].
- **Ten years — the citizenship clock:** the wait for most applicants from outside the EU and the Portuguese-speaking bloc who file from May 19, 2026; Europeans wait seven [5][6].

## What I'd watch:

What I am watching is not the Argentine price. It is the paper standing between the cash and the passport.

- **Argentina's missing rules.** The Agency for Citizenship-by-Investment Programmes leads the screen, with the state intelligence service and financial-crime investigators [1]. The National Directorate of Migration has the final word. I would want to know what the rules still to come say about dependent ages, refunds after a refusal, and how long a file takes. That is the gap between a purchase and an application.
- **Portugal's shrinking share.** If the 0.79% keeps falling while the work route takes up the slack, the cash channel into Europe is far smaller than the trade built around it claims.
- **The two different assets.** A passport is a travel paper and a claim on consular help. A residence card, held long enough, is a claim on living and working across 27 countries. Which one a buyer needs is the question the brochures never ask together.
- **The tax that follows the passport.** A second nationality does very little to a United States return filed by a citizen abroad. That part tends to stay on the shelf.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that neither country's headline number says what it first appears to.

Argentina's $350,000 is not a price for living there. It is cash for the treasury, vetted by intelligence and financial-crime agencies, and the money is non-refundable whatever the answer. What the buyer gets is a passport: travel access, consular help, a second nationality. Not a right to reside in Europe [1][3].

Portugal's foreign resident count is not a count of people holding a valid card. The report says so itself. The total includes 66,740 people on temporary protection and 76,907 whose titles had lapsed. It also carries 24,476 family cases still awaiting a decision [4].

The country is not filling up either. Its own summary calls the stock a sign that new arrivals have steadied.

Cash buys the Argentine paper and skips the clock. Time buys the European right to live and work, and no part of that is for sale. Whether the trade is worth $350,000 turns on which of the two a buyer actually needs. Neither announcement asks that question.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Argentina now sells citizenship for a $350,000 treasury payment or an $800,000 bond, with applications from the fourth quarter of 2026. Portugal's 2025 migration data shows the EU route running on work and waiting instead.
- **Why It Matters:** For anyone weighing a second passport, the two prices are not the same thing. Argentina sells a passport within a quarter. Portugal sells a right to live there. Since May 19, 2026, most applicants from outside the EU and the Portuguese-speaking bloc must wait ten years before citizenship is even on the table.
- **What I'd Watch:** The detail that decides both outcomes is procedural, so I am watching the paper on each side.
  - **Argentina's rules:** the rules still to come, which will set how dependents are priced, whether funds return after a refusal, and how long a file takes.
  - **Portugal's investment share:** 2,685 investment permits against 337,880 grants is 0.79% of the year. The direction of that share says whether Europe's cash path is shrinking or only quiet.
  - **The queue itself:** 261,443 of Portugal's 1,585,854 residents held no valid card at the reporting date, which is where a ten-year clock actually bites.
- **The Catch:** A $350,000 wire to Argentina buys a travel paper, not a European home, and the treasury keeps the money either way. Portugal's ten-year clock applies to filings from May 19, 2026, and its headline count includes people still waiting for a decision.
