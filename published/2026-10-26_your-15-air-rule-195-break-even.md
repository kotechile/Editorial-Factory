Your $15 to $20 air rule sits an order of magnitude below the break-even because it measures freight against cargo value while the bill is a cost of time, and that gap is the whole decision. A flight pays for itself across a month of sea transit once the cargo is worth near $195 a kilo. Take one 2026 rate table's own two ends, $4.50 a kilo by air against $0.50 by sea [2], apply a 25% carrying rate [3][4], and a $4.00 premium buys back this much cover:

| Cargo value | Days of cover the $4.00 premium buys |
| :--- | :--- |
| $50 a kilo | 117 days |
| $200 a kilo | 29 days |
| $500 a kilo | 12 days |

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Two tests share one name, and they measure different things. The first is a freight-to-value ratio: air is called cheap when the freight bill stays under 10% to 15% of the cargo's worth [2], which is where a $15 to $20 a kilo rule lands once air costs two or three dollars a kilo.

The second tests time, and one freight guide prints it in full: carrying cost equals cargo value times the annual carrying rate times the extra days, over 365, with 20% to 30% for the rate [3]. Hold a kilo of $17.50 cargo for 30 days at 25% and the carrying cost comes to 36 cents.

Then the same page sets its $15 to $20 rule under that formula [3], and the two do not meet. My read is that the page is describing two different purchases, one measuring freight against value and the other pricing a day of stock. The premium in the same publishers' own rate tables runs $2.70 to $7.30 a kilo [1][2]. For the rule to hold, the carrying rate would have to reach eleven times the top of the band that page tells you to load [3][4].

A rate that high is less an error than an admission. Below about $195 a kilo, what the flight buys is margin: stock that sells sooner, a promo that ships on time, a line that does not stop. That is a real buy and it deserves its own budget line, but it is not a 20% to 30% charge on the balance sheet. Treating it as one is how teams end up flying goods that should float, or holding four months of cover to dodge the premium.

The 30-day gap is a midpoint, not a law. Air runs 5 to 8 days door to door and sea runs 22 to 45 [1], so a 14-day gap pushes the break-even to $417 a kilo and a 40-day gap pulls it down to $146. The fewer days a flight saves, the more the cargo has to be worth before the trip pays.

## By the numbers

- **$194.67 — Break-even cargo value:** 365 times the premium, over 30 days at 25%, gives $194.67 a kilo; the same sum on July's $3.12 air spot against a $0.80 sea rate falls to $113 [1][2][3][5].
- **278% — Rate behind the rule:** the yearly carrying rate that makes a $17.50-a-kilo threshold break even against a $4.00 premium over 30 days is 365 times $4.00 over $17.50 times 30, or 2.78 [3][4][5].
- **$3.12 a kilo — July air spot rate:** world air cargo spot rates averaged $3.12 a kilo in July 2026, up 28% on the year and down 6% on the month [5].

## What I'd watch:

- **The premium's hidden parts:** a 2026 guide breaks out the add-ons beneath the base air rate, screening at $0.10 to $0.25 a kilo, airport handling at $0.15 to $0.35 and a congestion fee at $0.25 to $1.00 [1]. Each one widens the premium the sum rests on, and most quotes bury them.
- **The sea rate you divide against:** full container rates run $0.20 to $0.50 a kilo against $0.30 to $0.80 for part loads [2], so a part-load lane flatters air by shrinking the premium it is measured against.
- **The days the service really saves:** express air quotes 2 to 3 days against 5 to 7 for standard air [2], and paying up for the last few rarely clears two days of holding on goods well under $500 a kilo.
- **The credit nobody books:** a shorter, steadier lead time also shrinks the safety stock a node must hold. My read is that this credit is real, hard to price, and left out of the sum above on purpose, which makes the break-even a cautious one.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

These are published guide bands rather than signed rates. Nothing here prices a named shipper's premium, and a negotiated air deal can sit well under the $4.50 a table shows [2]. The spot figure is a monthly average that rose 28% in a year [5], so the $113 it implies moves with the market and should never be written into a policy.

The bigger caveat decides the argument. Holding cost is not the only thing a flight buys, and the guides' rule is less a mistake than an answer to a different question. When a missed shipment costs a promo, a contract penalty or an idle line, the carrying rate is the wrong base, and the rule can be right for reasons this sum cannot see.

What the figures settle is where to look, and the part I keep circling is that the cheaper figure is the one that gets repeated. Below the break-even, a 30-day gap will not pay for itself out of holding cost, so a flight defended with the 20% to 30% rate is defended with a figure that does not reach. Cargo value per kilo, a number already on the invoice, decides a question usually argued as a service-level debate, and [the carrying rate to load](/inventory-carrying-cost-rate/) is the other half of that decision.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The choice between air expedites and regional buffer is one division — the air premium per kilo over the daily cost of holding a kilo — and it puts the break-even an order of magnitude above the rule most planning teams inherit.
- **Why It Matters:** The popular rule is a freight-to-value ratio, not a cost-of-time sum, so teams citing it are buying margin while believing they are saving balance-sheet cost, and the same division tells them which items should never fly.
- **What I'd Watch:** Three inputs swing the answer, and each is checkable before the next shipment books.
  - **The premium's parts:** the add-on fees beneath the base air rate, from screening to congestion, which widen the premium the sum runs on.
  - **The sea comparator:** full container rates against part-load rates, because the lower sea figure makes the premium look bigger and air look worse.
  - **The second credit:** the safety stock a shorter lead time removes, which the sum leaves out and which therefore makes the break-even a cautious one.
- **The Catch:** The rate bands are published guides rather than signed prices, the spot average moves monthly, and the sum prices holding cost only, so a flight bought for margin, penalties or a stopped line still needs its own case.
