A public adjuster's fee comes from the insurer's whole check, not from the extra dollars the adjuster wins [1]. So a 20 percent deal only breaks even when the offer grows by 25 percent, a gap the contract hides. Florida, New York and Louisiana each cap what an adjuster may take, and the caps are built on different ideas [1][2][3]. The trade group that tracks these claims says adjusters charge up to 15 percent, but state limits vary [4]. The cap is not the whole story, because your deductible comes off the check before any share applies [1].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Public adjusting is a licensed trade with one job: argue a loss up with the insurer that owes it.

What strikes me here is how these three states handle the conflict, because the owner pays the adjuster direct [1][2][3]. Florida uses a ladder that shifts based on how fast the insurer pays and whether the governor declared an emergency. New York treats the fee as a flat rate, with a narrow break for claims reopened later. Louisiana kills the conflict by paying the adjuster by the hour if the insurer moves fast [1][2][3].

| | Cap | What it is charged on | Timing rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| Florida | 20 percent, and 10 percent for claims from an event under a governor's emergency order during the year after it | Claim payments to the owner, less lawyer fees and costs, and less the deductible | One percent where the insurer pays the policy limit and does so within 14 days of the loss or 10 days of the contract; nothing at all on a part of the claim paid or promised before the contract was signed |
| New York | 12.5 percent | The money paid out, for work done | Up to 20 percent on a claim reopened later, but only while the whole fee stays at or below 12.5 percent of the full payment |
| Louisiana | A share of each payment, under a written fee deal | Each check the insurer issues | Where the insurer pays or promises in writing to pay the policy limit within 72 hours of the loss being reported, the adjuster is paid for time and costs rather than a share |

Two details in the Florida rules matter more than the cap. The fee cannot rest on the deductible part of a claim. It also cannot rest on hotel or rental money unless the owner signs a separate form [1]. Both rules shrink the base the share is counted on.

## By the numbers

- **25 percent — The required lift:** An owner who keeps 80 cents of each dollar needs the offer to hit 1.25 times the old one before the fee pays for itself [1].
- **$4,012 — Average claim fee:** A fifth of the $20,062 average paid per home claim in 2023 goes to the adjuster under a standard contract [1][5].
- **$2,508 — New York cap:** That state's rate on the same average claim leaves about $1,500 more in the owner's pocket [2][5].
- **14.3 percent — New York lift:** One divided by 0.875 is 1.1428, so under the lower cap the offer must grow by about a seventh to pay the adjuster [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

I am watching three clauses that decide what the contract is worth, because only one of them is the rate.

- **The base pool:** A fee on money the insurer was always going to pay acts like a tax on your own claim, so what the adjuster counts as his share is the first question [1].
- **The prior offer:** Florida bars a fee on any part of the claim the insurer had already paid or promised before the contract date [1]. An adjuster who shows up after the first quote cannot bill for money already on its way.
- **The exit window:** A no-questions cancel right runs for 10 days after signing [1]. A written quote that fails to arrive within 60 days also lets the owner walk away at no cost.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that the caps are fair, but the math still favors one side. The adjuster gets paid whether or not his work moved the number.

The break-even assumes the lift is real and down to the adjuster. None of the three states prints a figure for the average lift a public adjuster wins, and no source behind this piece puts a number on it [1][2][3]. So your own first offer is the only start point the math can run against.

A policy-limit payout is the clearest case. When the insurer has already promised the full limit, there is nothing to argue up. This is why Florida drops the fee to one percent and Louisiana pays for time only [1][3]. Those rungs exist because a share deal would otherwise charge well for a payout that was never in doubt.

Timing decides which rule applies at all. Louisiana counts from the moment the loss is reported, so the same contract can pay hourly on one claim and a share on the next [3].

What none of this measures is a denied claim being reopened. A claim closed with no pay is where a share deal is easiest to defend, because the other choice is nothing. The deductible sets what the insurer pays before any cap applies, making it the first thing to price beside the fee. You can run that break-even math in [the deductible half of the declarations page](/your-5-deductible-risks-your-claim/).

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A public adjuster takes a fee from the whole insurance check rather than the extra money they win, so the deal is priced against the whole payout.
- **Why It Matters:** At a normal cut the payout has to grow by more than the fee itself before the owner is whole, and the deductible and the hotel money sit outside the base the share is counted on.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The base pool:** What the share is counted on, and whether the deductible and living costs are inside or outside it.
  - **The prior offer:** Money already paid or promised before the contract date, because one state allows no fee on it.
  - **The exit window:** The cancel window and the written-quote deadline that make the contract reversible.
- **The Catch:** No printed figure measures the average lift an adjuster wins, and the caps differ enough that one claim carries three prices.
