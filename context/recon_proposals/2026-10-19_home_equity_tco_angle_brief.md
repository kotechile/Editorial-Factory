**Emergent Collision Point:** The two prices inside one financed mechanical upgrade move on different clocks, and neither is locked. The equipment price is what the homeowner signs — a number fixed at the moment of sale, on a list that was repriced again this month. The cost of the money is not on that quote at all: on a HELOC it is prime plus a margin, reset monthly, and prime moved to 7.00% on 2026-09-17 after the Federal Reserve's 12–0 quarter-point increase. So the improving side of the equation (the efficiency rating, set at install, degrading slowly with age) is the only fixed term, while both cost terms float. Neither document says this: ICE prices the loan stock and never touches equipment; ACHR prices equipment and never touches the loan that buys it.
**Hook:** A homeowner who finances a heat-pump or air-conditioning replacement this fall approves a firm equipment price and, in the same breath, takes on a loan whose rate resets every month off prime — and in October those two numbers moved in the same direction, on the same calendar, without either vendor or lender mentioning the other.
**Tension:** The Fed's first increase since 2023 (2026-09-16) was aimed at energy-driven inflation, and it lands on the one household liability that reprices fastest. ICE also shows the migration: ARMs are more than 11% of rate locks, the most in nearly four years, and 3.1 million first-lien ARMs are active — the most in five and a half years. On the other side, manufacturer price letters dated October 1 through October 31 push the equipment up while remodeler nonlabor input costs are running 6.6% year over year, their fastest in this cycle. Who it hurts: the household that financed a replacement on a payback model holding the rate constant. Who it helps: cash buyers, and anyone who locks a fixed-rate second while list prices are still this side of the next letter.
**Target reader:** pro_homeowner — professional homeowner and asset manager, 35–45, financially literate, wants TCO depreciation math and gotchas, not contractor marketing.
**Single claim to defend:** For a financed mechanical upgrade in late 2026, only the equipment price and the efficiency rating are fixed at signing — the cost of money floats monthly and the equipment's replacement price floats on list letters — so a payback model that holds the rate constant overstates the return, and the honest comparison is cash versus a fixed-rate second versus a floating line, not upgrade versus no upgrade.
