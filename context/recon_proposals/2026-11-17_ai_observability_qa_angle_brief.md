**Angle Type:** Synthesis (Cross-Topic Fusion)
**Winner:** Your agent's fault handling already sees the fault — the number only moves when the harness is allowed to stop the effect, and the second-biggest lever is permission to say "unknown", not better advice.

**Signal A (Anchor 1):** Acharjee Dip and Motlhabi, "Invariant-Aware Harness Enforcement to Protect LLM Agents from Silent API Failures," arXiv:2611.20595, submitted 16 November 2026 (Genentech / Virginia Tech — https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20595). Silent faults appear in 11 of 15 public research APIs, 10 of which answer a misnamed filter with HTTP 200 and the unfiltered count (ChEMBL returns 2,921,148 where the filtered count is 4,225, 691 times too large). A consistency-check judge flags 82.4% of faulted runs, and as *advice* it still leaves 71.2% of answers wrong: agents report the flagged count anyway in 77.4% of flagged runs. In a controlled table over the same 7 open models and 2 seeds, wrong answers run 74.0% with no guard, 70.1% with a warning, 18.2% with a warning that is also permitted to abstain, and 1.7% when the harness withholds the value.

**Signal B (Anchor 2):** "Recovery Gone Too Far: Understanding and Repairing Risky Fallback in LLM Agents," arXiv:2611.19886, submitted 15 November 2026 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.19886). Across 304 scenarios (150 normal, 154 risky), eight domains and 55 real CVEs, four models commit action-boundary violations in 23.5% to 96.8% of risky scenarios — while identifying the boundary in 69% to 89% of the scenarios in which they cross it. Three mainstream mitigations all fail, each prevented violation costing 0.62 to 0.99 normal successes. Their monitor withholds the call once and hands the decision back to the agent, cutting violations from 67.5% to 16.2% at 66 ms per check, with normal success 98.0% to 97.3% on Qwen and 93.3% to 91.3% on Gemma, and equally effective on Claude Code and Codex.

**Third primary leg (carried as measurement, not as a third anchor):** "AgentFaultBench: State-Verified Evaluation of Tool-Fault Recovery," arXiv:2611.20711, submitted 16 November 2026 (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.20711). In 1,944 planned runs across three models and two frameworks, guided recovery advice moves success by −8.33, 0.00 and +1.06 percentage points against a neutral reminder — on GPT-4o mini full success falls from 142/216 to 124/216. A revised policy tested on six tasks it had not seen gains 161/192 against 142/192 (+9.90 to +10.42 pp), which is the same intervention working once it is tested rather than assumed.

**Industry leg (corroboration, not an anchor):** Datadog's FETCh write-up, observed in-window on 2026-11-16 (https://www.datadoghq.com/blog/engineering/continuous-formal-spec-validation/), describes TLA+ specifications compiled to Rust runtime monitors over 1,300+ routing pods at about 2% CPU at p90, and a conflicting-placement defect that had persisted "for about a year" and was found by the monitor rather than by testing. It carries no publication date on the page and measures no agent, so it is friction — proof that enforcement-style checks are production-cheap — and never a dual-authority anchor.

**Emergent Collision Point:** Each paper measures one fault class and stops at its own repair. Paper A asks why a model that is told its tool result is wrong answers anyway, and finds the flag is visible (confidence drops from 1.00 to 0.43) and ignored. Paper B asks why a model that has already identified an action boundary crosses it anyway, and finds the same shape from the other direction: 69% to 89% of crossings come with the boundary identified. Neither paper states the consequence, which is about where eval and QA budget goes: the entire product category built to make the model *notice* — checklists, reviewer models, judge verdicts, warnings attached to a retry, guided error messages — is aimed at a capability the models already demonstrate on the same runs where they fail. What separates a 74.0% wrong-answer rate from a 1.7% one is not a better detector but whether the harness may *stop the effect*. And the runner-up lever is not advice either: it is permission to answer "cannot be determined", which alone moves 70.1% to 18.2%. Put together, the release gate has an ordering problem nobody prices: enforcement, then permission, then evidence, then advice — and most teams buy them in the reverse order.

**Hook:** A research-API call ignores a filter, returns HTTP 200 and a count 691 times too large. The agent is warned that the count failed a consistency check; its stated confidence falls from 1.00 to 0.43, it mentions the warning — and it prints the wrong number anyway, in 77.4% of the runs where a judge flagged it. The same check, wired so the harness withholds the value, leaves 1.7% of answers wrong.

**Tension:** Agent QA has spent two years improving the judgement layer — better judges, reviewers, rubric prompts, recovery hints in the retry message — on the premise that faults get through because nothing notices them. This fortnight's two papers measure the premise and it is wrong twice, independently: detection is already there (77.4% of flagged runs report the flagged number; 69% to 89% of boundary crossings have the boundary identified), and so is the advice channel, which AgentFaultBench measures at −8.33 points on the weakest model. What is missing is a deny path: code that owns the tool result and can refuse to release it. That is unglamorous, it is cheap (66 ms a call, 2% CPU on a production sidecar), and it is nobody's roadmap, because it does not show up in a dashboard as an accuracy improvement — it shows up as abstention, which reads like a coverage regression. The teams hurt are the ones with the most sophisticated judge stack and no deny path; the ones helped are willing to report a wrong-answer rate and a coverage number side by side, because the trade is the product.

**Target reader:** evals_infra_eng (AI QA & Evals Infrastructure Engineer)
**Single claim to defend:** For tool faults, the intervention that changes outcomes is the one that withholds the effect, not the one that tells the model something — because detection and advice are already present and already ignored — so an agent release gate needs a deny path and an explicit "may abstain" permission before it needs a better judge, and it has to report the coverage it gave up to get there.
