# Angle Brief: agentic_ai — 2026-10-26

**Angle Type:** Synthesis (Cross-Topic Fusion)
**Winner:** Your Agent's Contract Gap: 63% Unmet
**Scores:** E=9.5 A=9 S=9.5 → Composite=9.4

**Signal A (Anchor 1):** When Code Becomes an LLM Call: An Empirical Study of Call-Site Contracts in Agentic Systems (2026-10-17 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.24545). 496 LLM call sites in 40 popular open-source agentic systems, 13.4 MLOC across four languages, every analysis step checked by human reviewers with 17,428 judgments (Gwet's AC1 0.91 to 0.99, 604 disagreements adjudicated). 48% of model outputs reach control flow or execution, and 71% are consumed only by the program or by other models; 73% of the decision-relevant outputs never reach a person. Programs enforce 78% of their constraints in code, yet 67% of prompt-phase constraints are only requested from the model. 82% of call sites check before and after the call, but 63% of the expected constraints are unmet and only 5.3% of call sites satisfy their whole contract; ten patterns explain 99% of the 1,697 gaps and half of them (49%) arise after the call. 114 gaps sit on a threat chain and 38 of their 60 mechanisms trigger at runtime; a patch of 1 to 366 lines stops the original payload for all 38 and blocks 23 completely. The worked case: Gemini CLI's opt-in policy enforcer has six return paths, five of them allow the call, so the model is asked the right question and the program does not enforce the answer.

**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Microsoft Execution Containers (MXC) 1.0.0, policy-driven containment for AI agents (2026-10-07 — https://blogs.windows.com/windowsdeveloper/2026/10/07/microsoft-execution-containers-policy-driven-containment-for-ai-agents/). Announced generally available by Logan Iyer, corporate vice president for the Windows platform: a policy-driven execution layer for untrusted or dynamically generated workloads that can contain model-generated output, plugins, tools, the agent harness, or the entire agent. Developers and IT declare the files, network destinations, processes and desktop access a workload needs, and MXC enforces that boundary with a process container (Windows 11, macOS, Linux), a session container (Windows 11 only), a WSL container (Windows 11 only) or a MicroVM (Windows 11 and Linux, experimental). The policy "remains outside the agent workload's control", with three modes — enforcement, learning and permissive — and the stated premise is that "an agent cannot be its own security authority". Corroborating leg, same month: NVIDIA OpenShell (surfaced 2026-10-22 — https://github.com/NVIDIA/OpenShell) enforces policy on every file access, system call and network connection at the kernel, keeps credentials out of the sandbox, and formally verifies a policy change before it applies.

**Signal C (third leg, carried as the mechanism and the price):** StateMirror: Pre-Execution Detection of Hallucinated Tool Calls in Coding Agents (2026-10-23 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.32648). A study of 4,398 agent trajectories finds that an invalid action costs a median of 3 additional interaction steps and 22.4K tokens to recover. StateMirror intercepts actions at the harness boundary, extracts state predicates with AST and command analysis, and validates them against the live repository: 0.940 precision and 0.817 F1 on 2,508 actions, 35.3% above the strongest model judge, median detection latency 17.73 seconds to 1.50 milliseconds with zero token consumption. Pre-execution feedback lets agents rectify over 95% of hallucinated calls; on 79 live tasks it cuts execution time 15.8% and token use 11.8% while holding task resolution.

**Emergent Collision Point:** Both legs measure or ship the same decade's answer to "how do you stop an agent doing the wrong thing", and they are looking at different layers. The containment layer that reached general availability this month — and the second one open-sourced in the same month — enforces what a workload may touch on the machine: files, sockets, processes, the desktop. The measurement takes the program inside that boundary and asks what each model call must guarantee, and finds 63% of those guarantees unmet and only 5.3% of call sites whole, with the sharpest failure being a program that asks a model a policy question and then allows the call on five of six return paths. Contained code can still be wrong code: a workload caged away from your Documents folder may still write an unapproved commit, dispatch an unapproved payment, or pass one skill's output into the next as trusted. Neither anchor states the collision. The paper has no containment in scope; the vendors have no per-call contract in scope. Fused, they say the enforcement budget is going to the line the agent cannot cross and not to the line it crosses on every turn — and the third leg prices the second line: a deterministic predicate check at the boundary costs about 1.5 milliseconds and no tokens, against a 17.7-second, 22,400-token recovery loop when the bad call is allowed through.

**Hook:** This month the runtime layer got productised twice — Microsoft put execution containers into general availability and NVIDIA open-sourced a policy-enforced sandbox — while a study of 496 call sites in 40 agent systems found 63% of the constraints those calls depend on are not met, and the only figure anyone has for checking them at the boundary is 1.5 milliseconds.

**Tension:** Sandboxing answers a real question and answers it well: what can the workload reach. It leaves untouched the question the program asks on every turn — is this specific reply acceptable enough to act on. That gap is not a capability problem, so a stronger model does not close it, and it is not a perimeter problem, so a tighter container does not either. It hurts the platform team that has already bought containment and now believes the remaining risk is model error; it helps the team that writes its constraints as code, because the measured remedy is cheap and the measured damage is concentrated where nobody is watching — 73% of decision-relevant outputs never reach a person.

**Target reader:** ai_architect

**Single claim to defend:** For an agent in production, containment and call-site contracts are two different controls at two different boundaries, and this window's evidence says the second is where reliability and security actually leak: 63% of the constraints a call site depends on are unmet, only 5.3% of call sites satisfy their whole contract, and the deterministic check that would close the gap costs about 1.5 milliseconds and no tokens — so a runtime strategy built only on sandboxes is enforcing the boundary the agent rarely crosses and auditing none of the ones it crosses per turn.
