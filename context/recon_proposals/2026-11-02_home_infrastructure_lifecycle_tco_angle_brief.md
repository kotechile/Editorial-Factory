**Signal A (Anchor 1):** RMI, *The Opportunity for Heat Pumps to Put Downward Pressure on Electricity Rates*, published 2026-10-15 — roughly 109 million US homes (three in four) could heat and cool with heat pumps inside today's winter grid headroom, 86 million more than the 23 million that have one; an all-electric-only strategy would cut residential rates by an average of 6% in summer-peaking states ($6.7 billion a year, $86 per customer), while a mix of all-electric and hybrid heat pumps would cut them 13% ($15.6 billion, $200 per customer) because the mix lifts deployment potential about 65% — Illinois goes from 30% to 100% of homes, split 21% all-electric and 79% hybrid, on 8.9 gigawatts of winter headroom (https://rmi.org/resources/the-opportunity-for-heat-pumps-to-put-downward-pressure-on-electricity-rates).
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** J.D. Power, *Quarterly Share of Wallet Tracker: U.S. Utilities, October 2026* (Q3 2026 wave) — the average monthly residential electric bill was $188, up 5.6% from $178 a year earlier; gas $119, up 5.3% from $113; water $102, down 4.7% from $107; the three total $409 a month, up 2.8% year over year and equal to 5.6% of a median household income of $87,460; 23% of electric customers still owed the utility a balance, against 22% a year earlier (https://www.jdpower.com/business/news/quarterly-share-of-wallet-tracker-u-s-utilities-october-2026).
**Emergent Collision Point:** The two documents answer the same question from opposite ends and neither states the result. RMI prices the heat pump as a grid asset: the saving is a percentage off the residential rate, collected by every customer on the system, and its size depends on how much winter load the grid can absorb. J.D. Power prices it as a household cost: $188 a month for electricity, up 5.6%, with nearly a quarter of customers already carrying a balance. Put together, the household that finances the equipment creates a benefit it shares with the neighbours who bought nothing, while its own bill falls only as far as the rate design lets it — so the sizing choice between an all-electric heat pump and a hybrid one, which a contractor sells as a comfort and cost trade-off, is actually the variable that sets the size of the public benefit.
**Hook:** A heat pump can cut a state's residential electric rate by 13% — about $200 per customer a year — and the neighbour who never bought one collects the same $200, while the household that paid for the equipment carries the whole capital cost alone.
**Tension:** The headroom is real and unused, but it is not the homeowner's to sell. A utility books the extra winter sales and, in most of the country, files a rate case to pass the lower average cost through — which is why RMI's own third policy recommendation is a rate redesign, not a rebate. If a utility recovers more of its fixed cost in a monthly charge instead of a per-kilowatt-hour price, the buyer's bill falls less than the rate does, and the machine's financial case quietly moves from the meter to the system. Meanwhile the households most exposed to the $188 bill, the 23% in arrears, are the least able to buy the equipment that creates the saving.
**Target reader:** pro_homeowner
**Single claim to defend:** A heat pump's largest verified financial effect is a shared rate cut whose size depends on which kind of heat pump gets installed, so the bill-savings line on a quote is the weakest number in the decision and the sizing choice is the strongest.
