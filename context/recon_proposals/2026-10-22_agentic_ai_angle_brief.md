**Winner:** Your Agent Team Loses 28 Points: The 188x Bill

**Signal A (Anchor 1):** Does Multi-Agent Collaboration Pay When Agents Serve Users? (2026-10-20 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27655). A pre-registered test of the field's most-cited coordination rule — that a team helps while the single agent is weak and hurts once it is strong, with the crossing near 45% single-agent accuracy — in the setting where agents now actually work: a conversation with a user under a written policy. Stock single agent against a centralized team built from the same model, six models from three families, about 7,950 simulated conversations over the airline, retail and telecom domains of the dual-control benchmark, one completion-token ledger per episode. The team is worse in 12 of the 13 valid cells and tied in the thirteenth, with a weighted mean gain of -28 points (95% CI [-44, -12]). On the two cells where the rule predicts a benefit it loses by 13 and 22 points, and without a budget ceiling it at best draws level at 5.5 times the single agent's token spend. It does not detectably reduce wrongful database writes; it converts other failures into budget exhaustion and inaction.

**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Do Agent Teams Pay Off? A Case Study on Vibe Code Bench (2026-10-09 — https://www.vals.ai/blogs/multi-agent-vibe-code-bench). Fifty full-stack app builds from a product spec, graded by browser agents clicking through the deployed app, with GPT 6 Sol and Claude Opus 5.5 run as a single agent and as a team at medium and max reasoning effort, one run per setup. Teams cost 1.8 to 5.1 times as much and only one of four score differences clears significance: Sol's medium-effort team gains 7.3 points (p = 0.005, +2.3 to +12.4), while the other three run from -0.3 to +3.4 points at p = 0.16 to 0.81. Sol's max-effort single agent cost $4.82 an app and scored 89.0%, against $3.07 and 84.9% for its medium-effort team. Opus's max-effort team reached 93.2% for $122 an app against 91.5% for $4.08 at medium (p = 0.36) and took up to 2.3 times as long. Most of the extra spend is cached input: at max effort Opus's subagents read a median 224 million cached tokens an app against 17 million for the lead and 55 million for the single agent.

**Signal C (third leg, carried as the mechanism):** What Claude Code's multi-agent code review costs and finds (2026-10-21 — https://quickchat.ai/post/claude-code-multi-agent-code-review). The shipped local /code-review command in Claude Code 2.1.303, run on 20 pull requests from a public benchmark with Sonnet 5.5 on every agent: the max fan-out (16.5 agents a review) matched 23 of 27 serious answer-key issues against 21 for the single-agent setting, and the same 47 of 65 issues overall, with the author stating two issues is about the scoring noise on that benchmark. Unmatched claims per PR rose from 11.9 to 23.9, wall-clock from 40 seconds to 56 minutes, and recorded cost from $0.11 to $20.95 per finished review — a recorded subtotal 188 times the single agent's ($398.03 against $2.12). An all-Opus max review on one 97-line PR spent $40.64 and printed nothing when a 30-minute limit stopped it.

**Emergent Collision Point:** None of the three measurements is about the same task, and each one on its own leaves an escape hatch. The dual-control paper can be read as one benchmark's quirk; the app-build study has a genuine win in it, so a reader concludes teams pay if you pick the right model and effort; the code-review measurement is one vendor's write-up about one command. Collided, they close each other's exits and name a mechanism none of them states. The cost paper shows the team's bill is *re-sent context* — each subagent carries its own copy of the spec and its working state, and that copy is charged on every call — rather than extra reasoning. The review measurement shows what the team actually produces with that spend: twice the claims and the same count of verified issues, i.e. the marginal output of the second agent is *unverified claims*, and the verification step is what has to be bought separately. The dual-control paper shows why the setting decides it: once a user and a policy are in the loop, the failure mode a team converts into is budget exhaustion and inaction, so the extra agents cannot even be credited with a wrong write they prevented. The emergent thesis: a second agent is not a capability purchase but a *claim-generation* purchase, its unit price is the context it re-reads, and the only configuration in the window that paid was the cheapest team on the board — the one whose lead did its own end-to-end testing instead of adding a verifier.

**Hook:** Eight thousand simulated customer conversations, fifty app builds and twenty pull requests, and the same result three ways: the agent team costs 1.8 to 188 times as much and moves the verified score by nothing you could stand behind.

**Tension:** The industry's multi-agent story was priced on autonomous benchmarks where no user takes part, and the field's own rule — coordination helps only while the single agent is weak — was derived there. Move to a user under a written policy and the rule stops predicting anything: the team loses in 12 of 13 cells. Meanwhile the vendor-side numbers look like a win because the win was measured at the cheapest effort, and the expensive configuration quietly costs 30 times more for a 1.6-point difference that is not significant. It helps architects who gate the decision on a paired measurement from their own setting; it threatens the platform team whose roadmap is "add a supervisor, add a reviewer, add a verifier", because each added agent is a new copy of the context and a new stream of claims nobody has scored.
