**Signal A (Anchor 1):** *Kairos: Transactional Security Enforcement at the System Boundary for LLM Agents*, arXiv:2610.18457v1, submitted **2026-10-13**. The paper states the structural problem the whole change-management market is built on top of: "Legitimate work and the first step of an attack often look alike when an individual action is observed, and what separates them lies later in the trace, so a monitor limited to allow and deny is forced to decide before the evidence accumulates." Its answer is a "reference monitor at the isolation boundary of an unmodified, opaque agent, where a tool call, a shell command, and a script the agent writes all cross the boundary as the same file and network operations"; "A declarative policy that states which sources are trusted decides most effects deterministically and defers the rest, which are held while the agent continues speculatively"; and "A decision oracle with bounded authority, such as a language model or a human, releases or refuses each deferred effect on the trace." Measured across "three benchmarks and three agent models": on AgentDojo it "reduces attack success from 47.0% to 2.7% while its benign utility stays at the level of the undefended agent" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.18457).

**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Ørsted's own production numbers for a pre-approved change band, reported by Network World on **2026-10-12**. On an open-source Nautobot platform with custom validators, zones that each carry a security profile and a ServiceNow front door, "the automation team identified lower-risk traffic flows that could be handled with peer review rather than requiring CAB approval. Those preapproved requests account for roughly 60 to 70% of incoming requests, Soares says. Other requests must still go to CAB." João Soares, the company's lead IT security consultant, is explicit that the residual is not negotiable: "If it doesn't fit into this low-risk, pre-approved request type, then we need to ensure it goes to CAB. It cannot be optional." The speed: "A pre-approved request can be deployed in about 10 minutes, including the time for a customer to submit the request and have a teammate review it. Requests requiring CAB approval can still take a few days because they must wait for human review." The record: "Logs and metadata connect firewall rules to the change requests that created them and identify the customer and team associated with that request" (https://www.networkworld.com/article/4229048/orsted-builds-compliance-into-automation-speeds-firewall-changes.html).

**Emergent Collision Point:** Both legs describe the same mechanism from opposite ends of the same year, and neither names the thing they share. Ørsted's number is a *coverage* number: roughly six in ten changes are handled without a change board because a security team decided, in advance and by category, that changes of this type are safe. That decision is made by humans, on paper, before any particular change exists — which is exactly the monitor the paper says cannot work: the category is the thing an attacker steers into, because the first step of an intrusion and a routine request are the same request until later in the trace. Ørsted's own guardrails are stronger than most (peer review still runs, the log ties a rule to its request, the residual cannot bypass the board), and the paper's 47.0% → 2.7% is not a claim that pre-approval is worthless. It is a claim about *where* the line has to be drawn: at the effect that crosses into production, with the ambiguous effects held while the agent keeps working, rather than at a request type approved in a policy workshop. Put an agent in front of that queue — which is what the tools shipped in the same three days do, with "risk-tiered autonomy so that potentially dangerous actions always require explicit human approval" and a "deterministic execution framework" that restores last-known-good configuration on its own — and the request type stops being evidence of intent at all. The operator's sixty per cent is then a bet that a probabilistic drafter cannot produce a request that fits an approved category while carrying an effect nobody approved. The vendor's risk tier is the same bet, made one layer deeper.

**Hook:** In one three-day stretch this October, an energy company published the operating model the industry is copying — about 60 to 70% of its firewall changes never see the change board — a vendor shipped agents that restore device configuration on their own under "risk-tiered autonomy," and a paper measured why that arrangement is the wrong place to draw the line: attack success falls from 47.0% to 2.7% only when the decision moves to the effect crossing the boundary, which is later than the request.

**Tension:** The buyer's instinct is to tighten the category list, add approvals, and slow the fast lane down; every one of those makes the coverage number fall and pushes volume back to a board that already takes days. The paper's result cuts the other way — deferral, not denial, is what preserves utility, and a monitor that only allows or refuses has to be right *before* the trace exists. So the fast lane is not the problem and the change board is not the control: the boundary is. Who this empowers is the team that owns the execution path — the privileged broker, the rollback, the trusted-source policy — and who it threatens is the change-management programme that has been sold as the safety case. Now add the regulator: a computational-load site above 50 MW at 100 kV must comply with its transmission operator's instructions, with exceptions only for what is physically impossible or breaks safety, equipment or statute, and the industry's request to exempt changes a site cannot control was refused. A site that cannot express "this instruction came from the balancing authority" as a policy fact has a boundary that cannot tell an authorised third party from an injected one.
