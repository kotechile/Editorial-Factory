**Thesis:** The $100,000 ceiling is not a limit on how much you exercise — it is a limit on how much grant-date value becomes exercisable for the first time in one calendar year, so the incentive/nonstatutory split is fixed by vesting and by any acceleration clause, before you make a single exercise decision: an evenly vesting four-year grant stays wholly qualified up to about $400,000 of grant-date value, a single-trigger acceleration on a buyout drops that threshold to $100,000, and a tranche exercised after the acceleration is a nonstatutory option in its entirety.

## Figures that ride in prose under these citations (read by eye, not given a row)

- **"for the 1st time" and the ordering rule.** § 422(d)(1) tests options "exercisable for the 1st time by any individual during any calendar year"; § 422(d)(2) applies the test "by taking options into account in the order in which they were granted"; § 422(d)(3) fixes the value "as of the time the option with respect to such stock is granted". Those three clauses are the whole article, and none of them carries a usable leading token of its own.
- **The acceleration rule, verbatim.** 26 CFR § 1.422-4(b)(4): "an option is considered to be first exercisable during a calendar year if the option will become exercisable at any time during the year assuming that any condition on the optionee's ability to exercise the option related to the performance of services is satisfied", and "If the optionee's ability to exercise the option in the year is subject to an acceleration provision, then the option is considered first exercisable in the calendar year in which the acceleration provision is triggered." The same paragraph then says a tranche that becomes first exercisable "by operation of such a provision does not affect the application of the $100,000 limitation with respect to any option (or portion thereof) exercised prior to such acceleration" — which is the warrant for the article's one timing rule.
- **A disqualifying sale does not create room.** § 1.422-4(b)(6): "A disqualifying disposition has no effect on the determination of whether an option exceeds the $100,000 limitation." Quote it; it kills the folk advice that selling the first tranche early frees up the year's allowance.
- **Bifurcation.** § 1.422-4(c)(1) allows an option to be "treated, in part, as an incentive stock option and, in part, as a nonstatutory option"; § 1.422-4(c)(2) says that absent a separate certificate or designation, "shares are treated as first purchased under an incentive stock option to the extent of the $100,000 limitation, and the excess shares are treated as purchased under a nonstatutory option." Also Example 6: an immediately exercisable option worth $150,000 at grant splits into an incentive stock option on $100,000 of stock and a nonstatutory option on $50,000 — which is what early exercise does to a grant.
- **The 10-percent-owner exception is a price and a clock.** § 422(c)(5) switches the § 422(b)(6) disqualification off when the price is at least 110 percent of fair market value and the term is five years or less; § 422(b)(3) sets the ordinary term at 10 years from grant. A founder who crosses 10 percent pays 10 percent more per share for less time.
- **§ 422 was added in 1981.** The amendment note on the § 422 page: "Added Pub. L. 97–34, title II, § 251(a), Aug. 13, 1981, 95 Stat. 256, § 422A" (it was renumbered § 422 by Pub. L. 101–508 in 1990). Do not describe the ceiling as indexed; § 422(d) contains no adjustment formula.
- **What the label is worth.** The ISO's advantage attaches to the spread at exercise (it enters the alternative minimum tax rather than wages), so its value depends on when the reader exercises relative to the 409A, not on the label alone.
