## Drafting rulings (binding)

1. **No national contents percentage.** Write the percentage as a mechanic and give both readings (the Texas guide's 20 percent example, the carrier convention of 50), then say the declarations page holds the reader's own figure. A sentence of the form "contents coverage is 50 percent of the dwelling" is barred.
2. **The cap is a theft cap, not a value cap.** The same ring is covered to the contents limit when it burns and capped when it is taken (row 1). Say both, in that order, and never write "your policy only covers $1,500 of jewelry" without "for theft".
3. **$1,500 is the ISO form's figure, not a national one.** The Texas HOB form in row 3 limits jewelry to $500, so the article says the form decides and the declarations page is the record.
4. **Do not convert the special limit into a claim statistic.** Row 10's 5.33 per 100 house-years and $20,062 severity are the industry's all-peril average (the table excludes Alaska, Texas and Puerto Rico); they are context for how large a $1,500 cap is, not a jewelry-claim number.
5. **No price for the endorsement.** Row 9 gives the raised limits and no premium. The article must not state what scheduling costs; it says the endorsement is priced on the appraised value and names the level that moves (row 9), and leaves the quote to the reader's agent.
6. **Accidental loss is a gap, not a cap.** Row 11 is quoted in prose (the page has no gate-able figure): dropping the ring is not a covered peril at all, which is why the appraisal and the schedule matter more than the limit.
7. **Derived arithmetic the drafter may use, recomputed from these cells:** 50% × $400,000 = $200,000 contents; 20% × $400,000 = $80,000, a $120,000 spread; $1,500 ÷ $200,000 = 0.75%; $1,500 ÷ $80,000 = 1.875%; $1,500 ÷ $9,000 = 16.7% of a $9,000 ring, leaving 83.3% unpaid; $1,500 × 13.4 ≈ $20,062 (scale only); $2,500 vs $4,000 = $1,500 unpaid. No other arithmetic.
