The window closes on a failure class this vertical has not argued: **the load that breaks an agent service is not the load that keeps it broken.** A ten-minute traffic spike on a real agent-serving engine left it at roughly a fifth of its prior throughput an hour later, at a request rate it had been serving all morning. Nothing in the usual gauges — utilization, queue length, memory use — showed the crossing. The mechanism is a loop the engine runs against itself: each call that now recomputes its context takes longer, a session ends only after its last turn, so slow calls keep sessions alive, the live sessions' combined context grows past the cache, and the misses that follow make the next call slower again. Killing the spike's own sessions restored service in two minutes; letting them finish kept the engine down for the rest of the run.

The second measurement in the window turns the same screw on the team rather than the rack. A platform that ships its own agent changes measured what "we rolled it back" is worth: restoring a saved copy returned behaviour exactly to the floor, a regenerated undo did not, and one admitted change flipped one task in six in both directions — 18 up and 20 down — so the average read as nothing while the reach was what no rollback repairs.

The friction is already in production. Zendesk's AI Agents incident ran from 18:00 UTC on 16 October to 08:56 UTC on 21 October, and its own timeline records the recovery work being paused "after it caused delays in another part of the service" — the cure re-degrading the patient, four days after the trigger.
