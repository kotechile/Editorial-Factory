## Notes on the evidence
- **Three hosts, six rows: `demand-planning.com` (three pages of one publisher, so these are one *publisher* but three separate claims), `onetonline.org` and `microsoft.com`.** Rows 1–3 are IBF's own four-part cost model and its realisation curve — a professional body's estimate, not a peer-reviewed measurement, and the article must label them that way. Row 5 is the only *measured* anchor and it is what turns the ranges into people: 250,000 ÷ 107,230 = 2.33 and 750,000 ÷ 107,230 = 6.99.
- **The arithmetic the article turns on must be shown in the piece, not asserted:** one-off cost is acquisition plus implementation, $300,000 to $700,000; the annual line is $250,000 to $750,000 a year, plus improvement at 15% of the one-off; three years therefore costs $1,095,000 to $3,055,000. Against row 4's $1.52 million per point of under-forecast improvement at a $1 billion company, the programme has to buy back 0.7 to 2.0 percentage points of under-forecast to cover three years of its own cost — and that is the honest test, because row 4 states a benefit per point but never states the base its 3-percent pre-tax claim applies to. Say so in the piece rather than completing the claim for it.
- **The licence share is the article's counter-intuitive number and it must be built visibly:** row 6's $210.00 per user per month for 25 planning seats is $63,000 a year; set against row 1's annual line that is 8% to 25% of it, and because row 1 already bundles "any systems expenses" inside the $250,000 to $750,000, three-quarters of the annual bill is people and process rather than software. Show the 25-seat assumption in the sentence — the number is a scenario, not a benchmark.
- **The one rule with numbers the article must land:** a module earns its budget only by reducing the annual line — hours of coordinator time, hours of review attendance per cycle, hours of reconciliation per cycle — and every feature that adds a review step (a scenario round, a consensus session, an exception queue) is a permanent annual cost, so the TCO rises precisely when the tool starts to work. Price a recurring hour at $51.55 (row 5), which is $2,681 a year for one hour a week.
- **Not to be claimed:** that S&OP is not worth running (row 1's own ROI case is 63% annualised at a $500 million organisation, and row 4's benefit arithmetic is the reason to run it); that these ranges are current market prices (they are 2021 estimates, labelled as of that date); that a best-of-breed module is always the wrong buy (the brief prices a decision, it does not pick a vendor); or any integration-cost or sync-latency figure — the earlier runs were right that none retrieves, and this brief does not smuggle one in.
- **The de-dup risk to watch while drafting:** today's news piece already says planning software is funded out of planner payroll. This article must not restate that. Its subject is the opposite end of the same budget — the annual cost of the cycle that exists whether or not a module is bought, and the realisation curve that decides when any of it pays back.
