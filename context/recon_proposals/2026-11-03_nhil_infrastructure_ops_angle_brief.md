**Emergent Collision Point:** Both documents describe the same structural defect at two ends of one wire: the evidence each layer trusts is taken on the source side of the device that isolates it. Google's PLC read voltage upstream of a breaker that was physically open, so a plant capable of carrying the load believed it had a healthy source and issued no start command; the grid's disturbance recorder reads at the point of interconnection, upstream of the facility's own transfer equipment, so a deliberate shed and a fall onto backup produce the same signature. Neither document says this. Google calls it a PLC misconfiguration; the paper calls it an estimation problem. Together they say the failure class is where the sensor sits, which is why adding a second PLC — or a second recorder — does not fix it: both copies read the same wrong node.
**Hook:** At 06:53 BST the building lost one of two utility feeders, and the plant did not notice, because the controller that was supposed to notice had been wired to the side of the breaker that stayed live.
**Tension:** The industry's answer to power risk is duplication — 2N paths, dual PLCs, redundant recorders — and each of those duplicates a *reading*, not an *independence*. The maintenance that makes a plant safer (taking a feed out to repair what a load test found) is the exact state in which the duplication is gone, and the automation has no state input that says so. The grid is now building the same class of blind spot as it accepts hundreds of gigawatts of computational load whose operator can only see electrical quantities at the fence.
