**Signal A (Anchor 1):** 10 CFR 431.110(c), Table 2 — the residential-duty commercial gas storage class (>75 kBtu/hr and ≤105 kBtu/hr and ≤120 gal) must meet a UEF of `0.9297−(0.0009 × Vr)` for equipment manufactured after 2026-10-06, up from `0.6597−(0.0009 × Vr)`; at 75 gallons that is 0.5922 to 0.8622. Compliance date set by the final rule at 88 FR 69686 (2023-10-06). (https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-10/chapter-II/subchapter-D/part-431/subpart-G/section-431.110)
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Harvard JCHS, *The Retrofit Gap*, 2026-10-01 — 23 million homeowners (25.7 percent) are energy insecure; owners earning $150,000+ did 39.5 percent of $139.1 billion in energy-related work while making up 9.9 percent of the energy-insecure, and owners under $30,000 did 8.9 percent of the spending while making up 25.6 percent of the insecure; water heater work alone was 4.5 percent of owners, $1,490 average, $5.9 billion. (https://www.jchs.harvard.edu/sites/default/files/research/files/Harvard_JCHS_Energy_Insecurity_Wedeen_et_al_2026_1.pdf)
**Emergent Collision Point:** A manufacturing standard and a spending survey measure the same object from opposite ends. The rule raises the floor of what can be built into the class of gas tank that large homes actually install — condensing-only, with new venting and a condensate path — while the JCHS tabulations show that the money for energy-related replacement is concentrated among the owners who are least likely to be carrying the energy bill it is meant to lower. So the efficiency gain arrives as a capital requirement for the households with the thinnest margin and as a modest savings for the ones who were already buying premium equipment. Neither document says this: the rule is written about equipment classes, the paper is written about who spends.
**Hook:** The October 6 manufacturing date did not ban a fuel or a size. It removed the cheap version of one specific box — the 75,000-to-105,000 Btu gas tank — so a like-for-like swap in a big house now arrives with a venting and condensate scope that the $1,490 average water-heater job in the federal survey does not include.
**Tension:** The asset has a fixed clock (water heaters are a 7-to-12-year item), so the decision is not whether to replace but when, and whether the replacement is planned or an emergency. Planned owners can pick the class, the vent route and the contractor; unplanned owners take whatever is on the truck. The retrofit gap is the same split measured in dollars: the top of the income distribution does nearly 40 percent of the energy-related work, the bottom does 9 percent — and the bottom carries the higher energy burden. A rule that makes the replacement class more efficient by making it more expensive moves the cost onto exactly the households that defer.
**Target reader:** pro_homeowner
**Single claim to defend:** The October 6 water-heater standard turned a routine like-for-like swap into a condensing retrofit for any house with a 75,000-Btu gas tank, so the honest way to read the change is as a scheduled capital item — and the JCHS retrofit data show that the owners who plan it pay the old price while the ones who wait pay the emergency premium plus the venting scope.
