**Emergent Collision Point:** Both standard answers to China exposure just changed their legal form, in opposite directions. Nearshoring's advantage was never a rate — it was the assumption that USMCA preference is a permanent feature of the ground you stand on. As of the July 1 non-extension, that preference is reviewed every year, on a public docket that closes January 12, 2027, which makes the Mexico case a licence with a renewal date rather than a treaty fact. Reshoring's advantage was supposed to be that nobody can take it away; the flagship onshore expansion of this window is instead capitalized by Japan and part-owned by the U.S. government, which makes its capacity a state-to-state arrangement with a price and an owner rather than a market alternative. Neither source says this. Each describes one instrument; together they say the total-landed-cost comparison is now comparing two political positions, and the ops leader's model prices both as stable inputs.
**Hook:** On October 2 the U.S. Trade Representative opened a public docket on whether the North American trade rules your Mexico quote depends on should keep working — the first of an annual series, because the U.S. declined in July to renew USMCA for another 16 years. Twelve days later, the largest onshore capacity announcement of the month turned out to be funded by Japan, with the U.S. government taking about four percent of the company that will run it.
**Tension:** The hedge you pick is now a political instrument either way. Nearshoring buys proximity and duty-free entry, but the entry rests on an agreement that is now re-reviewed annually while a labor complaint can freeze liquidation at an individual plant — USTR did exactly that at GLM Components in Puebla on October 8, suspending liquidation of the plant's unliquidated entries. Reshoring buys control of the physical line, but the capital arriving to build it is a government-to-government commitment: Japan's money, U.S. equity, a first wafer in 2029. A board that approved a Mexico line on the strength of "USMCA" and a U.S. line on the strength of "ours" approved two positions whose terms are set by other people's calendars.
