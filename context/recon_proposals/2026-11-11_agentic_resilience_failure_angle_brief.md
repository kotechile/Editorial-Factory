**Signal A (Anchor 1):** Attributing Agent Failures to Their History Needs an Executed Counterfactual — a reporting standard for agent failure attribution (2026-11-03 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.05650). Load-bearing finding: hash-identical snapshots restored into identically seeded processes **agreed with one another in 24 of 24 states while differing from the live continuation in 9 and 20 of 24**, and restoring the captured position of the random stream made **all 24** agree with the live run. A claim that an agent's history caused its failure should carry intervention-verified evidence — freeze an executable mid-trajectory state, alter only the history the agent sees, continue, and report a replay certificate matched to the estimand — and none of the published attribution methods (DoVer, REFLECT, Who&When Pro, Causal Agent Replay) reports one. Its second cautionary measurement: a carrier taxonomy's "concentration" could not be separated from benchmark construction (adjusted odds ratio 1.15, interval crossing 1).
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Dependable Long-Horizon LLM Agents — a trajectory-centred empirical study of 95 failed runs across six multi-agent systems from the Who&When and MAD corpora (2026-11-09 — https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.13254). Load-bearing finding: faults enter at a median **0.18 of the run** with a **0.67** median gap to the symptom; **72%** of failed runs already contained an observation contradicting the erroneous belief, and in **88%** of those no agent acted on it; something flagged a deviation in **31%** of runs and **not one response removed the deviated state**; in **37 of 88** Who&When traces the fault's origin precedes the step the corpus's human labels mark as decisive; a local checkpoint alone would have helped in **35 of 95** runs while in **56** the fault sat in a plan or policy a restore would simply replay; and in **26 of the 58** hand-crafted Who&When runs (**45%**) a provider content filter killed the orchestrator and the harness emitted a final answer **no agent had produced**, with the corpus attributing **21 of those 25** runs to an agent other than the orchestrator.

**Emergent Collision Point:** Each paper attacks the same object from opposite ends and neither states the combined result. Signal A says the *method* you use to explain a failure is unsound without an executed intervention, and then shows the instrument that would supply one — a restored checkpoint — does not reproduce the run at all: identical snapshots diverged from the live continuation in 9 and 20 of 24 states until the random stream's position was captured. Signal B says what those unsound labels actually contain: in 45% of the corpus's hand-built failures the agent that gets blamed never wrote the final answer, because a content filter killed the orchestrator and the harness printed one. Put together, the two most common failure artifacts in agent operations — the taxonomy label and the checkpoint replay — fail on their own terms: the label can name an actor who did not act, and the replay the label is supposed to be provable against is not the run. The postmortem therefore records a symptom twice: once in the wrong voice, once in a state that never occurred. Neither paper says the *diagnostic* is misattributed and the *proof* unavailable as one condition; each supplies half.

**Hook:** Open on the arithmetic of a postmortem that passes review — a run that failed at step 41 of 60, a label reading "agent error", a checkpoint restored to prove it — then the measurement that breaks it: restored into identically seeded processes, 24 of 24 snapshots agreed with each other and 9 of 24 disagreed with the live run. The title's promise is satisfied in paragraph one.

**Tension:** The failure-analysis stack the industry is buying (trace judges, seven-category taxonomies, LLM-written postmortems, replay-from-checkpoint) is a labelling system on top of an unverified state. The measured cost lands on the reader's two decisions: what to instrument (origin of the fault, not the step that surfaced it — median gap 0.67 of the run) and what to fix (in 56 of 95 runs the fault lives in a plan or policy, so the restore replays it; only 35 of 95 were genuinely checkpoint-recoverable). It also threatens the benchmark vendors: a taxonomy whose labels can blame a non-acting agent is not a measurement of the model.

**Target reader:** infra_engineer (Infrastructure / ML engineer — deep technical; "real numbers, caveats, reproduction details")

**Single claim to defend:** An agent failure postmortem is a claim about history, and a claim about history is only as good as an executed counterfactual — which requires a replay whose state is provably the live run's; the labels usually written instead name the wrong actor (45% of one hand-labelled corpus blamed an agent for an answer the harness emitted) and the checkpoint that would test them diverges from the run (9 and 20 of 24 states) unless the checkpoint carries the random stream's position.
