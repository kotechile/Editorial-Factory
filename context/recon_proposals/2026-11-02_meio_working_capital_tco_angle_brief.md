**Emergent Collision Point:** Both companies put more stock on the ground in the same nine months, and only one of them paid for it. Caterpillar's inventory absorbed $4.1 billion of cash — twice the prior year's drain — while customer advances returned $4.0 billion, so the build nearly cancelled itself inside working capital and operating cash flow rose 30%. **Hook:** Caterpillar's inventory absorbed $4.1 billion of cash in the first nine months of 2026, twice the prior year's drain, and its operating cash flow still rose 30% — because customers had already handed it $4.0 billion in advances against machines that had not shipped.
**Tension:** The two firms sit at opposite ends of the same timeline. An equipment maker sells years before it builds, so the customer's deposit funds the work in progress; a retailer sells minutes after the truck arrives, so its own balance sheet funds the stock and the borrowing rate is the price of the wait. What changed this quarter is that both chose to carry more, into a demand picture that does not support it everywhere: Tractor Supply's comparable sales rose 0.4% against 3.9% a year earlier, and Caterpillar's mining sales grew mainly because dealers restocked rather than because end users bought. The people this threatens are the planners whose inventory-days target is set without reference to the funding behind it, and the finance teams now underwriting a build at a policy rate that stopped falling — the FOMC held at 3-3/4 to 4 percent with inflation still elevated. The people it rewards are the ones who can move the sale earlier than the build: deposits, progress payments, annual contracts paid up front.
