**The specific material.** Two peer-reviewed studies of battery management in robotic fulfilment systems state, in their own abstracts, that the recovery policy moves throughput time and that ignoring it under-estimates how many robots a building needs. The vendor specification pages publish the duty cycle the arithmetic needs — active operation time at maximum payload, the charging ratio, and the rated number of full charging cycles — and two government pages supply the charger's power draw and the commercial electricity price. From those five numbers the reader can compute four things no prior artifact in this vertical states: how much of an 8-hour shift one charge covers, how many minutes of charging buy that back, what the electricity costs per robot-year, and how many years of rated operation the pack holds at one, two and three shifts a day.

**Authorised arithmetic (derived figures the article may state, each computed from the rows below; nothing else is authorised):**

- A 1,350 kg-class AMR runs **6 h 45 m (405 minutes)** at maximum payload against an **8-hour (480-minute) shift**, so **75 minutes — 15.6% of the shift** — is the gap a single charge leaves.
- At the same page's **1:12 charging ratio** (10 minutes of charging returns 2 hours of runtime), the **75 minutes of runtime costs 6.25 minutes on a charger** (75 ÷ 12). Say "about six minutes"; do not round it to five or ten.
- **0.75 kWh** of energy at **7.2 kW** for 6.25 minutes (6.25 ÷ 60 × 7.2), which at **13.41¢ per kWh** is **about 10 cents per robot-day** and **about 26 dollars per robot-year** over 260 working days. Electricity is a rounding error against machines; write the comparison as a contrast with the machines, never with a vendor price this brief does not carry.
- Covering the 75-minute gap with machines instead of chargers means **about 16 extra robots per 100** (75 ÷ 480 = 15.6%). **This is not Zou's 15%** — that figure is a queueing-model result about fleet size and system cost, and the two land near each other by coincidence; never add, average or merge them.
- Rated life: **3,000 full cycles × 405 minutes ≈ 20,250 hours** of operation. At 2,080 hours a year (one shift) that is **about 9.7 years**, at 4,160 hours (two shifts) **about 4.9 years**, at 6,240 (three shifts) **about 3.3 years** — so a 7-year book life spans the pack on one shift and outlives it on two. State the conversion the arithmetic rests on: one full cycle is treated as one pack's worth of runtime at maximum payload.

**A trap both vendor pages set, to be written into the article as the reason a case gets mis-sized:** each page's *summary* block quotes the empty robot's run time — the MiR1350 page's summary reads "Active operation time: Up to 10 h" while its own detail block gives 6 h 45 m with maximum payload, and the MiR250 page's summary reads "Up to 17 h 30 min" while its detail block gives 13 h with maximum payload. The headline figure is the unloaded one. Also worth one sentence, because it sets the ladder: working payload halves the working day (1,350 kg for 6 h 45 m against 250 kg for 13 h), so the shift gap is a function of what the robot lifts, not of the robot brand.
