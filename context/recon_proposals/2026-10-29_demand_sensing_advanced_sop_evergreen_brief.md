## Notes on the evidence
- **Hosts are spread across five independent publishers** — `arxiv.org`, `frepple.com`, `openforecast.org`, `demandforecast.ai`, `toolsgroup.com` — so the floor is met with margin (5 sources, 5 hosts; the floor is 3 sources on 2 hosts). Row 1 is the only *measured* leg and it is a big one: 28,903 real series, its own cell counts, and its own finding that method rankings barely move between cells. Row 2 supplies the second catalogue's cell split, the vendor's own "unforecastable" verdict on the lumpy cell, and its own conclusion that such a company should manage safety stock and service levels "rather than aiming at improving the forecast accuracy". Row 3 is the critique that keeps the article honest — the grid's boundaries were tuned for a Croston-versus-SBA comparison and assume stable ADI/CV. Rows 4 and 5 carry the mechanism (a rate forecast and a fitted count distribution for intermittent series; the service-level curve as the lever for the rest), both labelled vendor rather than measured.
- **The arithmetic the article turns on must be computed in the piece, not asserted:** 1,994 ÷ 28,903 = 6.9% of the M5 series in the smooth cell, and 28,903 − 1,994 = 26,909 (93.1%) outside it; on row 2's catalogue the smooth cell is 23% and the lumpy cell 36%. The two samples disagree because they are different populations — weekly grocery-store SKUs versus a manufacturer's parts catalogue — and the article must say so rather than blending them into one percentage. Do not write "half your SKUs", "most", or "always": write the measured share of the named sample.
- **The counter-intuitive finding is the spine and must not be smoothed away:** row 1 finds that the *ranking of forecasting methods* is nearly the same in all four cells. That means the grid does not earn its keep as a method-selection device (which is what it was built for, row 3), and the defensible use is a stop/continue decision on accuracy targets — supported by the same paper's finding that costs are higher in the lumpy and intermittent groups. Any sentence that has the classifier picking a winner method is the lazy thesis and is contradicted by row 1.
- **The one rule with numbers the article must land:** compute ADI as periods ÷ non-zero periods and CV² over the non-zero sale sizes; at ADI ≥ 1.32 with CV² ≥ 0.49 the series sits in the lumpy cell — stop scoring mean absolute percentage error on it (row 2: accuracy is not a reliable metric outside the smooth profile; row 4: an incumbent weighted MAPE of 146% on a low-volume portfolio), stop giving planners override time on it, and cover it with stock instead: a count distribution fitted to the forecast rate, a reorder point set from that, and the service-level curve priced item by item (rows 4 and 5, and row 2's own conclusion that such a company should focus on safety stock and service levels). At ADI ≥ 1.32 with CV² below 0.49 the series is intermittent, where a two-parameter rate forecast of the Croston family is legitimate (row 4's 1.40 versus an actual 1.17, with the SBA trim of alpha ÷ 2), and the metric to report is an absolute-error measure, never a percentage of zero.
- **Not to be claimed:** that a better demand-sensing engine is worthless (it is not — it belongs to the cells that can be scored), that the 1.32/0.49 boundaries are universal law (row 3 says they were tuned for one method comparison and assume stability), or that any of the vendor scenarios is a measured result. The claim is about *which series* get an accuracy target and which get a policy, which is why the decision line names the planning year and the licence renewal.
- **Rows 4 and 5 carry no performance claim.** Row 4 is a vendor's own portfolio figure and its worked arithmetic; row 5 is an undated vendor explainer's statement about a service-level curve. Both are cited for the mechanism and quoted with that provenance, and nothing in the article's argument depends on their magnitude.
