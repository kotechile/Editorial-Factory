**Signal B (corroboration):** The Next Platform's interview the same day carries the measured multiples: as much as 18 times the memory bandwidth of LPDDR, and five times the terabit-per-second-per-watt of HBM4, on first-generation lab silicon (https://www.nextplatform.com/ai/2026/11/02/qualcomm-has-eyes-on-the-ai-inference-datacenter-with-hbc-and-ai250/5306954)
**Emergent Collision Point:** Both companies accept the same premise — inference is limited by moving model data, not by arithmetic — and then build opposite machines. Microsoft keeps HBM and widens it, buying bandwidth with 950 watts and an interposer; Qualcomm removes HBM, puts the compute die under a stack of low-power memory, and buys bandwidth by shortening the distance data travels. The collision is not "who is faster". It is that the two headline numbers measure different things against different bases: 18.0 TB/s is an absolute HBM figure on a shipping rack design, while 18x is a ratio against low-power memory that the HBM part was never competing with. The one unit that could settle a purchase — tokens per megawatt — is published by Microsoft against an unnamed "leading GPU-based system" and is not published by Qualcomm at all. An engineer comparing the two spec sheets is comparing a ratio to a rate.
**Hook:** Two memory numbers landed three weeks apart this autumn: 18.0 terabytes a second in one rack, and 18 times the bandwidth of low-power memory in the other. Put them side by side and they cancel: one is an absolute rate, the other a ratio against a base the first machine does not use.
**Tension:** Inference economics is now a memory-movement problem, which means the buyer's decision has moved from "how many FLOPS" to "how many tokens per watt per dollar" — and the market has no shared instrument for that yet. Microsoft's answer is captive: Maia 300 is Microsoft's own silicon, aimed at Microsoft's own services, in production in 2027 and not sold as a part. Qualcomm's answer is merchant, but the silicon validated this month is lab silicon; the product samples in the first half of 2027, and its efficiency claim is a vendor estimate normalised to target operating conditions. So the efficiency gains are real and arriving, and neither is a number a buyer outside Azure can act on today. Nvidia, the incumbent both are attacking, has taken a third route — moving the memory controller off the GPU into a custom base die that is not a JEDEC standard — which means the reference design the rest of the market prices against is also diverging.
