**Emergent Collision Point:** In the same two-week window the two largest U.S. fulfillment spenders made opposite capex bets. Amazon put capital into the *machine* — scaling its own robot manufacturing so the per-unit cost of automation keeps falling (1M+ robots already built, 75% of orders touched). Walmart put capital into the *building* — a $300M box dedicated to oversized, non-sortable goods that no AMR grid or AS/RS tote handles. Neither company's release states the implication of the other: automation payback is no longer a single number to spread across a network. It now splits by SKU geometry — robots amortize on uniform, high-velocity goods, while big-and-bulky still amortizes as real estate plus labor. The wage-inflation case that sold the last wave of automation does not decide this split; SKU shape does.
**Hook:** Amazon just committed more than $100 million to build the machines, and it has already made more than a million robots. Walmart, in the same fortnight, committed more than $300 million to a building for the TVs and sofas those machines still can't pick — the two announcements are the clearest evidence yet that "will automation pay back?" is now the wrong question.
**Tension:** The capital-cost backdrop sharpens it: the Fed raised rates 25 bps on Sept. 16 (its first hike since 2023), so every long-payback fixed system is priced harder — which rewards the shortest payback and punishes a blended, network-wide ROI model. The winners are operators who segment the amortization by SKU class instead of underwriting one automation number for the whole footprint. It hurts anyone midway through a uniform "automate the DC" business case; it helps the RaaS/AMR-flexible play and the operators honest about which aisles robots will never touch.
**Target reader:** `ops_leader` — VP of Supply Chain & Operations, highly numerate, ROI-first, balance-sheet aware, grounded in physical physics.
**Single claim to defend:** As Amazon drives the robot's unit cost down through in-house manufacturing and Walmart commits $300M to real estate for goods robots can't handle, warehouse-automation payback has split along SKU geometry — the defensible 2026 capex model segments payback by SKU class rather than underwriting one blended automation ROI across the network.
