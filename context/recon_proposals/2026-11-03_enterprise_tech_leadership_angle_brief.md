**Signal A (Anchor 1):** Dun & Bradstreet, *AI Momentum* survey, Q4 2026 — released 2026-10-28, 10,000 businesses across 32 countries. "Sixty-five percent of businesses plan to increase spending on AI over the next twelve months, yet only 28% report broad or strong returns from AI efforts, unchanged from last quarter." "Only 8% of businesses report that their enterprise data is fully ready to support AI at scale." "81% of businesses with enterprise data that is fully ready for AI are obtaining broad or strong ROI, compared with 12% of those whose data is only partially ready." On strong ROI alone, "55% … compared with 2% of those with low or no data readiness." "57% of those with partially ready data reporting only pockets of ROI, compared with 16% of those fully ready." "just 9% say most pilots have scaled with clear gains." 37% "scaling AI pilots into production, up from 34% in Q3." (https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/dun--bradstreet-survey-of-10-000-businesses-finds-ai-returns-stable-for-most-but-data-ready-companies-pull-ahead-on-roi-302922158.html)
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Deloitte, *The State of AI in the Enterprise* 2026 — released 2026-10-29, 3,500 director-to-C-suite leaders across 25 countries and six industries, fielded May–June 2026. "Return on investment (ROI) is expanding beyond productivity, with reported revenue gains from AI increasing to 28% (up from 20% reported in Deloitte's January report), demonstrating that financial value is solidifying for enterprises." "81% of companies surveyed reporting faster decision-making and execution"; "only 25% say they are using AI to deeply transform the business." "While 71% track total AI inference and API spend, only 51% track token volume and 44% track per-unit inference cost." 40% "now allow AI systems to execute decisions or take actions without human approval, at least to a moderate degree"; 23% "say accountability remains unclear when an AI system produces a negative outcome." (https://www.deloitte.com/us/en/about/press-room/enterprise-ai-enters-an-roi-reckoning.html)
**Emergent Collision Point:** Both releases report **the same number — 28%** — inside 24 hours and draw opposite verbs from it: Deloitte says value is *solidifying* (revenue gains up from 20% in January), Dun & Bradstreet says returns are *flat* (unchanged from last quarter). Neither release is wrong, and neither release says why they disagree: one counts a benefit that appeared somewhere in the business, the other counts a return that is broad and strong across it. Underneath the disagreement sits the one variable in either data set that is not a definition — data readiness, where the return rate splits 81% to 12% and strong-ROI splits 55% to 2%. Deloitte's own instrumentation numbers explain why that split stays invisible to leadership: 71% of organisations meter total AI spend, 44% meter cost per unit of inference, and almost nobody meters the denominator that ties a workflow's cost to what the workflow produced. So the ROI debate is being conducted entirely in numerators.
**Hook:** Two surveys of enterprise AI, published a day apart, both found 28% — one called it a recovery, the other called it a stall, and the only variable either data set measures that predicts the difference is whether the company's data was ready before the model arrived.
**Tension:** The 28% is the number that reaches the board, and it is definitionally soft: it can be produced by counting any benefit anywhere (Deloitte's revenue gain) or withheld by demanding breadth and strength (D&B's bar). Engineering leaders are asked to defend a percentage they do not control the definition of, while the two instruments that would settle the argument — a cost per completed unit of work, and a readiness gate on the data estate — are held by 44% and 8% of organisations respectively. Who this helps: teams that can answer "cost per invoice reconciled, and is the data behind it verified" before the next budget cycle. Who this hurts: anyone who reports an AI win as a share of respondents and cannot name the denominator. It also sets up the harder second question the surveys both raise and neither answers: 40% of enterprises now let AI execute decisions without human approval while 23% say accountability is unclear when it goes wrong — a percentage that rises fastest where nobody can price the unit.
**Target reader:** eng_leader
**Single claim to defend:** The reported AI ROI percentage is a survey artefact of a definition, not a banked number — so the defensible engineering position is to stop arguing about the percentage and instrument two things the data says actually move it: a cost per completed unit of work, and a verified-data gate before a workload scales, because the measured return split (81% against 12%) lives in the second one and only 8% of enterprises qualify.
