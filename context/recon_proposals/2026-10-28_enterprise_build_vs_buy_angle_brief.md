**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Deno's announcement that its team is joining Cloudflare (published 2026-10-09) — "We will support the Deno runtime for another year with monthly releases containing bug fixes and security updates. After that year we will end our development of the Deno runtime"; "Deno Deploy will continue operating for six months before shutting down", with migration support for paying customers moving to Cloudflare Workers; JSR keeps operating with its infrastructure moving to Cloudflare (https://deno.com/blog/cloudflare). Corroborated on the two deadlines by The New Stack (https://thenewstack.io/cloudflare-acquires-deno-ryan-dahl).

**Emergent Collision Point:** Both events are about a layer, not a product, and neither announcement states the joint claim. Cloudflare's own account of the deal is that the runtime below its margin is a commodity it will now give away — workerd is open source, celld is being merged into it so self-hosting becomes "a first-class supported way", and Kenton Varda writes plainly that "being open source and giving people an escape hatch is good business" (https://blog.cloudflare.com/deno-joins-cloudflare). So the buy side stops selling the runtime and sells the platform above it, and the runtime a customer bought acquires a 12-month support window and a 6-month hosting window on the way out. Anthropic's post is the same economic fact read from the buyer's side: no vendor sold the shape its source-control layer now needs, so it rebuilt the layer — and the rebuild pulled review, code search, an access-control system for source control and CI in with it, because a layer is not one tool. The emergent thesis is a way to read a build-vs-buy spreadsheet that neither source offers: score the decision per layer, and ask whether the seller's margin sits above or below the layer you are buying. Below it, expect the product to be commoditized and its exit to be cheap but its future short; above it, expect a stable vendor and a layer that will eventually stop fitting.

**Hook:** Open on the two numbers running in opposite directions in the same fortnight — a runtime a customer bought with a six-month shutdown notice, and a source-control layer a buyer rebuilt in five months — because the first says the exit is short and the second says the replacement is not.
**Tension:** Who this empowers: the engineering leader who can name the layer a purchase sits in before the renewal, because that names both the exit and the successor. Who it threatens: the "buy removes maintenance" orthodoxy of the last two decades, and the easy reading of Anthropic's post as a licence to build — the post itself says Scion "is by no means a suitable replacement for git for general use" and that it would be "an inefficient use of engineering resources for every company using git to face similar challenges alone". The systemic friction: in both events the constraint moved from price to production capacity. A vendor can discontinue what you bought because it can produce a successor layer; a buyer can only leave if it can produce the replacement, and Anthropic needed seven people and five months while claiming an unusual freedom to spend tokens on internal work.
**Target reader:** eng_leader (engineering / technology leader — concrete numbers, decision frameworks, what to do Monday morning; skeptical of hype, ships for a living).
**Single claim to defend:** In October 2026 the build-vs-buy line for developer tooling was drawn by where the vendor's margin sits, not by the licence price: Cloudflare is retiring and commoditizing the runtime layer beneath its platform margin, while Anthropic rebuilt the source-control layer because no vendor sold a shape that 30,000 merges a day required — so the decision an engineering leader faces is which layer the purchase sits in, and whether they can produce the replacement at all.
