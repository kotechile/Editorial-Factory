**Emergent Collision Point:** A falling lien count is the one public gauge a homeowner reads as a green light — fewer liens implies the trades are getting paid. It is a completion gauge, not a solvency gauge: NCS's own reading guide says a low score "may reflect improved payment conditions, fewer projects reaching lien-triggering stages, or both," and a lien can only be filed at the end of a job. The same quarter that remodeling spending fell 13.0 percent, the lien index hit a four-year low and small-business reorganizations rose 46 percent. The market is not paying its subs better; it is stopping jobs earlier, so fewer of them reach the point where a lien gets recorded. The risk that actually lands on a homeowner's title is a firm running out of money mid-job, and the indicator built to measure that has been falling.
**Hook:** The lien index just matched its four-year low in the same quarter remodeling spending fell 13.0 percent, and the number that predicts a contractor disappearing is not the lien count at all.
**Tension:** Contract protection in a boom is a price argument. In a contraction it is a cash-flow argument, and the homeowner is the last party to learn which contractor is financing the job out of the next deposit. The trade's own leading indicators agree the contraction is not a one-month blip — JCHS projects year-over-year growth of just 1.5 percent by the third quarter of 2027 — while the enforcement apparatus is a rounding error beside it: California's 39 stings and sweeps produced 73 actions in the same week.
