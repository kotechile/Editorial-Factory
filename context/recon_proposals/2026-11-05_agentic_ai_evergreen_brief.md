**Decision the reader is facing:** Where the state of an agent-to-agent hop should live — nowhere in an ordinary tool call, in a handle the model carries back on the next call, or in a task object the protocol owns — and which of the three costs you are buying when two agents have to work on the same thing across more than one exchange.

**Thesis:** An agent-to-agent hop keeps its state in exactly one of three places, and the protocol is the invoice: a plain tool call keeps none, a handle keeps it in your store with your expiry and your per-call authorization, and an A2A task keeps it in the protocol — for a measured price of about 51% more code, 10 coordination abstractions against 4, 6 coordination stages per interaction against 2, and a formalized 37-state machine carrying 11 vulnerabilities a fully compliant adversary can exploit. The reason teams used to reach for the task has partly gone: MCP's 2026-07-28 revision moves the clarification round-trip inside a tool call without a shared store, so a task is now worth its price only when the work must outlive the call — pause, resume, stream artifacts, be subscribed to.

## Rulings and notes
- **What each measurement is, and is not.** The York comparison is one team's build of one software-engineering task, counted with cloc: 1,255 lines for the MCP version, 1,898 for the A2A version, two of the four agents LLM-based, validated on 30 prompts (10 simple, 20 complex). Lines of code are a proxy for coordination surface, never a runtime benchmark, and the article must say so in the same paragraph as the number. A2ABreak is a formal analysis of the specification — 929 formalized statements into 37 states and 76 transitions, then 11 vulnerabilities under a full-compliance assumption — so it bounds what the protocol permits, not what any shipped server does.
- **The dating trap this topic carries.** The York paper is dated 26 Jul 2026 and the MCP revision it evaluates against arrives two days later, on 2026-07-28: that revision introduces multi round-trip requests (`input_required`, with `inputResponses` and an optional `requestState`) and states plainly that the protocol has no protocol-level session. So the paper's finding that MCP does not meet the multi-turn requirement is a pre-revision reading, and the article must say the round-trip now exists inside a tool call. It narrows the gap; it does not close it, because the pattern carries no task object, no subscription, no artifact stream and no resumption — which is exactly the residual the thesis claims.
- **"24 hours" is an illustrative example, not a standard.** The tools page's sentence is about a shopping basket's stated retention, offered as the shape of the guidance ("the server's retention policy should be stated in the creation tool's description"). The article may not present 24 hours as a recommended lifetime.
- **The handle is a name, not a capability.** That is the specification's own point ("a handle is a name, not a capability. The server should validate the caller's authorization against the handle on every call"), and it is the one operational rule that survives the article: an opaque handle with a bounded lifetime, authorized on every use, is the middle option's whole discipline.
- **Matcher calibration, probed before writing.** On the York page a cell leading on `1898` lands on the §5.1 sentence that carries 1,255 and 51%; a cell leading on `51%` lands on the same paragraph. The coordination-abstraction and stage counts do **not** survive as their own rows — a cell leading on `10` evidences on the pseudocode's `10 if semanticFeedback…` and one leading on `6` evidences on the table of contents — so those two counts ride inside row 1's gloss, which is the page's own sentence for the same claim. On the tools page `24 hours` lands on the retention sentence. On A2ABreak `37 states` and `11 vulnerabilities` both land on the abstract's own sentences.
- **No hands-on claim.** The desk ran no protocol implementation and no attack; it read two papers and one specification.
