**Angle Type:** Single-Signal
**Winner:** "Your IPO Payday: A 47.7% Tax Bill"
**Scores:** N=8.5 A=9.5 S=8.5 → Composite=**8.8**

**Hook:** Oura told the SEC, in writing, that of the roughly $532.6 million its IPO would raise for the company, about $526.4 million — 98.8% — was earmarked to cover the tax withheld when its employees' restricted stock units settled. Then, on September 29, hours before the shares were to price, the company pulled the deal.

**Tension:** A double-trigger RSU only vests when the offering happens, and the tax on that vest is remitted by withholding shares at a rate the company estimates in advance. So the employee's first liquidity and their tax bill are the same event: they cannot receive one without the other, they do not choose the rate, and they cannot sell into it until the lock-up window opens. Oura modelled its own 13,338,451 delivered shares against 12,150,103 withheld at an assumed 47.7% blended rate and booked about $160.0 million of stock-based compensation expense on the settlement — then the event itself became optional, and the shares and the bill both evaporated. This is not one company's accident: Oura was one of a run of 2026 listings pulled days before pricing (Holtec on September 16, Firmus on October 9, Clear Street earlier in the year), and the survey of private companies queued behind them says only 26% feel confident about their own liquidity readiness while companies themselves estimate 37% of employees do not understand the tax implications of the event they are waiting for. The technical trap the filing exposes: 25% of Oura's time-vested RSUs were automatically forfeited for no consideration on a without-cause termination, and that clause only stopped applying to outstanding awards once the company went public — a term that moves at the event, not at the grant.

**Target reader:** equity_career_strategist (senior IC to VP-level tech professional)

**Single claim to defend:** At a liquidity event the number that decides what an employee actually keeps is the settlement and withholding mechanic the company chose — how many shares are withheld, at what assumed rate, and on whose schedule — and not the valuation the offer letter quoted; the event itself is contingent, so the mechanic is the only part an employee can plan against.
