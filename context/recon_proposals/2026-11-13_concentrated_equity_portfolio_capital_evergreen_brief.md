**Thesis:** A collar is not a way to de-risk without selling — the put is legally a short sale, so a position held under a year has its long-term clock restarted when the put closes; the pair is a straddle, so the put's loss is deferred against the stock's own unrealised gain instead of offsetting the sale you eventually make; and the dividends stop qualifying for the whole window the downside is covered. The only structure that escapes the holding-period rule is a naked qualified covered call, which sells your upside without buying any protection.

Supporting citations carried in prose and in `## Sources` rather than as gate rows, because their operative sentences contain no figure a matcher can lead on (each quoted verbatim in `2026-11-13_concentrated_equity_portfolio_capital_evergreen_verified_brief.md`):
- 26 U.S.C. § 1092(a)(1) — a loss is allowed only to the extent it exceeds the unrecognised gain in offsetting positions, i.e. the put's loss is a paper loss until the stock is sold.
- IRS Publication 550 (2025), Straddles — "unrecognised gain … the amount of gain you would've had on an open position if you had sold it on the last business day of the tax year at its fair market value", and the worked example: a $15,000 loss closed while $12,750 of unrecognised gain sits in the offsetting position leaves $2,250 deductible and carries $12,750 forward.
- IRS Publication 550 (2025), Puts and calls — "Buying a put option is generally treated as a short sale … If you have held the underlying stock for 1 year or less at the time you buy the put … your holding period for the underlying stock begins on the earliest of: the date you dispose of the stock, the date you exercise the put, the date you sell the put, or the date the put expires."
- 26 U.S.C. § 1233(b) — the statutory rule the regulation above serves: where substantially identical property has been held not more than 1 year, the holding period "shall be considered to begin … on the date of the closing of the short sale".
- 26 U.S.C. § 246(c)(4)(A) — the holding period is "appropriately reduced … for any period … in which the taxpayer has an option to sell, is under a contractual obligation to sell, or has made (and not closed) a short sale of, substantially identical stock or securities", with the qualified covered call carved out.
- 26 U.S.C. § 263(g)(1)–(2) — no deduction for interest and carrying charges allocable to personal property that is part of a straddle, including "all other amounts (including charges to insure, store, or transport the personal property)", capitalised into basis instead; the article's claim is confined to that text (a margin-financed collar's interest is capitalised) and does not assert that the put premium itself is a carrying charge — the premium is the option's basis.
- IRS Publication 550 (2025), qualified covered call option — for options entered into after July 28, 2002 the call must be granted "not more than 12 months before its expiration date", so a two-year collar call fails the qualified-covered-call test on term alone.
