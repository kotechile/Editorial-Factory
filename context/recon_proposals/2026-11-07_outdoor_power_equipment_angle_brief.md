**Emergent Collision Point:** Neither document says anything about the other, and the pair produces a
thesis neither can state alone: in the same four weeks, the largest American mower maker began handing
its gas walk-behind line to licensees — deliberately separating the badge from the factory — while the
regulator demonstrated what that separation costs at the other end of the market, where the name on the
machine belongs to a party that will not answer. Signal A is the separation designed on purpose;
Signal B is the separation with no one behind it. The load-bearing consequence is that the buyer's
recourse — a free repair kit, a refund, or a cord in the bin — was decided by who imported the machine
and not by who designed it, which is a specification no product page prints.

**Hook:** DR Power's owners got a free repair kit and, if they wanted, a free installation at a dealer,
for 42,860 trimmer mowers that had caught fire 19 times. PowRyte's owners got an instruction to cut the
cord and throw the machine away, with no refund attached.

**Tension:** The market is consolidating onto a handful of battery platforms and a handful of
manufacturing owners, and the badge is the asset that survives the factory. That is good for the
licensor's margin and neutral-to-bad for the person holding a five-year-old machine whose parts now
come from a company they have never heard of. It also splits the safety system: the walk-behind standard,
the GFCI requirement and the recall are enforced against whoever is reachable, so the same defect
produces a repair kit in Wisconsin and a bin bag in Jiangsu.

**Target reader:** homeowner_pro

**Single claim to defend:** What a homeowner actually buys is not the brand but the party obliged to
answer for the machine — and this month three outdoor machines showed three different answers, decided
by who imported them.
