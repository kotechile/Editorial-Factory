## Notes on the evidence
- Hosts are deliberately spread across five independent publishers — `ideas.repec.org`, `research.manchester.ac.uk`, `demand-planning.com`, `lokad.com`, `doss.com` — so the floor is met with margin (5 sources, 5 hosts; the floor is 3 sources on 2 hosts). Rows 1–2 are the measured academic leg (what happens when practitioners adjust model forecasts), row 3 is the measured benchmark leg (how often the whole process loses to a random walk), row 5 is the measured practice leg (how few teams score accuracy at all, from a dated 661-respondent survey), and row 4 is the critique leg (a supplier's own argument that value added must be tied to money, which is why the article ends on the working-capital tether rather than on accuracy for its own sake).
- **Corroboration, not a gate row:** Infomineo's "Demand Sensing vs Demand Forecasting: Run Both Well" (2026-09-21) applies the same test to a sensing layer — every layer, sensing included, "should have to prove it beats the layer beneath it" — and carries the horizon distinction the article's first rule needs (forecasting is scored at the lag the plan was frozen; sensing is scored at the nearest one to four weeks). Its only numeric token is a repeat of the Morlidge 52% figure, so it cannot carry an independent row; cite it as corroboration, never as the anchor for a figure.
- The claim to defend is deliberately narrower than the two published pieces: this is the *evaluation method* for the forecasting process, not the buffer arithmetic (2026-10-08) and not the shock-immunity argument (2026-10-08 news). The article must NOT imply that a positive FVA removes demand risk — it prices the reducible, forecast-side error only.
- Rules with numbers the article must land: (a) FVA is computed per step and per SKU family as benchmark error minus step error, at the lag the decision is frozen — a step that cannot be scored at that lag has no verdict; (b) keep a step only while its value added is non-negative, and treat a negative read as a deletion candidate, not a training problem; (c) direction and size are the gate — the adjustments that most often damage accuracy are the small ones and the upward ones (rows 1–2), so an override policy that whitelists tidy-up edits is buying error; (d) any accuracy gain that does not convert to inventory dollars is not a business win (row 4), which is where the $8.5M-on-3-points field case belongs.
- Do not blend the studies into a single "most forecasts are bad" number: 52% of 300,000 forecasts (row 3) and "just over half of SKUs" (row 1) measure different things on different samples, and the 2009 result is company-level (three of four companies improved on average), not SKU-level.
