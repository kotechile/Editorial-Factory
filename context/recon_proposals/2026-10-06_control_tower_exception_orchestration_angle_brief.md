**Signal A (Anchor 1):** Tive, *Cargo Theft Prevention in the Age of AI* — published 2026-09-29; survey of 442 supply chain, transportation, operations, security and executive leaders. 45% of organizations using active in-transit monitoring recover more than half of stolen cargo, versus 30% relying on passive monitoring; real-time route-deviation alerts show the strongest recovery delta (53% vs 29%); 55% of respondents who could identify the primary method behind their latest theft cited identity-based fraud — traditional identity theft, fictitious pickups, and synthetic or AI-generated identities (https://www.tive.com/press-release/tive-research-45-of-companies-using-active-monitoring-recover-more-than-half-of-stolen-cargo-1-5x-the-rate-of-those-relying-on-passive-monitoring).
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** *Supply Chain Management Review*, "From forecast to action: How agentic AI is rewiring supply chain exception management" — by Souraj Roy and Samriddha Basu, published 2026-09-30; argues the insight-to-action handoff is the real bottleneck, that routine high-volume exceptions with clear rules and measurable cost of delay are candidates for bounded agent action, and that autonomy should vary by decision — resolve a small variance, seek approval for a consequential substitution, surface options for a major disruption (https://www.scmr.com/article/agentic-ai-supply-chain-exception-management).
**Emergent Collision Point:** Signal B describes the tiering rule the industry is adopting for agent authority — give autonomy to the exceptions that are high-volume, rule-clear and bounded in downside. Signal A shows that the exception class growing the losses (identity-based fraud at pickup) does not present as an anomaly at all: the load leaves under a verified-looking identity, so no route-deviation, dwell or dark-trailer alert ever fires. The tier that the industry is automating is therefore defined on exactly the axis (frequency and rule-clarity) where a control tower has no signal, while the exception that empties the trailer stays in the human queue — not because it is judged risky, but because nothing surfaced it. Neither source states this: Tive reports who recovers cargo; SCMR reports how to delegate. Fused, they say the autonomy map is keyed to the wrong dimension.
**Hook:** On Sept. 29 Tive published a survey of 442 supply chain leaders showing that in-transit monitoring is the difference between recovering a stolen load and writing it off — and, in the same survey, that 55% of the thefts those leaders could actually identify started with a fake identity at the pickup dock. One week earlier, an SCMR analysis laid out the case for letting AI agents clear routine exceptions on their own. Put the two together and the trap is obvious: the load that a fraudster drives away looks, to the control tower, exactly like a clean pickup.
**Tension:** Visibility platforms are sold on catching what goes wrong in transit. Fraud-driven theft is winning precisely because it never goes wrong in transit — it goes right until the trailer is empty. Meanwhile the industry is deciding how much authority to hand an agent over the exception queue, and the tiering logic being written (volume, rule clarity, bounded downside) routes autonomy to the physical anomalies the tower already sees and keeps human judgment for everything else — including the silent one. Sits hardest on shippers and 3PL control-tower owners who bought visibility to prevent loss, and on the platform vendors whose recovery ROI story is strongest on the exception class that is shrinking.
**Target reader:** supply_chain_architect
