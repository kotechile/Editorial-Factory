## Derived arithmetic the drafter MAY use (labelled as arithmetic, never as a source's figure)

- **Slack inside the window:** 14 − 11 = **3 hours**. The first three on-duty hours are the only margin between the wall and the driving cap; every dock hour past that cuts drivable hours one-for-one. State the two figures it comes from.
- **The premium hour:** $28.19 × 1.5 = **$42.29**; $19.35 × 1.5 = **$29.03**.
- **A four-hour recovery:** 4 × $29.03 = **$116.10** per hand on the dock; a six-hand crew = **$696.60**. The same four hours on a driver = 4 × $42.29 = **$169.14** (and only where the premium reaches that driver at all — row 7).
- **The window's edges:** a 06:00 start closes the 14-hour window at **20:00**; the earliest legal restart is **06:00** the next day (10 consecutive hours off duty).
- **When the premium starts:** only past **40 hours inside the workweek the employer fixed** (168 hours, row 6) — so a Saturday recovery is premium time only if the crew already passed 40 hours in that period.
- Do NOT invent: an average cost of a late shipment, a demurrage or detention rate, an air-freight premium, a recovery rate, a crew size for the reader's own operation, or a dwelling figure.

## Discrepancies to record (the table is the claim of record)

- **The rule limits *driving*, not on-duty time.** A driver may stay on duty — waiting at a gate, loading — past the 14th hour and may not *drive*. Never write "the driver goes off the clock at hour 14" or "the shift ends at 14 hours"; write that the driving has to be finished inside it.
- **The 11 hours are a total inside the 14-hour period** (§ 395.3(a)(3)(i)), which is the whole reason dock waiting is not free: the wait is on-duty time and the drivable total does not grow.
- **The short-haul paragraph is a real exception, not the rule** (row 4): it exempts the 150 air-mile driver from the log requirements and lets that driver run past the 14th hour on up to five days of seven. Do not generalise the drayage case to a line-haul recovery.
- **The same regulation family cuts both ways:** 49 CFR 395 gives the short-haul driver relief, while 29 CFR Part 782 can take the driver out of the overtime requirement altogether (row 7). The article must not claim the driver's recovery hour costs 1.5× as a fact — for the dock crew it does.
- **Rows 8 and 9 are national medians for the occupation, not a crew's wages** (O*NET OnLine, 2025 vintage). Say "the 2025 median" every time, and never present them as the reader's own rate card.
