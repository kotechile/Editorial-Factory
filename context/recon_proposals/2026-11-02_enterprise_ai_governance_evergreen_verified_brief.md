## Notes for the drafter

- **The load-bearing distinction is rotation versus revocation.** A rotation programme creates a second credential; the exposure only shrinks when the first one is destroyed. Google's own procedure says so ("delete the replaced key"), and its sentence about not using expiring keys explains why teams stop short — an expiry they cannot automate is an outage, so the old key stays.
- **The Kubernetes facts are the mechanism, not the metaphor.** A bound token cannot be revoked individually: the pod goes, the token goes. Legacy Secret-based tokens are invalidated after a year of *disuse*, so a token in daily use never ages out at all.
- **The number to put in policy comes from three directions at once:** the platforms default to about an hour (bound token 1 hour; assumed role 1 hour, with 12 hours as the ceiling and 15 minutes the floor; short-lived Google tokens 1 hour, 12 hours only by policy), the vendor's fallback recommendation is 90 days for a static key, and the federal ladder for a *person's* session is 24 hours at AAL2.
- **Do not claim NIST governs agents.** Say plainly that the guideline covers people, and that it is the ladder a reviewer already has in hand.
- **The honest counter-argument** (for **The catch**): a short lifetime is not free — workloads that cannot mint credentials, or vendors who only accept a static key, turn a one-hour policy into an outage, which is exactly the failure the vendor's anti-expiry sentence describes. The answer in the sources is not a longer key but a way to stop issuing one (workload identity, short-lived tokens).
