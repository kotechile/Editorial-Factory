**Emergent Collision Point:** The two legs answer the same purchase question from opposite ends and neither says what the pair says. The launch fixes a memory number into a product price — 128GB is a rung, and the rungs below 48GB also lose 1,024 GPU cores and two CPU cores — while the index prices that same capacity as a commodity that moved 13.3% in thirty days and $294 on a 128GB kit in one month. Neither document states the consequence: the ladder's "affordable" rung and its "capable" rung are no longer the same machine, and the capable rung is priced on an input that has risen in ten of the last twelve months, so a buyer sizing by gigabytes is timing a commodity, not choosing a product.
**Hook:** 128 gigabytes of desktop DDR5 costs $2,319 at today's median, up $294 in thirty days — and the cheapest laptop NVIDIA launched this month that holds that much memory costs $5,109.
**Tension:** Who this empowers — a buyer who prices the memory line separately and buys the smallest rung that keeps the full chip (48GB), then adds capacity later or rents the peak. Who this threatens — the buyer who reads "128GB" as the spec that makes the laptop future-proof, and the OEM rungs that sell memory at a markup against an index rising monthly. The 24GB entry rung is the tell: it is the same capacity as the model NVIDIA ships with the machine, on a device where the operating system and the agent runtime also live.
