## What the article must carry

- **The mechanism, stated in slot terms a reader can follow.** A three-lamp tester works on voltage between slots: one lamp for neutral-to-ground, one for hot-to-neutral, one for hot-to-ground (row 8 article). A bootleg ground runs a jumper from the grounding screw to the neutral screw, so the hot slot puts voltage on both the neutral and ground slots, the tester sees the pattern it calls correct wiring, and it reports a pass (row 8). That is the whole article in three sentences, and it is why a better lamp pattern cannot fix it.
- **What the wrong answer costs, priced in volts, not adjectives.** On a reversed-polarity bootleg ground "the metal yoke of the receptacle is energized at 120 volts" and so is the metal frame of anything plugged in, while the tester shows correct wiring (row 8). Quote the 120 volts and name the class of source: a licensed electrician and code-compliance inspector writing for home inspectors.
- **The ladder, with the two dollar steps computed.** $16.53 to $36.46 is $19.93, and $36.46 to $159.99 is $123.53; the top rung is 9.68 times the bottom one. What the $19.93 buys is a voltage readout and a GFCI trip timer (row 6) — and the RT250's own table says "AFCI: No" and lists no false-ground condition (rows 5, 6). What the $123.53 buys is the load test: 12A, 15A and 20A voltage-drop measurement, AFCI and GFCI inspection, and a dual open-neutral/open-ground fault call (row 7). Say plainly that Klein's feature list for the RT390 does not claim to identify a false ground (row 7) — a negative fact established by reading the whole specification, and the reason the top rung is not the answer to the bootleg question.
- **The ceiling, and why it is not the recommendation.** The one product class that advertises the bootleg call is IDEAL's SureTest: "Identifies false (bootleg) grounds", voltage drop under 12A/15A/20A loads, ground impedance 0.00 - 1.99 ohms, ground-neutral voltage 0.0 - 24.0 VAC, GFCI test current 6.0 - 9.0 mA (rows 10, 11, 12). It lists at $421.33 from a distributor (row 10) — and the manufacturer's own page for that SKU says discontinued (row 13), so the price buys an outgoing model. State both, and state that this comparison found no independent measured test of the false-ground call, so its hit rate is unpublished and is not claimed here.
- **The 12-amp overlap is the seasonal hook, and it is arithmetic the reader can check.** 12 amperes at 120 volts is 1,440 watts, which is the class of load a plug-in heater adds to the circuit; the analyzer's highest published load test is the same 12 amps the reader is about to draw (row 7, derived: 12 A × 120 V = 1,440 W). That is why the load test is worth having for a house about to be loaded — and why it still does not answer the ground question.
- **The free method, named, because it is the actual answer for one house.** Read the panel first: in an older home with apparently grounded receptacles, look for equipment grounding conductors, and if none or very few are present suspect bootlegging (row 8 article). Then take one receptacle apart and look for the jumper between the grounding screw and the neutral screw (row 8 figure 2 caption: "The jumper wire from the grounding screw to the neutral screw"). That is a screwdriver and ten minutes, and it is a stronger answer than any of the three testers.
