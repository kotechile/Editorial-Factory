**Emergent Collision Point:** The supply side has already priced 2027 without waiting for the substitution to be measured. Samsung reports the 2027 volume sold at an agreed target price, and the configuration the industry is moving to in order to spend less per GPU (8-Hi) is 10-20% dearer per gigabyte than the one it replaces. Signal B is the measurement that would tell a buyer whether to pay: flash can replace GPUs that exist only to hold model weights, and it does so mostly for paused sessions and short chats, hardly at all for long retrieval and never for tool sessions. Neither source states the collision. Samsung cannot say which of its customers are buying capacity they could have tiered; the paper cannot say what that capacity costs, because it prices the stack in GPU-equivalents, not contracts. Put together, they say the 2027 bill is being signed on a workload-average, while the evidence for the cheaper tier is workload-specific.
**Hook:** Samsung sold its entire 2027 HBM output at an agreed price last month. Two weeks later, a simulation of 62,279 inference runs found the one substitute that could cut a GPU fleet by half pays off in a narrow band of workloads — and the cheaper HBM configuration costs more per gigabyte.
**Tension:** The buyer's instinct in a shortage is to buy less per GPU and more GPUs later; both halves of that instinct fail here. Smaller stacks cost more per bit, and the cheap tier's savings are concentrated in exactly the traffic that is hardest to forecast — sessions that pause and chats that end early — while the traffic that most needs capacity (long retrieval, tool loops) gets nothing from it. Memory makers are meanwhile pushing the key-value cache down to enterprise SSDs, which is a third tier with its own price curve. Who this helps: operators who can measure their own session mix before they sign. Who it hurts: anyone holding a 2023-vintage assumption that next year's memory is cheaper.
