**Emergent Collision Point:** The market's story of the quarter is that the Red Sea return releases capacity and takes cost out of Asia–Europe and Asia–Mediterranean lanes. The invoice's story is the opposite direction: a fixed **$91 per TEU** that switched on 19 October, open-ended, on cargo moving down those same lanes. Neither document states the consequence — the base rate is what a 2027 bid negotiates downward as capacity returns, while the surcharge is a per-unit line that does not travel with it and does not expire with it. A shipper who budgets the lane on the direction of spot rates books a lower number and pays a higher one. Drewry's own routing arithmetic, reported by The Loadstar on 2026-10-12, makes the gap checkable: the canal saves about **$1m a round voyage, or $43 per slot**, on two 24,000 teu services — against MSC's **$91 per teu**, which the same report computes at **$2.18m** on a full 24,000 teu ship. The efficiency the return promises is larger on the analyst's spreadsheet than on the shipper's invoice, and the direction is reversed.

**Hook:** Suez transits are climbing back toward full restoration by Q1 2027, Asia–Europe spot rates are being pushed up 6% into the 2027 contract round, and the first per-TEU number that actually lands on an Asia–Mediterranean shipper's bill this quarter is a $91 surcharge that went live on 19 October and says "until further notice".

**Tension:** Whoever holds the per-unit line holds the margin. Carriers get a fixed, route-blind charge that survives the capacity release they themselves are creating; shippers get a negotiation about a base rate that is falling while the add-on that sits on top of it is not on the table. The systemic friction is that the two halves of a freight bill are priced on different clocks — the base rate annually, the surcharge indefinitely — and the return to Suez accelerates the first while leaving the second untouched.
