**Scores:** E=8.5 A=9.5 S=9.0 → Composite=9.0
**Signal A (Anchor 1):** Anthropic, PBC Form S-1, filed 2026-10-26 (https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/2130357/000162828026067168/anthropicpbc-sx1.htm). A "single share of Class F common stock" goes to the Founder LLC immediately before the offering and carries "50.1% of the total voting power" with no economic rights. The Long-Term Benefit Trust's Class T has "no voting rights with respect to matters submitted to stockholders generally and has no economic rights", and the Class T directors "will constitute a majority of the authorized members of our board of directors". The class created for employees, Class E, "will be entitled to vote only on certain limited matters and will not have any economic rights." Employees earn it slowly and lose it fast: "one share of Class E common stock for each six months of employment completed with us", one more "for each share of Class A common stock that such employee has pledged to donate", and after "an employee's separation from employment with us other than for cause, one-half of such employee's then-outstanding shares of Class E common stock will be automatically redeemed by us annually", with all of them redeemed on "death, incapacity, termination for cause, or commencement of service with a direct competitor" — and "Any redemption of Class E common stock will be made for no consideration." Class A holders "generally will not be able to affect the outcome of any matter submitted to a vote of our stockholders or the election of our directors, regardless of how they vote". The filing adds that the structure "is likely to have the effect of limiting the likelihood of an unsolicited merger proposal, unsolicited tender offer, or proxy contest for the removal of directors" and "may have the effect of depriving our stockholders of an opportunity to sell their shares at a premium over prevailing market prices". Employee RSUs vest only when a performance condition is satisfied by "the effectiveness of the registration statement"; as of 2026-06-30 "no stock-based compensation expense had been recognized for such RSUs", and $23.7 billion of related expense was unrecognized, over a weighted-average 2.1 years.
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Anduril's employee tender offer, reported 2026-10-14 by CNBC and confirmed to CNBC by the company (https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/14/anduril-tender-offer-valuation.html). Employees "can sell shares at $94.80 apiece"; the offer "opened on Oct. 13 and closes on Nov. 9" and is "capped at $1.3 billion"; it values the company at "roughly $90 billion", eligibility runs to "employees in 16 countries", and the company's May 2026 round had "doubled its valuation to $61 billion".
**Emergent Collision Point:** Neither document says what the pair says. A private company's employee shares have no market price at all — no tape, no quote, no bid — so the only number an employee can ever act on is one a board chooses to publish inside a dated, capped window. Anduril proves the mechanism exists and shows its price; Anthropic's filing shows, in the company's own words, who holds the switch and that the structure is built to stop the outside bid that would otherwise force a price. The collision turns an abstract governance clause into a line item: the value of $23.7 billion of unvested employee stock is set by five classes of shares, and the employee side of the table is split so that the shares with the economics can never decide anything and the share with a vote carries no economics, vests at half a share per year of service and is redeemed for no consideration the moment the employee leaves.
**Hook:** Anthropic's filing creates a share class for its 5,300 employees that has no economic rights and votes on two narrow questions, while one share held by the founders' LLC carries 50.1% of the vote and no economics at all — and in the same month, Anduril handed its staff a per-share number ($94.80) that only a board can produce.
**Tension:** Who this empowers: the employee who reads the class table before accepting an offer and negotiates the grant, the refresh and the tender history instead of the valuation. Who this threatens: the recruiter selling "equity at a $350 billion valuation" as though it were a price, and the pre-IPO company that would rather you compared headline numbers than voting structures.
