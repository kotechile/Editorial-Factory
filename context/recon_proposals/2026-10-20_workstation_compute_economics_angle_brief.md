**Signal A (Anchor 1):** NVIDIA, 2026-10-15 — Nemotron 3.5 Bolt 74B-A9B "24GB" is released with a working set that is not 24GB. NVIDIA's own research page says the architecture search "targets a deployment footprint below 24 GB at batch size 1 and 128K context", that all experts are quantized to IQ2_XXS at "2.0625 bits per element", and that the measured **83.99 TPS** was run "with llama.cpp on Windows 11 ARM x64, with the same RTX Spark hardware (Surface Laptop Ultra, 128GB)" (https://research.nvidia.com/labs/nemotron/Nemotron-3.5-Bolt-24GB/). The model card states the deployment envelope plainly: "llama.cpp on RTX/DGX Spark (64GB / 128GB) or RTX discrete GPUs (32GB+ / 48GB+); 28GB and 32GB recipes below", with a 74B total / 9B active Mamba-2–Transformer hybrid LatentMoE, IQ2 GGUF and 256K context (https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Bolt-74B-A9B-24GB-GGUF). NVIDIA's release notes ship it as one product with the hardware: "Bolt 74B-A9B are available now, with Bolt E4B coming Oct. 23 … Built for NVIDIA RTX Spark laptops arriving Oct. 16" (https://blogs.nvidia.com/blog/nemotron-3-5-super-vl).
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Tom's Hardware, 2026-10-16 — RTX Spark laptops went on general sale, and the day's price list is one memory ladder: Asus ProArt P14 with **24GB for $2,599** at the bottom; Surface Laptop Ultra **48GB for $3,999**; Asus ProArt P16 **64GB "starting at $4,499.99"** and **128GB "$6,999.99 with 2TB"**; HP's **128GB/1TB OmniBook X "comes in at $5,109"** and OmniBook Ultra at $5,309. Memory capacities are "24GB, 32GB, 48GB, 64GB, and 128GB", the full 6,144-core chip only from 48GB up, and unified memory tops out at 128GB of LPDDR5X at 45–80 W (https://www.tomshardware.com/laptops/nvidia-rtx-spark-devices-are-now-officially-on-sale-secure-the-surface-laptop-ultra-or-other-rtx-spark-laptops-here-starting-at-usd2-599-where-to-buy-pricing-and-availability-revealed-on-several-models). The same day's Surface ladder runs $2,599.99 (24GB) to $5,899.99 (128GB), with the full chip from $3,699.99, and carries the caveat that "total capacity is not equivalent to 128GB of dedicated, high-bandwidth VRAM" (https://mlq.ai/news/nvidias-rtx-spark-pcs-arrive-october-16-with-up-to-128gb-of-shared-memory).
**Emergent Collision Point:** Neither release says what the pair says. NVIDIA's software side spent the year compressing a 120B model down to a 74B one that runs at 2 bits, and the result is a **28–32GB deployment envelope on a 32GB+/48GB+ card** — the label says 24GB, the recipes do not. The hardware side, in the same 48 hours, shipped the memory in a different form: a 128GB pool in a laptop for $5,109–$6,999.99, against a 32GB discrete card at €6,499.90 in France that week, up €2,000 in nine weeks while nine of sixteen tracked cards did not move. So the two halves of the local-AI decision stopped being the same purchase. The card sells bandwidth (and the thing it is being bought for, a mixture-of-experts model that activates 9B of 74B parameters, is *less* bandwidth-bound than a dense model of the same quality — all weights resident, few read per token). The pool sells capacity at roughly a fifth to a quarter of the card's cost per gigabyte, and the vendor's own speed number was measured on the pool. That is the collision: the model's design made capacity the scarce input at the exact moment the market made capacity cheapest in a form factor that is not a graphics card. The tell is that the cheapest 24GB card that a builder would reach for — a used RTX 3090 at $1,361, up 47% on its twelve-month average — is not in the vendor's deployment table at all.

**Tension:** The build-vs-rent maths in this vertical has priced one variable for two cycles: memory. The 2026-10-13 run showed the input being rationed by product mix and by rate card. Who this hurts: the builder who budgeted a 24GB card on the strength of the label, and the secondhand buyer paying $1,361 for a 2020 card the vendor does not list. Who it helps: anyone who can put the working set in a 128GB pool, and the resale market for last-generation VRAM. What it hands the reader is a different comparison: not card vs cloud, but card vs pool.
