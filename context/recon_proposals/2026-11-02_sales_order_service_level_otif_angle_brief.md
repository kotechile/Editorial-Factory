**Signal B (Anchor 2):** SPS Commerce, *Retailer Deductions in Q4: How Walmart and Kroger Compare*, last updated 2026-10-13 — Kroger's ORAD (Original Requested Arrival Date) program sets a **93%** on-time threshold, raised from 90%, and a **95%** case fill rate; a supplier-caused late arrival costs **3% of the PO invoice**, a fill below 95% costs **3% of the missing items' value**, a shipment more than **30 minutes** past its appointment costs **$200**, and a label, case-pack or UPC error costs **$500 per PO**; the inquiry window is **180 days**. Suppliers dispute only **20%–30%** of deductions and win back an average **40%** of what they dispute. The policy document it restates supplies the mechanism: the fee is "assessed via invoice deductions", and "**many of these fees will not apply if Kroger manages your inbound lanes**". (https://www.spscommerce.com/community/articles/retailer-deductions-in-q4-how-walmart-and-kroger-compare and https://kroger.onenetwork.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/1-VNC-Program-Updates_final_signed.pdf)

**Emergent Collision Point:** Kroger's supplier-compliance program is not a penalty table; it is the only working measurement of Kroger's inbound service level. The 93% ORAD threshold and the 95% case fill are enforced by taking money off an invoice the supplier issued on a lane the supplier booked — the policy says so twice, in the fee mechanic ("assessed via invoice deductions") and in the exemption ("many of these fees will not apply if Kroger manages your inbound lanes"). Direct sourcing is Kroger moving onto Kroger-managed inbound: its own South America and Asia hubs, its own import model, and a grape programme that already runs through "service partners" rather than a scored vendor. So the instrument retires exactly where the exposure grows. On the direct share there is no supplier invoice to take 3% of, no appointment 30 minutes late that bills $200, and no compliance scorecard that reports the miss — and the policy's own answer, that Kroger "is responsible for location data on Kroger managed loads", is an admission that the reporting obligation moves to the buyer. Neither document says this: the investor deck is about cost of goods, and the deduction analysis assumes a vendor lane that the sourcing plan is dismantling.

**Hook:** Kroger is about to source roughly a tenth of its products itself, and the 3%-of-invoice late fee that has disciplined its suppliers since the ORAD threshold went to 93% has nothing to attach to on that volume — Kroger's own compliance letter says the fees do not apply once Kroger manages the inbound lane.

**Tension:** The saving and the risk are the same dollars. The intermediary's margin bought a party who carried the inbound promise — import of record, drayage, DC appointment, case fill — and who paid a 3% fine when it broke. Take the margin out and the promise stays, minus the party who was paid to hold it, minus the meter that priced the miss. Suppliers feel it as a shrinking scorecard; the retailer feels it as a metric that reads clean because nobody is left to report against it.

**Single claim to defend:** A deduction program is the only working measurement of a retailer's inbound service level, and moving buying direct silently retires it — so the direct share is where a service failure now costs the buyer instead of the seller, with nothing in the P&L that names it.
