(https://blogs.cisco.com/ai/project-quarry-agent-security-detections and https://www.theregister.com/security/2026/10/28/cisco-open-sources-tool-for-detecting-ai-agents-on-hacking-sprees/5305867)
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Pandey et al., "Local Allow, Global Deny: Why Per-Action Authorization Does Not Compose in LLM Agents", arXiv:2610.27620, submitted 2026-10-20. Central result: "relational purpose violations form a 2-safety hyperproperty with no per-trace locus of blame, so a per-action monitor that never raises a false alarm detects none of them, independent of the underlying model or decision procedure; they become visible only under cross-execution monitoring, which none of the six guardrails we survey implements". Empirically, on a live agent the consent-composition failure occurs in 22 of 60 rollouts (37%, Wilson 95% CI 26–49; 0/60 on benign controls); a per-action intent-closure guard leaves 20 of 60, "3.3 points below no guard" (Fisher p=0.85); the composition-aware guard removes every offending trace at 0/60 while blocking 0/60 benign. Across nine LLM judges, "Eight judges never block the witness (0/30 each)" step by step, while the same model shown both executions blocks 30/30 (Llama-4-Maverick 17/30). (https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.27620)
**Emergent Collision Point:** Both sources agree that the event is too small a unit and both move the unit up exactly one level — Quarry to the session, the paper to the pair of executions. Quarry stops at the session because a session is the widest thing a trace-level rule can read; the paper proves that any monitor reading one trace, at any granularity, cannot detect a relational violation without false alarms, and that this is a property of the monitor's *observability*, not of the model's ability. So the control that enterprises are evaluating this quarter — "does it see the whole session?" — is the wrong procurement question by construction: the class of policy that matters in a governed enterprise (purpose, consent, delegation) is relational across runs, and the newest detection language is one trace wide however completely it reads that trace.
**Hook:** On 28 October Cisco shipped a free rule language whose selling point is that it reads the whole agent session; eight days earlier a paper proved that the violations governance teams write policies for have no single-trace locus of blame — and on a live agent, the per-action guard left 20 of 60 offending runs standing against 22 of 60 with no guard at all.
**Tension:** The buyer's instinct is to widen the window — event, then session, then trace — and every widening feels like progress. The paper says the axis is wrong: observability is not a window width but a comparison across executions, and none of the six guardrails it surveys does it. That reframes three budgets at once — the trace store (you need retained comparable runs, not just longer sessions), the policy artifact (a declared consent or purpose bound the monitor can evaluate cumulatively), and the vendor question (can the thing compare two runs?), and it says a per-action intent check, which most enterprises already own, is statistically indistinguishable from no check on the class it was bought for.

**Single claim to defend:** Session-level detection is a real advance and still the wrong boundary: agent governance has to be enforced by a monitor that compares executions against a declared cumulative bound, because the violations that policy actually names are relational and a per-trace monitor — at event width or session width — cannot see them without false alarms.
