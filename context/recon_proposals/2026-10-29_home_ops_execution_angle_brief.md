**Signal A (Anchor 1):** AB 956 (Quirk-Silva), Chapter 791, Statutes of 2026 — approved by the Governor and filed with the Secretary of State on 2026-09-29. It amends Government Code § 66323 so a local agency must "ministerially approve" "Two detached, new construction, accessory dwelling units that do not exceed four-foot side and rear yard setbacks for a lot with a proposed or existing single-family dwelling"; a city "may impose ... A total floor area limitation of not more than 800 square feet of livable space" per unit; and a city need not approve a junior ADU "on the same lot where two detached, new construction, accessory dwelling units have been constructed". The act amends only Civil Code §§ 714.3 and 4751 and Government Code § 66323; it is not an urgency measure, so it takes effect 2027-01-01 (https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/billTextClient.xhtml?bill_id=202520260AB956).
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** CSLB press release #26-02, 2026-10-20: Colton and Austin Paulhus, the brothers behind the former ADU builder Anchored Tiny Homes, "each face 33 counts of diversion of construction funds, one count of conspiracy to commit a crime, and one count of employee wage theft", after the board investigated "more than 200 consumer complaints across 18 California counties alleging abandoned ADU projects, unpaid subcontractors, payments in advance of work performed or often not performed"; CSLB "revoked Anchored Tiny Homes' license in November 2024", and the settlement conference is "continued until January 12, 2027". In the same release the board restates the payment rule: "Down payments should never exceed $1,000 or 10 percent of the project's total cost, whichever is less. Any additional payments cannot exceed the value of the work performed or material delivered." Complaint coverage: over $1 million billed across 13 projects "despite performing no work and supplying no materials" (https://www.kcra.com/article/sacramento-county-da-files-charges-anchored-tiny-homes/74096522), more than $600,000 diverted "for expenses and activity unrelated to company projects", no plea entered at the 2026-10-14 hearing (https://www.nbcbayarea.com/investigations/tiny-home-builders-customers-prosecutors-angry/4158224).
**Emergent Collision Point:** AB 956 is a unit-mix change sold as a permitting change: the guaranteed total on a single-family lot stays four homes, but one of them moves out of the house and into the yard, so the project a homeowner actually finances becomes two full detached builds instead of one small interior conversion. The board's release is an enforcement notice about a builder that took the money first. Read together, the state has just made the larger-prepayment version of the project the default by right, and left untouched the only rule that governs when that money may legally change hands — a $1,000 down-payment ceiling and progress payments capped at the value of work already performed. Neither source says this. AB 956 never mentions payment; CSLB's release never mentions the new unit mix, and its own guidance is a PDF a homeowner has to go find. The collision is a timing one: the permit arrives in months, the enforcement arrives in years — two years from the 2024 revocation to a 2026 felony complaint, with a 2027 settlement conference still ahead.
**Hook:** From January 1, a California city must approve two detached backyard homes on a single-family lot, and the only money rule attached to the job is the one the state's own licensing board restated the week it announced 35 felony counts against an ADU builder: the down payment cannot exceed $1,000, and every payment after that has to trail completed work.
**Tension:** The payment statute is written for a $30,000 kitchen, not for two structures with their own foundations, panels and utility trenches. A builder who cannot carry the job to the first draw is exactly the builder who asks for a big deposit — which is the conduct the Anchored case charges. The homeowner's protection is not a cap in a statute; it is a draw schedule tied to inspections, a licensed general contractor with the working capital to reach the first one, and a paper trail that survives if the builder stops answering. Under AB 956 a city may cap each unit at 800 square feet of livable space and must hold to four-foot side and rear setbacks — but fire separation, not ADU law, decides how those two boxes actually sit on a standard lot.
**Target reader:** pro_homeowner
**Single claim to defend:** The state just changed which backyard units a California homeowner is guaranteed by right, and did not change the payment rule that governs the money — so on a two-cottage lot the schedule of draws, not the permit, is the thing that decides whether the project gets built or becomes a complaint file.
