**Emergent Collision Point:** Neither story is about the other, and neither states the rule the pair exposes. The buy-side move is a *product-mix* decision: NVIDIA keeps the die, discards the 32 GB consumer SKU built on it, and ships the same silicon as an 84 GB professional board whose price it has not published. The rent-side move is a *price* decision: the provider most often named as the cheap alternative raises twice in three months, and its steepest increase lands on the memory line — while the 96 GB workstation card's hourly rate does not move at all. Together: the scarce input is not FLOPS, it is memory content, and the market rations it two ways at once — by allocation on the silicon side and by rate card on the rental side. That is the collision: "buy a card instead of renting" (the 5090 is reportedly gone) and "rent instead of buying" (the cheap row repriced) both lost their cheap answer in the same fortnight, and the tell is that the price of the card NVIDIA *did* withdraw was never its list price at all.
**Hook:** The card that made local AI affordable on a desk — Nvidia's RTX 5090, the only consumer GPU with 32 GB of memory — is reported to be out of production, its GB202 silicon reserved for professional boards. Eleven days before that report, on 1 October, the cloud provider usually named as the cheap alternative raised its pay-as-you-go GPU rates for the second time in three months, with its largest increase, about 41%, on memory.
**Tension:** The build-vs-rent decision this vertical exists to price has carried two numbers: the card and the GPU-hour. In the last 30 days both were re-set by the same input. NVIDIA's own arithmetic is public — its product pages show the same 21,760 cores as the 5090 paired with 84 GB instead of 32 GB and no price — and Tom's Hardware spells out the incentive: GB202 on a professional or data-center board sells for "substantially more" than the $1,999 card it was going into. On the other side, the provider that built its pitch on being cheaper than hyperscalers moved its floor up twice, and its rate card shows the increase tracking memory rather than compute: every memory-heavy Hopper and Blackwell tier rose, the 96 GB desktop-class card did not. Who this empowers: teams that already own memory-heavy hardware, whoever still has a 32 GB card in hand, and anyone buying on $/GB rather than $/FLOP. Who it hurts: the solo builder who budgeted a $2,000 card, the small team that planned a 2027 rig off a $4,999 SKU, and anyone who signed a rental assumption in the spring and is now repriced in the autumn.
