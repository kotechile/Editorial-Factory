**Emergent Collision Point:** The two documents are both measurements of warehouse capacity published six days apart, and they point opposite ways — because they price different things. The survey measures whether the respondents' own committed capacity and costs got better or worse last month, and it says worse on both. The marketplace measures what a short-term pallet actually cleared for across 21 markets, and it says the average barely moved (+2.7%) while the dispersion tripled. Neither document can state the consequence: the thing an OTIF score needs is *committed* capacity — stock standing in a known node for a season — and that is the kind of capacity the survey says is contracting and repricing upward, while the capacity that got cheap is the flexible kind a planner cannot build a fill target on. So the buffer a service level depends on has split into two markets with two prices, and the expensive one is the one the standard playbook buys.
**Hook:** In the same week, a six-university survey said warehousing capacity was contracting for the sixth month of 2026 and storage prices were rising at their fastest pace since May 2022, while a marketplace index showed national vacancy still climbing and the price of a pallet in Dallas down 9.2% to $10.97.
**Tension:** Inventory is being pulled in at the fastest rate since September 2022 and inventory costs have reached their highest since March 2022 — the buffer is being bought into exactly the market that prices commitment highest. The shipper who answers an in-full miss by lifting safety stock at the same node pays the fastest-rising line in the chain; the shipper who can hold the same stock on short commitments in a Buyer's market (Phoenix, Reno, Savannah, Houston, Dallas) buys the same service for less, and pays for it in execution risk and in the possibility that the leverage disappears when CoStar's expected vacancy peak passes.
