## Derived arithmetic (computed by this desk from the verified figures — recheck after any rewrite)

- **A 2 percent deductible on a $400,000 dwelling limit = $8,000.** 0.02 × 400,000.
- **A 5 percent deductible on the same limit = $20,000**, i.e. $12,000 more retention on every claim.
- **The average 2023 claim is $20,062 (row 5).** At the 5 percent deductible the insurer's share of an average claim is $62 — $20,062 − $20,000.
- **Linear break-even on the 2 percent → 5 percent step:** 2.8 percent × $12,000 = **$336 a year** (row 6). The premium credit has to beat roughly $336 for the bigger deductible to be the losing trade; because 2.8 percent is the whole wind-and-hail class and a hurricane deductible triggers only on a named storm, the hurricane-specific frequency is lower, so the true bar is lower than $336, not higher.
- **California:** 15 percent of a $1,000,000 dwelling limit = **$150,000** before the quake policy pays; the 5 percent and 10 percent options do not exist above that limit (rows 7-8).

## Flags and limits the drafter must carry
1. **The 2.8 percent figure is a wind-and-hail frequency, not a hurricane frequency.** Do not present it as the chance a hurricane deductible will be triggered; state it as the frequency of the whole peril class the deductible belongs to.
2. **The claim frequency and severity are national all-peril figures** (excluding Alaska, Texas and Puerto Rico) and are 2023-vintage. A hurricane deductible applies only to a hurricane loss, so any per-year expected cost built from them is an order-of-magnitude check, not a quote.
3. **Florida permits the percentage form only where the deductible provision is clear and unambiguous** — the statute's (2)(b) bar is conditional, and (3)(a) then mandates the percentage ladder. Do not write that Florida "bans" percentage deductibles.
4. **No premium-credit percentage is cited in any row.** Nothing in this evidence set states what a carrier charges for buying a percentage deductible down; the article must tell the reader to get that number from the carrier rather than supply one.
5. **The Florida ceiling in row 3 is a state rule for risks under $500,000** and carries its own opt-out statement; it is not a national cap.
