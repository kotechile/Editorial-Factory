The National Fraud Enforcement Division names "fraud schemes involving tariff evasion, importation of goods or services, or forced labor" as one of four corporate enforcement priorities, sets ten factors prosecutors must give "great weight," and stands up a Corporate Enforcement Section that every ongoing corporate investigation must report to within seven days. Two days earlier, CBP detained another importer category — two Withhold Release Orders on Indonesian palm-oil producers, bringing "60 WROs and eight Findings" under 19 U.S.C. § 1307 (https://www.cbp.gov/newsroom/national-media-release/cbp-issues-withhold-release-orders-mitra-aneka-rezeki-and-hardaya).
**Emergent Collision Point:** The two moves are on different legal theories and neither is a tariff rate. Signal A is a *goods* liability: an anti-dumping/countervailing case attaches to a product defined by its country of origin, and if it lands it becomes a cash deposit on every covered entry — 12.70%–32.58% on tank cars from Mexico, with the deposit clock starting as early as Dec 2026. Signal B is a *buyer* liability: the enforcement file attaches to the importer's own documentation and, after Directive 26-12, converts a compliance gap into a corporate criminal exposure with ten aggravating factors and a centralized review. A total-landed-cost model — the standard engine for a nearshoring decision — prices duty *rates* and freight, so it is structurally blind to both: one arrives later as a retroactive deposit on the product line, the other is not priced at all because it is a legal standard, not a cost. Worse, the standard mitigation pulls the wrong lever — dual-sourcing spreads disruption risk but multiplies documentary surface, i.e. the exact exposure Signal B prices.
**Hook:** "On the last day of September, two U.S. trade cases opened against Mexico. The next morning, the Justice Department made tariff evasion and forced labor a corporate prosecution priority. Neither event showed up on a duty rate, and both now sit on the desk of anyone who moved a factory to Mexico to escape a tariff."
**Tension:** Nearshoring was sold as the way to de-risk a single-source offshore supply chain: shorter lanes, USMCA duty-free access, a friendly border. Both legs of this window's material say the destination-country tariff is the smallest of the three liabilities. The country you move to can be investigated as a subsidizer (Mexico appears on both the injury and the subsidy side of the hydraulic-cylinder case), and the file you must keep — who made the part, in which plant, under whose labor regime — is now a federal enforcement question rather than a procurement chore. It hurts lean importer-of-record teams who outsourced compliance to a broker; it helps firms that already model provenance as data.
