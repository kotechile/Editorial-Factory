**Signal A (Anchor 1):** Brownsville Public Utilities Board's new leak-notification programme, reported 2026-10-20 and live from 2026-11-01: AMI-driven text/email notices whose cadence the utility's own spokesperson describes as "every 24 hours, weekly, or biweekly depending on the consumption" (https://www.krgv.com/news/new-brownsville-pub-program-will-notify-customers-of-water-leaks). The programme sits on a $29.7 million AMI rollout covering about 52,000 electric and 58,000 water meters, with BPUB's project page promising "real-time usage monitoring" beside "Daily Usage Data" (https://www.brownsville-pub.com/projects/advanced-meter-infrastructure).

**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Home Assistant 2026.11's beta notes, published 2026-10-28, add LoRaWAN — "a long-range, low-power wireless protocol, often used for battery-powered sensors in places your Wi-Fi never reaches" — with ChirpStack and The Things Network connections and a dedicated network page, plus Zigbee Green Power for batteryless switches and sensors (https://rc.home-assistant.io/blog/2026/10/28/release-202611/). The transport's envelope, per the platform's own integration docs, is 5–15 km at 51 bytes a message (https://www.home-assistant.io/integrations/thethingsnetwork).

**Emergent Collision Point:** The utility's leak alert is clocked by its billing and call-centre operations, not by the damage curve: the meter is sold as "real-time", the customer product is "daily", and the notification repeats no faster than once every 24 hours — weekly or biweekly at lower flow. In the same week, the local-first layer absorbed the two capabilities that had kept a homeowner's own sensor out of the places leaks start: kilometre-range radio that reaches a crawlspace or an outbuilding, and devices that run without a battery. Neither document says the thing that matters: the utility sells you a signal on its schedule, while the hub lets you own the clock.

**Hook:** Brownsville switched on a leak-alert programme on 1 November; its fastest repeat is one message a day, and at lower consumption one a week. The meter can see a leak in real time. The message cannot.

**Tension:** Utilities are spending nine figures on meter networks and marketing the output as "early leak detection", but the notification cadence is a service-design choice — the same meter data supports daily, weekly or biweekly messages, and the utility picks. Meanwhile the protocols that let a homeowner put a sensor where the water actually enters the house — no Wi-Fi, no battery change — arrived in the local hub this week. The reader's decision is not "utility or hub"; it is whether the free alert is the plan or the backstop.

**Target reader:** pro_homeowner

**Single claim to defend:** A utility's smart-meter leak alert is a notification schedule, not a detector — the cadence is set by the utility, and only an owner-run sensor lets you choose the interval and the threshold that match what a leak costs.
