**Winner:** The Surface Transportation Board unanimously refused to throw out the Union Pacific–Norfolk Southern merger — the first coast-to-coast Class I railroad in U.S. history — sending it into a full merits review on a fixed clock, over the objections of rival railroads BNSF and CSX and a five-group coalition of chemical, fuel, and fertilizer shippers.

**Scores:** N=8 · A=9 · S=9 → Composite=8.6 (≥8 → proceed)

**Hook:** On Sept. 18, 2026, the Surface Transportation Board (STB) denied three motions that sought summary denial of the revised Union Pacific–Norfolk Southern merger application (Docket FD 36873). The motions — filed Aug. 6 by BNSF Railway, CSX Transportation, and a coalition of the American Chemistry Council, American Fuel & Petrochemical Manufacturers, the Alliance for Chemical Distribution, the National Industrial Transportation League, and The Fertilizer Institute — argued the railroads had failed to present a *prima facie* case that the deal serves the public interest. The Board let the case proceed anyway, with opening comments due Nov. 18, 2026 and responses due Feb. 16, 2027.

**Tension:** A UP-NS combination would stitch Union Pacific's 23-state western network to Norfolk Southern's 22-state eastern network into a single system — what the companies bill as "America's first transcontinental railroad." That is the first true coast-to-coast Class I merger in the modern era, and the battle lines are drawn: UP/NS promise single-line service (10,000 interline lanes converted to single-line, 2 million truckloads off highways), while BNSF and the shippers argue the deal's only real competition fix — Committed Gateway Pricing — applies to less than 1% of rail traffic and could leave many customers paying *higher* rates. Who it hurts: captive shippers locked to a single railroad, and bulk chemical/fertilizer/fuel shippers who fear losing competitive routing. Who it helps: UP/NS and shippers who gain single-line, faster cross-country service. The STB's own newest member, Richard Kloster, concurred only to warn the applicants "still have a long way to go."
