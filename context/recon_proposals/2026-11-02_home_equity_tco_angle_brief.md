**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Tax Foundation, 2026-10-30 — 12 property tax measures are on the November ballot across 7 states; Florida Amendment 3 lifts the non-school homestead exemption to $150,000 in 2027 and $250,000 in 2028; Oklahoma State Question 847 cuts the homestead assessment cap from 3 percent to 1.75 percent; the report's own finding is that exemptions and assessment limits "largely shift property tax burdens onto other less-favored properties" (https://taxfoundation.org/blog/property-tax-ballot-measures-2026), corroborated by the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy, 2026-10-21, that property taxes nationally "have increased only slightly more than overall inflation since 2020" while home prices rose more than 50 percent (https://www.lincolninst.edu/publications/land-lines-magazine/articles/understanding-property-tax-revolts-an-explainer).
**Emergent Collision Point:** The ballot measures cut a line that grew roughly with inflation, in a year when the line that grew 45 percent after inflation — insurance — appears on no ballot in any of the seven states. Neither source can say this alone: the tax analysis measures the tax, the insurance analysis measures the premium, and the collision is that the affordability revolt is being voted on the smaller, slower bill while the faster one is repriced by weather and underwriting rather than by any vote.
**Hook:** Home insurance now costs 14 percent more than the property tax bill, on average, in the two-thirds of U.S. counties where it is the larger of the two — and the 12 property tax measures on tomorrow's ballot do not touch it.
**Tension:** A homestead exemption is a transfer, not a cut: the levy is set by the revenue the locality needs, so shielding the first $250,000 in Florida moves the bill onto renters, second homes and commercial property (Tax Foundation; Lincoln Institute's Langley puts a sustainable statewide exemption at $20,000–$40,000). Meanwhile the premium is set by hail, wildfire and reinsurance — a line the owner can influence only through the condition of the house, not through a vote.
**Target reader:** pro_homeowner
**Single claim to defend:** The property-tax revolt is aimed at the slower-growing of the two recurring bills on a house; the faster one is priced by risk, appears on no ballot, and is the one a homeowner can still move with capital.
