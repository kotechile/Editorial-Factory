**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Adobe Analytics' final Prime Big Deal Days figures, reported 2026-10-08 (https://www.digitalcommerce360.com/2026/10/08/amazon-prime-day-big-deal-days-sales-2026) — **$9.86 billion** of U.S. online spend across 6–7 October, **+8.5%** year over year, with discounts peaking at **20% off listed price** and category sales against a September 2026 average day at **+85% electronics, +158% toys, +90% video games, +73% personal care**; Adobe reads the event as pushing retailers to "invest in earlier pre-holiday promotions than they would otherwise hold".
**Emergent Collision Point:** Both anchors are price moves reported inside the same week — Adobe's on the 8th of October, PepsiCo's on the same day for a quarter that closed on 5 September, so the pair is thematic, not causal. They are also two different shapes of the same lever. The event's +158% is volume moved *within* a calendar: a two-day discount pulls forward units that would have sold in a later week, so the spike is real sales and borrowed demand at the same time. PepsiCo's numbers are the other shape — a standing affordability investment in the food business that lifted organic volume and cost 280 bps of segment margin, and a beverage business whose price held while organic volume declined. Neither document says what the pair implies: a plan's promotional coefficient is a property of the *occasion*, not of the business, so a lift measured in a two-day event cannot be carried into a base forecast, and a standing price investment cannot be read as a promotion. One is repaid in adjacent weeks; the other is repaid out of margin, and only one of them shows up as a hole in next month's number.
**Hook:** Toys sold 158% above a September day during Amazon's two-day October event, at discounts that peaked at 20% off — and in the same week PepsiCo said the affordability investments it has been funding all year lifted volume in its food business while costing that segment 280 basis points of core operating margin, with its beverage organic volume down and its full-year EPS outlook cut.
**Tension:** A demand plan consumes whatever arrives as "sales", and October is now a month of manufactured peaks: Adobe's own read is that the event is teaching retailers to pull pre-holiday promotions earlier, which moves the peak rather than growing the year. Gartner's Denver agenda for next week names the factory-side consequence — monthly buckets colliding with weekly schedules and "poorly managed forecast-consumption windows" producing "phantom demand spikes", costly changeovers and emergency runs. So the plan gets a spike it did not forecast, commits capacity to it, and then faces the trough the spike borrowed from — while the year-round price programme that was supposed to protect volume shows up not as a spike at all but as 280 basis points of margin, a line no forecast reviews.
**Target reader:** ops_leader
**Single claim to defend:** A price move shows up in a plan in two different currencies — a promotion lends units from adjacent weeks, a standing price investment spends margin — and a forecast that records both as demand will over-commit capacity to a spike it has to give back and under-report the margin it is already spending.
