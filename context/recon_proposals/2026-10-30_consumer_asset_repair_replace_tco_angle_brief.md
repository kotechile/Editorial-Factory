**Emergent Collision Point:** The two anchors describe the same object from opposite ends and neither states the result. iFixit measures *access* — how easily a person can reach the part, which is what a teardown can see. Apple's page prices *money* — and its price page holds two columns that never appear beside each other: the insured schedule and the uninsured one. The insured schedule is identical on every iPhone from the 12 to the Duo ($0 battery, $29 screen, $99 inner display, $99 other accidental damage, $149 theft or loss); the uninsured schedule runs from $89 to $1,499. So the entire repairability difference between a $599 repair and a $1,499 repair is invisible to anyone who buys the plan and fully borne by anyone who does not. iFixit's score cannot see this, because a teardown has no column for a subscription. And because the aftermarket cannot undercut a bonded display, the plan is the only substitute the market offers. Consequence, computable from the two pages: the Duo's premium is $199.99 a year against the 18 Pro's $149.99 — $50 more — and it buys $600 more of tail ($1,499 vs $899) and $620 more of screen risk ($999 vs $379).
**Hook:** $999 for the inner screen is exactly half of the $1,999 Apple charges for the phone it sits in — and $114 more than the whole trade-in ceiling Apple quotes on the same buy page ($885).
**Tension:** The phone is the most repairable foldable iFixit has opened, and that achievement is priced nowhere that a buyer will look. The score rewards access; the fee schedule prices risk; the plan is the only instrument that converts one into the other, and its premium is the only place Apple admits which model is riskier. A buyer who reads the repairability coverage and skips the price page buys the wrong thing for the right reason. Who this threatens: the "repairable = cheaper to own" assumption the right-to-repair movement has been selling for a decade. Who it empowers: the owner who prices the risk as a subscription instead of a hope.
