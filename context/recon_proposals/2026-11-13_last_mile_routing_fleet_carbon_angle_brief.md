**Signal B (Anchor 2):** CarParts.com's internal last-mile network carried **more than 6,000 packages** in the quarter ended 3 October, roughly doubling Q2, on a medium-term target of **300,000 packages a year — about 5% of total outbound volume** — with three of its four distribution centres now running next-day delivery through that network; Q3 gross margin rose 30 basis points to 33.4%, "primarily driven by lower freight costs" (2026-10-29 results — https://ir.carparts.com/news/news-details/2026/CarParts-com-Reports-Third-Quarter-2026-Results/default.aspx and 2026-11-11 — https://www.supplychaindive.com/news/carpartscom-revs-up-in-house-last-mile-delivery-for-cost-speed-benefits/874287/).

**Emergent Collision Point:** The two documents price opposite sides of one decision and the accounting standard splits them. A parcel that moves on a national carrier's truck is a purchased transport service — Scope 3, the category the GHG Protocol calls "Upstream transportation and distribution" (https://ghgprotocol.org/sites/default/files/standards/Corporate-Value-Chain-Accounting-Reporing-Standard_041613_2.pdf). The identical parcel on the shipper's own van is fuel burned in a vehicle the shipper owns — Scope 1, by EPA's own definition (https://www.epa.gov/climateleadership/scope-1-and-scope-2-inventory-guidance). So a 5.9% rate increase is not only a cost signal; it is a boundary price. The moment it is cheaper to insource, the tonnage moves from a line a shipper can only estimate from a carrier's invoice to a line it must measure from its own fuel and report as its own — which is exactly the line California's first disclosure cycle asked for on 10 November (https://ww2.arb.ca.gov/sites/default/files/2026-10/2026_Climate_Disclosure_Mailer.pdf). Neither the rate announcement nor the earnings release says any of this: one prices a service, the other reports a margin.

**Hook:** CarParts.com will route about 300,000 packages a year through its own vans to escape a carrier rate card that rises 5.9% on 21 December — and every one of those parcels changes which line of its carbon report it lands on.

**Tension:** Insourcing can cut real miles (denser routes, no linehaul handoff, fewer failed deliveries) and still push the *reported* number up, because the owned litre is counted at 100% while the purchased mile was one estimate inside a category the first California filing did not even require. Who this helps: shippers with depot control and dense routes, and the software vendors selling fleet telematics. Who this threatens: parcel carriers whose pricing power was itself the moat, and any sustainability team that has been reporting a falling Scope 3 number produced by switching carriers rather than by cutting miles.
