**Emergent Collision Point:** The reshoring/nearshoring decision has been framed as a *finished-good* problem — where to assemble the bottle. But the tariff regime just migrated upstream into the *inputs* (resins, molds, closures, packaging film). That breaks the clean story: a company that has already localized 98% of its spend — Coca-Cola — still cannot opt out of the tariff shock, because its packaging inputs are traded across a tariff-torn North American border. The decision engine that only re-costs the finished-good landing is solving the wrong problem.

**Hook:** Coca-Cola's CFO just told Fortune the company's $10 billion US build-out is "a growth play, not a tariff hedge" — because there's almost nothing left to reshore. The same week, Rabobank found roughly $3.1 billion of North American plastics trade sitting under a ~53–55% all-in tariff, with Section 338 stacking even on free-trade goods. Reshoring didn't kill tariff risk. It moved it upstream, into the packaging.

**Tension:** Who this empowers — ops leaders who run a tariff-adjusted *input* landed-cost model (the bill-of-materials level, not the finished SKU) and who treat "domestic" as a supply-chain claim, not a tariff shield. Who this threatens — leaders who underwrite reshoring/nearshoring on FOB-to-finished-good math and discover the tariff has simply relocated to their Tier-2 packaging, resin, and mold suppliers. The systemic friction: policy now changes faster than purchase orders can be repriced, so fixed-price commitments on inputs are becoming a bet against the tariff regime itself.

**Target reader:** ops_leader (VP of Supply Chain & Operations Leader — numerate, balance-sheet aware, ROI-first, grounded in physical physics).

**Single claim to defend:** Reshoring does not eliminate tariff risk — it relocates it upstream into inputs (packaging, resins, molds), where Section 338 duties now stack on top of base rates and override USMCA free-trade terms; so the supplier-risk decision engine's job has moved from "where do I assemble?" to "what is the tariff-adjusted landed cost of my bill of materials?" — a claim proven by Coca-Cola's "$10B as growth, not tariff hedge" (98¢/dollar already domestic) colliding with ~$3.1B of plastics trade now under a ~53–55% all-in duty.
