**Emergent Collision Point:** The two documents measure opposite ends of the same failure and neither can state the connection. Cleo prices the exception's aftermath and finds that the ledger a tower can reach — the chargeback, the expedite, the dispute — carries about a tenth of the cost, while the other nine tenths are decided afterwards in a retailer's scorecard, in shelf allocation and in the next order. ASCM and DSCI then show where the automation went: it sits in planning (2.28 and 2.12 of 5) and thins out exactly in execution (1.59 at the bottom), which is where the exception lives. Put together, the layer an organisation builds to catch exceptions is both the least automated part of its chain and the part that cannot see the cost it is supposed to be preventing — so "exceptions resolved" is a metric for the cheapest tenth of the loss, and the more of them a tower closes the more precisely it optimises the wrong end.
**Hook:** Cleo puts the annual OTIF bill at about $100 billion and the part a manufacturer can actually bill, dispute or expedite at $10 billion to $11 billion — nine dollars of consequence outside the ledger for every one inside it, which is the ratio a control tower is quietly built to ignore.
**Tension:** Who this empowers: the supply chain architect who can now argue for measuring the scorecard consequence rather than the alert count, and for spending the next increment on execution rather than another forecasting layer. Who this threatens: the visibility platform whose business case is exceptions surfaced and alerts closed, and the planning suite that has absorbed the AI budget while the execution layer it feeds stayed at 1.59 of 5.
**Target reader:** supply_chain_architect
