**Angle Type:** Synthesis (Cross-Topic Fusion)

**Signal A (Anchor 1):** How Much Should an Agent Remember? Deployment Lessons from Self-Distilled Memory for LLM Agents (IBM Research / CUGA / watsonx Orchestrate), arXiv:2611.26361, submitted 19 November 2026 — a self-distillation pipeline shipped in production (altk-evolve, IBM's CUGA agent, watsonx Orchestrate); the central finding is that "the right amount of memory is a property of the model, not the memory": DeepSeek-V3.2 gains +9.5 points of task completion and +16.1 points of scenario completion with its full 191-guideline set, while gpt-oss-120b starts at 39.9%, reaches 51.2% with all 199 guidelines but 55.4% (mean of two runs) with a *selected subset of about 29*; body figures verified against arxiv.org/html/2611.26361v1 (fetched 2026-11-23) (https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26361).

**Signal B (Anchor 2):** NestDaddy's daily audit of every remote MCP server in the Official MCP Registry, published 22 November 2026 — 86.8% of servers answer every daily check but 11.0% miss at least one check in ten; 25.1% of servers rewrote at least one tool in the last 30 days; 33.7% (13,848) list tools only after sign-in; 322,242 public tools across 22,576 servers; median first answer 253 ms; the report's own framing: "an agent that learned your tools last week can break when a name or an input changes silently" (https://nestdaddy.com/blog/state-of-remote-mcp-servers-2026).

**Signal C (corroboration, same week):** Two more memory papers arrived within 48 hours of Signal A — GrepMem (arXiv:2611.25123, 18 November 2026): an append-only plain-text log + a regex search tool reaches 95–97% on ContextBench versus at most 21% for lossy baselines, with no embeddings, no index, no extra models; and MemFolder (arXiv:26427v1 → 2611.26427, 19 November 2026): RecordFolder keeps 99.0% of average performance on LoCoMo/PersonaMem/LongMemEval with 50% fewer records, beating the strongest baseline's retention by 1.9 points. Three independent teams converged on "simpler, smaller, self-distilled" in one week — corroboration, not anchors.

**Emergent Collision Point:** Signal A says the memory tier is becoming a *solved* engineering problem: the open questions are now how many guidelines, whose model — 29 of 199 beat the full set on a weak model, and a grep over a plain log beat every structured stack. Signal B says the surface those memories are *about* has no equivalent closure: a quarter of registry servers changed at least one tool in the last 30 days and 11% miss a check in ten, so the procedure your agent distilled into a guideline last month describes a tool that may no longer exist under that name. Neither source states the shared conclusion, which is the article's thesis: **teams are pouring effort into the half of the architecture that just closed, while the half that churns — the tool fabric — has no memory of its own changes and no owner watching for them.** A perfect distilled memory of a tool that was silently renamed on Tuesday still calls the wrong tool, and the failure arrives as a plausible answer, not an error. The concrete prescription neither paper gives: audit your tool fabric the way you now audit your memory tier — by change rate and answer rate, not by star count — and re-verify distilled guidelines against the registry's tool-change history every cycle.

**Hook:** A model with 29 distilled guidelines beat the same model carrying all 199 — in the same week that 25.1% of MCP registry servers changed a tool, and 11% stopped answering one day in ten.

**Tension:** The architecture conversation has been about memory tiers since October; this week's three papers show that argument resolving into simple answers. But the guidelines those teams distill are snapshots of a tool surface that changed under 25% of its servers this month, and nothing in the memory pipeline re-checks them. The architect who upgraded to the newest structured-memory stack has bought certainty about the layer that no longer loses anything — and inherited exposure to the layer that does.
