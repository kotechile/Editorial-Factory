**Signal A (Anchor 1):** CBP interim final rule, "Heightened Requirements for Foreign Importers of Record", published 2026-11-06 at 91 FR 69977 (USCBP-2026-1387; CBP Dec. 26-19), effective 2027-01-05 — new 19 CFR Part 110 defines a U.S. importer of record as "an entity that is organized under the laws of the United States, is located in the United States, and maintains at all times: (i) at least 51% of beneficial owner(s) who are United States citizens or lawful permanent residents and have control of the entity; or (ii) direct ownership of a significant amount of unencumbered real property in the United States"; new 19 CFR 141.21 bars a foreign IOR from entering merchandise for consumption "unless it is a CTPAT validated IOR or designates a customs broker who is CTPAT validated"; new 19 CFR 141.22 requires a U.S. IOR to declare its qualification at entry and entry summary. CBP's population data: 16% of importers of record are foreign, 0.4% of those are CTPAT validated, 68% already use a CTPAT validated broker, and "The remaining 32% of foreign IORs will not be able to continue importing into the United States unless they switch brokers or become CTPAT validated themselves" (https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/11/06/2026-22735/heightened-requirements-for-foreign-importers-of-record).

**Emergent Collision Point:** The reshoring buildout is an import programme: the quarter that carried the investment surge carried it as imported machinery, and a large share of the plants being announced are foreign-owned (AUDI's first U.S. plant, U.S. Steel's hot-strip mill, the machine-tool order run). Every one of those machine entries needs an importer of record, and from 2027-01-05 the rule decides who qualifies by *ownership*: a U.S.-organised entity whose beneficial owners are not U.S. citizens or green-card holders fails limb (i) and must fall back on limb (ii), direct ownership of a significant amount of unencumbered U.S. real property. Read together, the plant is no longer only the thing the reshoring case buys — it is the asset that can qualify a foreign-owned operator to clear the machines that build it. Neither document says this: the rule talks about shell companies and duty collection, the statistical release talks about GDP contributions, and the collision is that the two are the same balance sheet.
**Hook:** A plant that breaks ground in Georgia in 2027 and imports its own machinery will find that the customs question is not the tariff rate on the machines but who owns the company importing them — and that the answer has a 51% threshold and a real-property escape hatch.
**Tension:** The rule is aimed at shell importers who vanish with the duty bill (CBP recovered 3.1% of $1.16 billion it found), but its test reads on ownership, not on conduct, so it also catches the foreign-parented manufacturer that just built the plant the policy wants. Who it helps: U.S.-owned operators who own their sites — they clear as before. Who it hurts: the FDI plant on leased or incentive-held land, and the domestic buyer whose supplier files DDP as a foreign importer of record.
