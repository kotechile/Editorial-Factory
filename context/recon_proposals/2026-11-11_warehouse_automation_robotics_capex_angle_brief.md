**Emergent Collision Point:** Neither document states the claim the pair supports. AutoStore reports a margin and never mentions what that margin costs the buyer to reverse; Exotec reports that buyers cannot switch and never mentions that the difficulty has a published counterpart in the seller's accounts. Put them together and the 71.3% gross margin is a *first estimate of the exit cost*: the part of the invoice that is not recoverable materials is, by construction, the part a buyer cannot get back when it wants out. The 94% is what that looks like ten years later from inside the building. The article's claim is that a warehouse automation decision should be underwritten as an asset with no resale value, not as capital equipment with a residual — and the one number that says so is on the seller's income statement, not in the buyer's payback model.
**Hook:** AutoStore kept 71 cents of every revenue dollar as gross profit in the quarter it booked its highest-ever third-quarter order intake; eight days earlier, 94% of 250 automation buyers said switching providers would be prohibitively expensive. The two figures are the same contract read from opposite ends of the invoice.
**Tension:** The vendor needs the order book to keep growing (guidance raised to about USD 750 million, order intake up 16.5%) and the buyer needs the system to be replaceable in a decade. Every dollar of gross margin is a dollar of the buyer's sunk cost, and the industry's answer to multi-vendor accountability (60% of surveyed systems were designed by more than one company; one in seven buyers say nobody owns the outcome) is consolidation — AutoStore buying Logiqs, Symbotic buying cellumation — which raises the exit cost again. The party that benefits from the lock is also the party selling the upgrade path.
