**Emergent Collision Point:** In the same week, the two carbon meters the U.S. supply chain answers to moved in opposite directions: one state nailed a filing date down, the federal agency slid its own by four and a half months and may delete the program the date belongs to. Neither document says anything about the other, and neither says where a last-mile fleet sits in either system. That is the emergent question — under SB 253 the filer is the $1bn-revenue shipper, and under the GHGRP the filer is the fuel supplier or the large facility; a carrier only enters either regime through Scope 3, and CARB's proposed 2027 rule requires five of the fifteen Scope 3 categories (1, 3, 5, 6, 7) while leaving upstream transportation and distribution (category 4) and downstream transportation and distribution (category 9) voluntary. So the one figure customers are already demanding from carriers — grams per shipment — is the figure no regulator has made mandatory, which turns it from a compliance artifact into a contract term.
**Hook:** On 12 October California's Office of Administrative Law approved the climate-disclosure regulation, making 10 November 2026 the first hard carbon filing date for large companies doing business there; two days later the federal EPA used an interim final rule to push its own reporting deadline from 30 October 2026 to 10 March 2027, saying it could not get its reporting portal ready in time.
**Tension:** The state filing date is 18 days out and covers only scopes 1 and 2, so nothing in it requires a shipper to report the freight it buys; the federal programme that did standardise fuel-side reporting is being pushed into 2027 and proposed for deletion. A carrier asked this quarter for a per-shipment carbon number is therefore answering a procurement question with no statutory definition behind it — and whichever party writes the definition (fuel-based or activity-based, well-to-wheel or tank-to-wheel, which factor vintage) decides whether the fleet looks efficient or wasteful.
