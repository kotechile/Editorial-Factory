## Notes on the evidence
- Hosts are spread across five independent publishers — `cmr.berkeley.edu`, `ideas.repec.org`, `arxiv.org`, `mmh.com`, `strategosinc.com` — so the floor is met with margin (5 sources, 5 hosts; the floor is 3 sources on 2 hosts). Rows 1–3 are the measured leg (three separate studies, three separate samples: one retailer's 2001 execution study, 370,000 records across 37 stores in 2008, ~24,000 SKUs across 11 grocery stores in 2026), row 4 is the achievable-target leg and row 5 is the planning-system requirement that turns record accuracy into an S&OP input rather than a warehouse KPI.
- **Row 3's matcher note:** the gate's evidence window lands on the study's sample sentence (`~24,000 SKUs sold in 11 stores`) because the token `11` first appears there, before the abstract's `11% store-wide sales lift`. Both figures are the paper's own abstract text and were read in the fetched body at retrieval time; the 11% rides inside row 3 rather than carrying its own row, and the article must attribute it to this study's field quasi-experiment in grocery retail, not generalise it to distribution centres.
- **The counter-intuitive finding is the article's spine and must not be smoothed away:** inaccuracy is *negatively* associated with promotional activity (row 3) while it *rises* with average inventory level and restocking frequency. The lazy thesis ("promo spikes corrupt the data") is contradicted by the only study that measured it; the defensible one is that the everyday fast-moving, frequently restocked, perishable item is where the record drifts, which is exactly the A-item a sensing engine is bought to manage. Do not blend the three studies' rates: 65% of records (rows 1–2, two different retailers, one 2001 and one 2008) and the 2026 grocery sample's associations measure different things on different populations, and the 2017 trade cases (row 4) are practitioner reports, not a study.
- **The one rule with numbers the article must land:** count by category first, not by store — 26.4% of the variance in inaccuracy sits between product categories against 2.7% between stores (row 2) — then weight within the category to the records that overstate stock, because that is where the audit's sales lift concentrated (row 3), and hold the plan's input at the 95%-99% band an MRP/ERP engine needs (row 5), against the 99%-plus that a properly run cycle-count program has been shown to reach (row 4).
- **Not to be claimed:** that record accuracy removes demand risk (it does not — the reducible error only), that the 11% lift is universal (one grocery chain, 11 stores, a quasi-experiment), or that a sensing upgrade is worthless. The article's claim is about *sequencing and allocation* of the next dollar, which is why the decision line names the budget cycle.
