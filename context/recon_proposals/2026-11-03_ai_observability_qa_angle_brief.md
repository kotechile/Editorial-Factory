**Winner:** Your eval score is decided twice outside the model — once when the run is rendered into a packet, once when the reply is parsed into a verdict — and neither decision is versioned, checked or reported.

**Signal A (Anchor 1):** Xia, Lu, Li, Sejdinovic and Zhu, "Does the Judge See What the Evaluation Requires? Input Assurance for LLM Agent Evaluation," arXiv:2611.02676, 2 November 2026 (CSIRO, Adelaide University, UNSW; https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.02676). The renderer that converts recorded runs into judge packets omitted every structured tool call, leaving 53 of 60 judged steps blank while the packets matched their hashes, both separately written checkers passed and staging succeeded; 18 of 19 constructed fault cases were stopped only after three controls were added.
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** Hasan and Lwowski, "Same Outputs, Different Scores: Auditing the Unchecked Graders of Multimodal LLM Benchmarks," arXiv:2611.03377, 2 November 2026 (WACV 2027; https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.03377). Reproducing the official scorers of eight peer-reviewed benchmarks on five open vision-language models, the scoring rule alone moves accuracy by up to 22 points on identical outputs, and by exactly zero wherever a model emits a clean option letter.
**Industry leg (corroboration, not an anchor):** Artificial Analysis publishes its own "Multiple Choice Extraction Regex" and says "we always take the last match found to account for self-correction in responses," while versioning the index weights and not the parser (https://artificialanalysis.ai/methodology/intelligence-benchmarking). Carried for the reader, with no load-bearing claim resting on a page that states a design rather than a measurement.
**Emergent Collision Point:** Each paper measures one end of the same pipe and stops at its own boundary. Paper A shows the judge is fed a packet whose content a renderer decides, and that the pipeline's own checks — a hash, a checker, a staging copy — certify the bytes rather than the evidence. Paper B shows the verdict is then read out of free text by a regex or a fallback rule that can move the number 22 points on identical outputs. Neither paper says the score is manufactured at both ends of the model, and no source states the consequence: a team can make its judge more accurate, its prompts better and its model stronger, and still be grading a packet with the calls missing and an answer that was never given. The fix that follows is not a better judge. It is a contract on the input and a reported rate on the output.
**Hook:** A vision-language model asked whether a part is defective replies "The object is a nail," and one published benchmark scores that answer correct, because its grader snaps any string to the nearest option with a zero cutoff. In another pipeline, a judge was handed packets with every structured tool call stripped out — 53 of 60 judged steps blank — and the hashes matched, the checkers passed and nothing reported a problem.
**Tension:** Every eval budget in the last year went into the middle of the pipe: better judges, bigger rubrics, calibrated prompts. Both ends stayed unowned. The renderer is somebody's script from a Tuesday; the scoring rule is "a few lines at the bottom of a scoring script" that peer review never opened, because reviewers judged the dataset. The teams hurt are the ones with the most mature harness — they are the ones whose numbers get compared across quarters — and the ones helped are the teams willing to report a non-compliance rate, which is cheap and rare.
**Target reader:** evals_infra_eng (AI QA & Evals Infrastructure Engineer)
**Single claim to defend:** An eval score is produced by two pieces of plumbing that sit outside the model — a renderer upstream and a parser downstream — and because both fail silently at exactly the inputs where the model is weakest, the score can only be trusted when the pipeline reports what it rendered, what it parsed and how often the fallback fired.
