**Emergent Collision Point:** Both announcements are about what the buyer is allowed to know before signing, and they answer it on opposite sides of the fence. The operator-side record is an activity log: tens of thousands of totes, tens of thousands of hours, a production-capacity figure for the vendor's factory — and not one denominator that a payback needs. The vendor-side record is the model itself: an ROI tool, a design simulator, a downtime-reduction estimate and a storage-utilisation estimate, all generated by the party being paid. Neither source states the consequence of the other. The consequence is that a warehouse automation business case has quietly changed hands: the inputs a chief financial officer's approval rests on — expected utilisation, expected downtime, expected storage gain, expected labour offset — now ship as vendor products, while the one thing the vendor never publishes is the number that closes the loop, the cost per unit of work at a real site. A buyer who fills in a vendor's spreadsheet is not amortizing a machine; it is amortizing the vendor's assumptions. And the RaaS route does not escape it — `Pio Pal` moves the same authorship into a rate card instead of a model, where the operator can see the price per line but still not the utilisation or downtime it is built on. The operator's own two inputs — its loaded labour cost per tote and its peak share of annual volume — are the only lines in the file it actually owns.
**Hook:** Two years and four months after GXO and Agility signed the industry's first humanoid RaaS agreement, the flagship deployment has moved more than 100,000 totes and logged more than 65,000 hours of real-world operation — and its published record still carries no price per tote, no labour cost saved, no payback. In the same month, AutoStore started shipping `CubeExplore`, a GenAI tool that builds the ROI case for buying AutoStore systems, alongside its own estimates for downtime avoided (35-45%) and storage gained (10-15%). The vendor now supplies the model; the operator still has not been given the denominator.
**Tension:** This is the quiet reorganisation of an approval process, not a pricing scandal. A payback model is only as good as the two assumptions nobody can pull out of a vendor's comparable-project database: how many units of work *this* site really gets per year, and what the labour it displaces actually costs once attendance, overtime and turnover are loaded in. Both belong to the operator, and neither appears in either release. When the model, the simulator and the efficiency estimates all come from the seller, the buyer's financial control degrades into arithmetic on someone else's numbers — and the damage does not show up as a wrong price. It shows up as a piloted fleet that "works" (100,000 totes) that nobody can scale on evidence, and as a subscription whose per-line rate is legible while its utilisation assumption is not. Who this helps: vendors, because a modelled business case closes faster than a measured one, and buyers with enough volume and site data to run their own benchmark. Who this exposes: the mid-market operator who has no throughput baseline to check the tool against, and the CFO who signs a multi-year RaaS contract whose break-even quietly migrated into the vendor's model. The harder fact underneath both releases is timing: GXO's own expansion starts in early 2027, and Agility is building for 10,000 robots a year against an installed base that has logged 65,000 commercial hours — capacity supplied ahead of measured demand, which is exactly the condition under which a seller's model gets optimistic.
**Target reader:** `ops_leader` — VP of Supply Chain & Operations, numerate and balance-sheet aware, who has to sign or reject an automation business case this quarter and has been handed a vendor-authored spreadsheet to do it with.
**Single claim to defend:** In October 2026 the warehouse automation market moved the business case itself into the seller's product line, so an operator's payback model is now built from vendor estimates of utilisation, downtime and storage gain — and the only defensible numbers left in it are the two the operator measures itself, its loaded cost per unit of work and its own peak share of annual volume, which is why a deployment that can report 100,000 totes without a cost per tote is not evidence of payback at all.
