**Thesis:** "Bonded" is a number, and the number is set by the state's leverage over the contractor, never by your exposure on the job: California's ceiling is a flat $25,000 whether the contract is $40,000 or $400,000, Oregon's residential bond is one-twentieth of the liability insurance the same statute demands, Arizona's general residential bond may be as little as $5,000, and Washington gives a homeowner two years to sue on the bond while Oregon cuts a complaint off at 14 months — so the bond is a licence condition you verify, not a promise you rely on, and the insurance certificate plus the claim deadline are the two things that actually decide whether you get paid.

Notes the drafter must respect (read by eye out of the fetched pages, because the gate checks a figure and never its gloss):

- **A homeowner is a named beneficiary — say so, and then say what that is worth.** Cal. B&P § 7071.5 names, first, "A homeowner contracting for home improvement upon the homeowner's personal family residence damaged as a result of a violation of this chapter by the licensee." The bond is "executed by an admitted surety in favor of the State of California". So the homeowner can claim — but the pot is § 7071.6(a)'s flat $25,000, and § 7071.6(b)'s final sentence only confirms "nothing in this section shall be construed so as to prevent any beneficiary specified in subdivision (a) of Section 7071.5 from claiming or recovering the full measure of the bond". "Full measure of the bond" is $25,000, not "full measure of your loss": write the cap, never "you can recover everything".
- **You are not paid by asking.** ORS 701.150(1) is the cleanest statement of the gate: the board issues a determination "[i]f a licensed contractor fails to pay a complainant amounts due under a court judgment or under a final order of the Bureau of Labor and Industries" — and (2) adds that "the surety may not pay on a complaint until the surety receives notice from the board that the complaint is ready for payment". Washington's route is a suit in superior court with the surety named as a party (RCW 18.27.040(3)). The article must not describe the bond as a fund you draw on by complaining.
- **The two deadlines are the piece's most useful numbers and they are not the same.** Washington: two years from substantial completion **or abandonment, whichever occurred first** — abandonment is the clause that matters when the contractor walks. Oregon: 14 months after the earlier of the licence expiring/cancelling or the bond being cancelled, and the clock runs from a licence event, not from your discovery. Carry both in the same section, dated.
- **The bond does not refill.** ORS 701.068(3): "the aggregate liability of the surety under the bond for complaints against the contractor may not exceed the penal sum of the bond no matter how many years the bond is in force", and a continuation certificate, reinstatement, reissue or renewal "may not increase the liability of the surety". Carry verbatim in prose under row 5 — no row (no figure the matcher can anchor).
- **When the claims exceed the bond the state suspends the licence, which is the tell for what the instrument is for.** ORS 701.068(5): if the amount the licensee must pay against the bond exceeds the bond, "the board shall suspend the contractor's license". California does the same lever in the other direction: § 7071.6(d) lets the board require "a contractor's bond in twice the amount required pursuant to subdivision (a)" where the applicant was convicted of, or cited for, contracting without a licence and "the violation … constituted a substantial injury to the public." Prose, with the sections cited.
- **Arizona's residential bands are the sharpest contrast and must be quoted, not paraphrased.** § 32-1152(B)(5): general residential contractors "shall furnish a surety bond or cash deposit in an amount of not more than $15,000 and not less than $5,000"; (B)(7): specialty residential contractors "$7,500 and not less than $1,000"; (B)(1)(f) shows the same page's small-volume commercial bond as "five thousand dollars" in words. Bands, not points: say "may be as little as", never "is".
- **The insurance comparison is arithmetic the article performs visibly.** ORS 701.081(1) puts a $25,000 bond beside a $500,000 general liability floor — a bond worth one-twentieth of the insurance in the same subsection (25,000 ÷ 500,000 = 5 percent). Label it as the desk's arithmetic on two cited figures. Never quote a ratio as the statute's own sentence. ORS 701.081(2) and (3) carry the same pattern for specialty ($20,000 bond / $300,000 insurance) and limited ($15,000 / $100,000) contractors if the body wants a second rung.
- **What the bond covers is not what insurance covers.** RCW 18.27.040(1) conditions the bond on paying "all persons performing labor, including employee benefits", state taxes, "all persons furnishing material or renting or supplying equipment", and "all amounts that may be adjudged against the contractor by reason of breach of contract including improper work" — that last clause is the one a homeowner uses, and it is a contract-damages remedy, not a property-damage policy. The article must not say the bond covers water damage, and must not say the general liability certificate covers defective work.
- **No hands-on claim, no price, no premium.** This desk has not bought a bond or filed a claim against one, and no bond premium is cited anywhere: a premium is a rate that would date the piece. The arithmetic is the ceiling against the reader's own contract number.
- **Why the article is not the lien piece.** The 2026-10-08 artifact covers a subcontractor's lien against title and the Notice of Completion; this one covers the owner's claim against a state-required surety instrument. Different claimant, different instrument, different deadline. Say the distinction once, plainly, so a reader who has read both is not confused.
