**Signal A (Anchor 1):** Console replacement prices went up after launch, in the vendors' own words. Sony's US list, read live and effective 2026-04-02: "U.S. PS5 – $649.99 PS5 Digital Edition – $599.99 PS5 Pro – $899.99" (https://blog.playstation.com/2026/03/27/new-price-changes-for-ps5-ps5-pro-and-playstation-portal-remote-player). Microsoft's update, posted 2026-06-25 and effective 2026-08-01: "The price of XBOX consoles will increase by US$100 for 512 GB models and US$150 for 1 TB models", because "console storage and memory prices have increased by more than 2.5x and we expect another doubling by the fall of 2027", on machines that are "typically not sold at a profit, but instead for less than they cost to make" (https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2026/06/25/xbox-console-price-update). Nintendo's own revision, posted 2026-05-07 and effective 2026-09-01: "from $449.99 to $499.99" (https://www.nintendo.com/us/whatsnew/price-revision-for-nintendo-switch-2-system).
**Signal B (Anchor 2):** The parts-and-resale side did not follow, and iFixit tabulated the gap on 2026-11-11: "Buy all three major current consoles today and you'll pay $500 more than you would have at launch", and "A complete-in-box New Nintendo 3DS XL now sells for more than $400. It cost $199.99 new in 2015, or about $285 in today's money. If yours breaks, Nintendo won't fix it: Nintendo Japan stopped repairing the 3DS in March 2025, when it ran out of parts" (https://www.ifixit.com/News/120222/gta-vi-took-13-years-your-console-should-last-at-least-that-long). The market record prices that same boxed handheld at $450.00 complete and $838.78 new, with two boxed sales logged 2026-10-19 at $430.00 and $440.00 (https://www.pricecharting.com/game/nintendo-3ds/new-nintendo-3ds-xl-black). And the fix itself is cheap where it exists: iFixit sells the DualSense joystick — the part that fails first — for $14.99 against a $649.99 console, while rating the job "Difficulty: Difficult" and "Time Required: 1 - 4 hours" (https://www.ifixit.com/Guide/DualSense+Joystick+Replacement/142488). iFixit's 2026-11-12 field report says why that does not settle the decision: at a Lyon workshop, would-be fixers "had the motivation, and they were gaining the knowledge. But replacement parts were simply too expensive for them", and "Even with new EU repair legislation, affordability remains a challenge" (https://www.ifixit.com/News/120266/what-gets-people-fixing).
**Emergent Collision Point:** The two anchors describe the same maths from opposite ends, and neither states the result. Microsoft's post prices the *replacement* side and concedes the raise is a cost pass-through on a machine already sold below cost; iFixit's post prices the *keeping* side and finds the cost of a part is what decides whether the machine lives. Put together, the repair-or-replace ratio has inverted without a single device ageing differently: the denominator rose by $100 to $300 a unit in eight months, while the numerator — a $14.99 stick, a pot of thermal paste, an hour of labour — did not move. That should be a windfall for repair. It is not, because the part is the one input in this chain whose price and supply the owner cannot shop: Nintendo ended 3DS repairs in March 2025 when it ran out of parts, and the boxed unit it will no longer service now clears $450 against a $199.99 launch price. The residual value the resale market is bidding up is a claim on a parts channel the vendor controls, and the vendor's schedule is set by cost, not by what the device is worth used. Neither document says this: one prices the box, the other prices the screwdriver, and the buyer is standing between them.
**Hook:** The three current consoles cost $500 more together than they did at launch — and the part that usually fails first still costs $14.99, if you can get it and if you can solder it.
**Tension:** Replacement inflation makes old hardware worth keeping, and the resale market has already repriced it: a 2015 handheld that Nintendo stopped servicing in March 2025 now sells for more than twice its launch price. So the asset appreciated and the service contract expired in the same period. Who this threatens: the "repair is the cheap option" story, which is true of the part and false of the access to it — a console with a $15 failure mode and no part is worth its scrap. Who it empowers: the owner who buys the part while it is still listed, and who treats the machine's service life as something to check at purchase rather than at failure.
**Target reader:** systems_tinkerer_pro
**Single claim to defend:** The repair-or-replace ratio on consumer hardware has inverted from the replacement side, not the repair side — so the decision now turns on whether the part exists, at what price and at what level of skill, and the vendor's parts schedule rather than the device's age is what sets the residual value of what you own.
