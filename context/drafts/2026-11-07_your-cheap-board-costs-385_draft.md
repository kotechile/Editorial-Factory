<!-- lead -->
Two prices landed in the same fortnight, in businesses with nothing in common. The 16GB Raspberry Pi 5, the cheap board a home server used to start with, costs $385 today after its maker ran short of the memory chips it needs [1]. A Spotify Premium Family member who cannot prove they live at the plan manager's address has seven days to fix it. Missing that window now costs the plan and brings a 12-month ban on joining another shared plan [2]. Neither move raises the price you were quoted at sign-up. Both put a number on the part of the deal that used to be left alone: the friend who was never quite in your household, and the memory you bought in case you needed it later.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

- **The shift:** Two vendors stopped absorbing the informal half of a deal and priced it.
- **The number:** $385, the 16GB Pi 5's price after an $80 rise.
- **The risk:** A failed household check locks a member out of shared plans for 12 months.

<!-- tension -->

## What was free is now invoiced

Spotify's household rule is old; the checking is what arrived this month. Family plans have always been sold "for up to 6 family members residing at the same address" [3]. On 15 October Goldman Sachs told clients that "a meaningful share of family plan users are not actual household members" [4]. The same note called tighter checking "additional monetization upside", because enforcement had been "minimal in practice" [4]. Spotify's help page now sets out the order: seven days from the email, a scan in person if the address does not check out, then a twelve-month bar on any other shared plan [2].

Raspberry Pi told the same story with memory instead of addresses. The board launched at $120 in January 2025 [9]. On 1 October the two-gigabyte models rose $12.50, to $67.50 for the Pi 4 and $77.50 for the Pi 5 [5]. On 2 November the same 16GB board rose another $80, and Eben Upton's post gives the reason plainly: no second source for 16GB memory, plus shortages of other parts [1]. The post holds the price of the all-in-one that carries the same memory, a 256GB drive and a keyboard [6]. It also tells buyers to check what they run before paying for headroom [1].

Neither company is doing anything exotic. A vendor can carry an informal discount while it stays small and quiet. It stops being able to carry it once an analyst writes down that the discount is large, or once a part inside the product gets scarce. What was free was never free; it was unpriced. My read is that pricing it lands on whoever was quietly relying on the gap.

A household of six on the family rate pays $21.99 a month, $3.67 a head [3]. A member who fails the check falls back to the $12.99 individual plan. Not everyone it catches is freeloading. The adult child at university, the partner who works away and the sibling sharing a flat are the cases Ars Technica raised when the prompts began [4]. The hardware side has the same shape. The buyer who paid for 16GB so as not to touch the box again absorbs the shortage, while the board that does the actual job moved $12.50.

## By the numbers

- **$307.50 — the high-density premium:** what fourteen extra gigabytes cost over the 2GB model (desk arithmetic).
- **12 months — the shared-plan lockout:** how long a member who fails the household check is barred from another Family or Duo plan [2].
- **$111.90 a year — the demotion penalty:** the gap between the $12.99 individual plan and a six-way split of the $21.99 family rate (desk arithmetic).

## Where the invoice lands next

Not every vendor is making this trade. Home Assistant shipped its largest release this week and folded the community store into the product [7]. Its notes state that "Everything in the Marketplace is free, and that will not change" [7]. That is the same fortnight and the opposite decision, which is the useful part: the pattern is a choice, not a law of the market.

Inside Raspberry Pi's own price list the gap is now visible. The bare board costs more than the all-in-one that carries the same memory, a drive and a keyboard [6]. A buyer at this price pays for the part that is scarce, not the part that is useful, and the vendor has said so in its own advice [1].

The memory market is set elsewhere. TrendForce expects memory contract prices to rise 10–15% this quarter, as suppliers favour server parts over consumer ones [8]. Upton calls the landscape "difficult but ultimately temporary" and promises to roll the increases back when memory costs fall [1]. The part I keep circling is that a household does not get to wait for that cycle. The check runs in days and the memory cycle runs in quarters, so the audit now carries both.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

Two vendors are not a trend, and what connects them is a pricing move, not a shared cause. Spotify did not raise its fee; it enforced a term that was always there, and the twelve-month limit applies to joining another shared plan, not to keeping an account. Raspberry Pi did not change its model; it passed on a parts cost the company says is temporary, and 16GB is a real capability when a box runs several virtual machines. Nor does the check catch only people bending the rule: a travelling spouse or a student living away will fail a test built for households. What I keep coming back to is the arithmetic this desk runs on [an espresso machine](/calculators/coffee-arbitrage/), where a bigger outlay up front meets a smaller cost each time, and the answer moves with how many cups you actually make. What changed this fortnight is that two inputs stopped being free to leave blank: how much you use, and whether you can prove where you live.
