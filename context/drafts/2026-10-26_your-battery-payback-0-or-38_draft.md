<!-- lead -->
Two homes on the same street, both buying a battery, can face a payback that differs by 38.1¢ for every unit of power the box shifts. San Diego Gas & Electric prints both prices on one page, under a plan it sells to homes with battery storage [1]. A household that buys its power from the utility pays 34.5¢ in the super off-peak hours, 38.9¢ off-peak, and 72.6¢ from 4 to 9 p.m. [1]. A household whose power comes from a community choice aggregator, and whose bill carries delivery charges only, pays 25.6¢ in all three periods [1]. The gap between its cheapest and dearest hour is 0¢. The battery and the roof are the same. The price gap the homeowner is buying is not.

## The big picture:

A battery does not earn a rate. It earns the difference between two hours, and whoever writes the bill sets that difference. In most of the country one company sells both the power and the wires, so it prices the two hours far apart on purpose. Where an aggregator supplies the power, the utility sells only the wires, and wires cost about the same at noon as at midnight.

Look at the second San Diego plan, the two-period one. A delivery-only customer sees 22.3¢ off-peak against 23.0¢ on-peak, a gap of 0.7¢ [1]. The bundled version of that same plan reads 31.0¢ against 58.9¢, a gap of 27.9¢ [1]. One plan, two contracts, and a spread forty times wider on one side of the page.

The rule is not uniform, which is the whole reason to read the schedule instead of the flyer. On the electric vehicle plan, super off-peak delivery drops to 4.7¢ while on-peak stays at 31.8¢, so even a delivery-only home gets 27.1¢ of room [1]. That plan rewards charging a car overnight, and the design survives the split between power and wires. What does not survive it is a schedule built on a flat wires charge.

A national average is no help at all. The Energy Information Administration puts the 2025 average home price at 17.30¢ per unit, with state averages running from 35.72¢ in Hawaii to 8.20¢ in North Dakota [3]. A payback model built on any average describes a house that is not this one.

The season matters as much as the map. Consolidated Edison charges $0.2786 from 8 a.m. to midnight between June and September, against $0.0522 off-peak [2]. In the other eight months the peak price is $0.1711, so the gap a battery harvests falls from 22.64¢ to 11.89¢ [2]. The same hardware earns about half as much for two thirds of the year.

My read: the useful question is no longer which battery. It is which bill. Every payback line in a quote is a bet about a schedule the salesperson has usually never seen.

## By the numbers

- **38.1¢ — Bundled on-peak gap:** the storage plan prices 34.5¢ in the super off-peak hours and 72.6¢ on-peak for a customer who buys power from the utility [1].
- **0¢ — Delivery-only gap:** the same plan charges a community-choice customer who buys delivery only 25.6¢ in every one of its three periods [1].
- **22.64¢ — Con Edison summer gap:** the $0.2786 peak price against $0.0522 off-peak, narrowing to 11.89¢ outside June through September [2].
- **6.09¢ — Payback threshold:** with 5.0 kWh of usable storage at 90% round-trip efficiency, each cent of gap is worth $16.43 a year, so $1,000 of installed cost needs 6.09¢ to come back inside ten years [4].

## What I'd watch:

- **The two prices on the household's own schedule.** Both utilities print them by period, and the work is one subtraction. At a 0.7¢ gap, a 5 kWh battery that cycles daily collects $11.50 a year before any loss [1][4].
- **Whose name is on the supply line.** San Diego says it plainly: a community choice aggregator (CCA) customer "must also pay for electric generation at prices determined by the CCA," and points the reader to the aggregator for those prices [1]. The utility publishes half the gap. The other half is a second schedule that has to be fetched on its own.
- **The length of the peak window.** Con Edison's peak runs sixteen hours a day in summer [2]. A small battery can only shift the load it can cover inside that window, which is why one cycle a day is an assumption rather than a promise.
- **The fixed charges under the gap.** Con Edison's plan carries a $21.00 monthly customer charge on top of prices that leave out surcharges [2]. San Diego's prices leave out a base services charge that began in October 2025 [1]. The spread is measured on the energy prices, and the bill also carries the plumbing.
- **The loss term.** Enphase lists 90% round-trip efficiency for its 5 kWh home battery [4]. A tenth of the harvested gap is spent moving power in and out of the cells, which is why the threshold is not 5.5¢.

## The catch

A battery is two products on one invoice. The first is the spread trade, and its value is the gap. The second is backup power, and none of these numbers measure it. My read: a household that loses the grid twice a winter may be entirely rational to buy a battery that never pays back on the spread alone. That is the honest counterargument to everything above, and it is why this piece prices only the first product: that is the one the payback line claims to measure.

The figures are narrower than they look. Two utilities and four plans are not a national survey; they are the schedules I could fetch and read in full. The battery here is a 5 kWh unit, and a larger one earns more per cycle without changing the threshold per dollar.

The rates move, too. San Diego's numbers took effect August 1, 2026, and Con Edison splits summer from the other eight months [1][2]. A reader in two years will find different cents in the same columns and the same subtraction to do.

What does not move is the structure. Power and wires are two bills in a growing number of states, the battery harvests the gap on whichever schedule applies, and a flat wires charge leaves almost nothing to harvest. That is the part worth carrying into a quote.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The payback on a home battery is set by the gap between a household's on-peak and off-peak prices, and that gap now differs sharply between a bundled utility customer and a community-choice customer who buys only delivery. On one San Diego plan the same hardware faces a 38.1¢ gap or a 0¢ gap, depending on which column of the tariff applies.
- **Why It Matters:** A quote carries a payback number that assumes a spread, and the spread belongs to the retail contract rather than to the equipment. Households comparing quotes across a block, a utility line or a plan change are often comparing two different products while looking at one box.
- **What I'd Watch:** I am watching how these schedules get read, because the gap decides the return far more than the hardware does.
  - **The subtraction:** take the on-peak and off-peak prices off your own schedule, subtract one from the other, and hold the result against the quote.
  - **The supply line:** if an aggregator supplies the power, the utility's table is delivery only and the aggregator's own schedule is the other half.
  - **The season:** a summer gap is not a winter gap, and the yearly figure is the one that repays capital.
- **The Catch:** The spread trade is only one of the two things a battery sells, and backup power has real value that no gap measures. Two utilities are not a national survey, the rates are dated, and a small battery cycling once a day is an assumption about the household's peak load.
