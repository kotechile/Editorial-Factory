The national mechanic's lien index fell to 47 in the third quarter, matching its 2022 low, after remodeling spending dropped 13.0 percent in a single month [4][2]. Fewer liens is supposed to mean the trades are getting paid. The small-business failures point the other way: Chapter 11 elections for firms of that size ran 46 percent more than in the same stretch of 2025, and the ones filing are the size of the contractors bidding your kitchen [5].

<!-- tension -->

## Why a falling lien count lies

A mechanic's lien is the claim a supplier or subcontractor records against a property when the work it furnished goes unpaid, and the clock that decides how long it survives is [the one form most homeowners never file](/mechanics-lien-clock-payment-release/). The construction credit agency that compiles the measure counts those recordings across the country and publishes the result as an index, where 50 is neutral. It fell to 47 from a revised 52 in the second quarter and 56 a year earlier, the third straight step down from 58 in the first quarter of 2026 [4].

What I keep circling is a caveat the index's own publisher prints and almost nobody quotes. The same page that reports the drop says a lower score "may reflect improved payment conditions, fewer projects reaching lien-triggering stages, or both" [4]. The index cannot tell those two stories apart, and the people who compile it say so in as many words. Something else has to decide which one is happening, and the spending data does.

Home building ran at $848.8 billion in September, down 5.5 percent from August [1]. The remodeling line inside that total fell at the sharpest monthly rate the series has posted this year [2]. When fewer jobs start, fewer reach the end where a lien is the last resort, so the count falls for a reason that has nothing to do with getting paid on time. The regions with the biggest drops fit that pattern: the West at 38 and the Midwest at 32 sit at multi-year lows, while the South, where building has held up best, is the only region still above neutral at 60 [4].

## By the numbers

- **13.0% — Remodeling's one-month drop:** improvement spending in September, on top of an 18.0 percent decline over the year [2].
- **276 — September reorganizations:** Subchapter V elections in the month, 38 percent above September 2025, in a group the index counts at 2,442 for the year to date [5].
- **47 — Lien index in Q3:** ten percent below the prior quarter and 16 percent below a year ago, the weakest reading since 2022 [4].

## Where the money stops between draws

**My read starts with the calendar rather than the bid.** A remodel is paid for by the homeowner in stages: a deposit, then draws against work in place, then a final payment held back until sign-off. In a growing market a contractor carries a thin margin between draws because the next job is already signed. In a shrinking one, the deposit for your job becomes the working cash for the last one.

That is the mechanism behind a number that reads as unrelated. Subchapter V elections, the debt workout Congress built for small firms through Chapter 11, reached 2,442 in the first nine months of 2026, up from 1,672 [5]. The index's publisher describes that group as one that "can include subcontractors and suppliers who depend on getting paid on time", which is the closest the public data gets to naming the firms a homeowner actually hires [4].

State enforcement is not the backstop it appears to be. California's licensing board closed 39 stings and sweeps in a coordinated 15-state push and produced 73 actions [6]. Those catch people working without a license. They do not get a deposit back from a licensed firm that stops paying its subs.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

I could be wrong about the direction, and the index's own keepers are careful to leave the door open. Some of this really may be better payment: contractors holding data-center work have kept their crews busy, and a firm with a signed backlog has less reason to stretch a homeowner's deposit. The Harvard Joint Center for Housing Studies (JCHS) still expects annual remodeling spending near $525 billion through the third quarter of 2027, which is flat rather than fallen, and its projected 1.5 percent growth is positive [3].

The bankruptcy leg is the softer one, too. Epiq counts every small firm that elects Subchapter V, not contractors alone, and it publishes no building share. What it shows is a direction and a size across the economy, not a count of failed remodelers. The lien count and the bankruptcy count are two gauges reading the same stress from opposite ends of a job.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** Lien filings fell to a four-year low while remodeling spending fell 13.0 percent in a month and small-business reorganizations rose 46 percent.
**The number:** 47, the lien index reading, ten percent below the prior quarter.
**The risk:** A low lien count reflects fewer jobs finishing, not getting paid on time, so a bidder's solvency matters more than its price.
