Two U.S. trade cases opened against Mexico on the same day. On September 30, 2026, the U.S. International Trade Commission (USITC) voted to keep a case alive against hydraulic cylinders from Mexico [1]. U.S. producers filed for duties on railway tank cars from Mexico alone [2]. The next morning, the Department of Justice (DOJ) named tariff evasion and forced labor as corporate enforcement priorities [3]. Neither is a tariff rate. Both now sit in the file of anyone who moved a factory to Mexico to escape one.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Mexico was the answer to the last four years of sourcing risk. It is close. It sits inside a free-trade agreement. A parts order reaches a U.S. plant in days, not weeks. The two September moves say the destination country is not the variable that removes trade exposure.

I have been reading these dockets all week. What stuck with me: the two instruments rest on different theories.

A trade case attaches to a good, defined by where it is made. The cylinder case covers five countries, Mexico among them. The USITC found a reasonable sign that a U.S. industry is hurt by imports "subsidized by the governments of China, India, and Mexico" [1].

A second case narrows to one product and one country: certain railway tank cars and parts from Mexico [2]. If it lands, it is not a bargaining position. It is a cash deposit taken at the border on every covered shipment. That deposit is estimated to start as early as December 2026 [5].

The enforcement file runs the other way. Directive 26-12 names no product and no country. It names a behaviour: tariff evasion, importation offences, forced labor. It makes that one of four corporate enforcement priorities [3].

It lists ten factors prosecutors must weigh heavily. One is a scheme that ran three years or more. Another is harm of $25 million or more. It also creates a new section, and every serious corporate investigation must be reported to it within seven days. Two days before the directive, U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) detained another category at the dock: two Withhold Release Orders on palm oil from two Indonesian producers [4].

## By the numbers

- **12.70% to 32.58% — Alleged margins:** what the tank-car petitioners claim on Mexican railway tank cars. These are claims, not rates Commerce has set [5].
- **Ten — Aggravating factors:** the list DOJ prosecutors must weigh heavily in corporate fraud cases [3].
- **Seven days — Reporting window:** the time Fraud Division prosecutors had to hand every ongoing corporate investigation to the new section [3].
- **60 — Enforced Withhold Release Orders:** the total CBP said it now oversees, after two new palm-oil orders took effect at all U.S. ports [4].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The people closest to this are not repricing freight. My read: the sourcing model itself is under pressure.

- **The deposit clock, not the rate:** importers of covered tank cars have a date to plan around. A preliminary countervailing decision is estimated for late December 2026. Orders already in the pipeline sit on the far side of it [5].
- **Country exposure inside the case:** Mexico appears on both the pricing side and the subsidy side of the cylinder case. "We are inside the free-trade agreement" is not an answer to a subsidy claim [1].
- **Records as the second liability:** the directive reaches the buyer's own paperwork. The test is whether an importer can name the plant, the producer and the labour trail behind a part [3][4].
- **Dual-sourcing cuts one risk and adds the other:** a second plant halves the disruption a buyer is hedging against. It also doubles the paperwork the enforcement file reaches.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

Neither case has produced a duty. The margins are what the petitioners claim, not what Commerce has found. A negative injury finding would end the tank-car case outright. The deposit dates can slip. The USITC's report on the cylinder case is not due until November 16, 2026 [1][5].

I could be wrong. But the asymmetry is the point. A rate is visible in the quote. It is capped by a schedule. It is priced in a day. A case and a compliance file are neither.

The cost lands later, on whichever firm kept the thinner record. In a supply chain built for speed, that is often the one that moved last.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Two U.S. trade cases opened against Mexico on September 30, 2026. The next day, the Justice Department made tariff evasion and forced labor enforcement priorities. Neither is a tariff rate.
- **Why It Matters:** A sourcing decision now carries two liabilities. One is a goods liability: a duty case tied to a product's country of origin. The other is a buyer liability: an enforcement file tied to the importer's records. A landed-cost model priced off rates sees neither.
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether deposit risk and the record-keeping standard start showing up in sourcing contracts.
  - **The deposit date:** A preliminary countervailing decision estimated for December 2026 that would start cash deposits on covered tank-car entries.
  - **The country-in-the-case test:** Whether a destination inside a free-trade agreement can still be named as a subsidizer, as Mexico is in the cylinder case.
  - **The record:** Whether the importer can name the plant, the producer and the labour trail behind a part.
- **The Catch:** No duty has been imposed. The margins are claims, and a negative finding ends the tank-car case. What is live is a schedule and a standard, not a bill.
