<!-- lead -->
On September 18, U.S. Customs and Border Protection gets a new power. CBP is now comprehensively reviewing every importer-of-record Form 5106 on file — not just new filings — and where the information is inaccurate or incomplete, it will void the IOR number, "rendering it invalid for any purpose, including entering imported merchandise into the United States" [1]. A stale physical address, a broker's office listed as the business location, a P.O. box: each is now a defect [4]. There is no grace period and no advance warning. The cargo simply stops at the port.

<!-- tension -->
Published August 19 in the Federal Register, the rule is the sharpest edge of something larger than one form [1]. Across the last thirty days, a stack of dated enforcement obligations has landed at once. Tariffs of up to 100% on imported drones took effect today, September 3 [5]. Canada's dollar-for-dollar, rate-for-rate counter-tariffs on $27.6 billion of U.S. goods hit September 8 [2]. And on December 4, a minimum-import-price regime kicks in for polysilicon and its derivatives, layered on top of a new 15% tariff [3].

Look at what those dates share. This is no longer a tariff headline. For two years, duties were announced, challenged, and renegotiated. Now the machinery is operational — aimed at importers' paperwork and port-level clearance, not just their bill of materials. The IOR rule is the clearest tell: it adds no duty at all. It weaponizes a form every importer has already filed.

The backstory is as concrete as the dates. The U.S. imposed a 50% tariff on $27.6 billion of Canadian goods effective August 22; Canada suspended negotiations and is now matching dollar-for-dollar, rate-for-rate [2]. The drone tariffs followed a Commerce Department national-security investigation opened last year, and most of them landed with no transition window [5]. Each is a decision that has finished the arguing phase and moved into the collection phase.

The people it hurts are easy to name. Mid-market importers and 3PLs that run compliance through a broker, and have not looked at their own IOR data in years, are exactly the ones whose 5106s still list a registered agent's address or a P.O. box [4]. The people it helps are the ones selling the fix — brokers, compliance software, and the domestic manufacturers these duties were written to protect [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
Three moves before September 18.

First, pull your Form 5106 and check the physical address. CBP's instructions require the address associated with the actual business or individual [1]. A registered agent, customs broker, freight forwarder, or P.O. box is now a defect [4]. If CBP voids your number, it notifies you in writing at the most recent email on file and copies the broker that last filed entry on your behalf — but reestablishment runs through a dedicated CBP program, and it is not instant [1].

Second, map where the new duties hit your landed cost. Drones and thermal-imaging components: up to 100% [5]. Canadian-sourced steel and aluminum that previously carried 25% now carry 50%, alongside furniture and apparel; appliances, dairy, and certain steel and aluminum derivative products carry 25% [2]. Polysilicon-adjacent solar inputs carry minimum import prices — $21 per kilogram for raw polysilicon, $100 per kilogram for ingots and wafers — while ingots and downstream derivatives face an additional 15% tariff [3].

Third, put the dates on a calendar with a named owner: September 8 (Canada), September 18 (IOR), December 4 (polysilicon). One owner, one list, one place. If a container is already at the port when a number gets voided, it does not wait for the paperwork to catch up [1].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
Two caveats keep this from being apocalyptic. A voided IOR number can be reestablished, and the notice is built to force a cleanup rather than an ambush — CBP writes to you first, at the address and email you gave it [1]. The tariff wave is also full of carve-outs. Drones get reduced rates from allied countries including the EU, Japan, South Korea, Switzerland, Taiwan, and the U.K. [5]. Polysilicon gets country-specific treatment, and the 15% tariff applies to ingots and downstream derivatives rather than raw polysilicon [3]. The risk is real and dated; it is not the end of trade. But "the broker handles it" is no longer a defensible answer to a form that can stop your cargo.
