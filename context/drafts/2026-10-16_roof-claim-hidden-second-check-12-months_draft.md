<!-- lead -->
Your roof claim's hidden second check is the one many owners never collect. A replacement-cost policy pays the worn value first. The Hartford's own guide to this step pays $750 for an item that costs $900 to replace today. It then releases the other $150 only after a receipt shows up [1]. California puts the same order in law. The insurer pays actual cash value until the damaged property is fixed or replaced. Then it pays the rest [2]. The clock on that rest runs 12 months at the least, counted from the first payment. A declared emergency lifts it to 36 months [2].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The roof is where this bites. A roof is the one part of a house that shows up with a wear schedule attached.

Wind and hail caused the most home losses in 2023. They made up 42.5 percent of them [6]. The average claim paid that year was $20,062, up from $13,884 in 2019 [6]. One state regulator puts the average roof replacement at $10,000 [4].

Here is the part I keep circling. The first check is not a stall. It is the policy working as written. The second check is the one that waits on papers the owner supplies. Those papers can only exist after the work is done.

Two states now write that order into law, instead of leaving it to the contract. Florida requires the first payment on a replacement-cost home loss to be at least the actual cash value, less the deductible [3]. California spells out the rest of the order, and the clock that governs it [2].

## By the numbers

- **$750 of $900 — First check:** The Hartford's own guide pays the worn value first, then releases $150 once the receipt arrives [1].
- **12 months — Recovery floor:** California bans any shorter limit on collecting the rest, counted from the first actual cash value payment. A declared emergency raises it to 36 months, and proven delays add six months at a time [2].
- **$20,062 — Average claim paid:** The typical home claim in 2023, against $13,884 in 2019. Wind and hail were the largest cause, at 42.5 percent [6].
- **2 percent or 50 percent — Roof-deductible cap:** A separate roof deductible cannot top the lesser of 2 percent of the Coverage A limit or half the cost to replace the roof. It applies only to claims paid at replacement cost [5].

## What I'd watch:

I have been reading settlement rules this week instead of rate filings. The money sits in the papers.

- **The rider, not the headline cover:** The Iowa Insurance Division warns that many policies now carry a rider built for roof claims. That rider can swap replacement-cost cover for actual cash value [4]. One line decides whether a second check exists at all.
- **Two prices for one scope:** A replacement-cost scope carries the worn figure and the full figure. The gap between them is the money in play [1][2].
- **The proof a carrier takes:** Florida lists a canceled check, a money order receipt, a card statement, or a signed payment plan as reasonable proof that the deductible was paid [5].
- **The 15-year line at renewal:** An insurer cannot refuse to renew a policy just because a roof is under 15 years old. For an older roof, the owner may get an approved inspection first, at their own cost, before the carrier can demand a new roof [3].
- **The clock, and what moves it:** After a declared emergency, proof of loss cannot be demanded before 100 days have passed. Proven delays push the recovery date out further [2].

## The catch

My read is that this setup is less predatory than it looks. It is also harder to work than it sounds.

The holdback is not a penalty. Under a replacement-cost policy the carrier owes the gap between worn value and full value. It just will not pay that gap before the repair exists [1][2]. Nothing is taken away. The money sits behind an event the owner controls.

The 12 months is a floor, not a trap. It is the shortest limit the law allows. A policy can grant longer. An emergency loss lifts it to 36 months, and an insurer must add six months at a time for delays it cannot pin on the owner [2].

The catch is the map. Two states spell out the clock in law. Elsewhere these answers live in the policy wording, and I would not assume a carrier reads its own rider the way an owner does. The regulator's $10,000 roof figure also sits undated on the page. I treat it as a scale marker, not a 2026 price [4].

And the second check can be designed away before any claim happens. Florida pairs its roof deductible with two rules: the deductible applies only to claims paid at replacement cost, and never to a total loss or to repairs touching less than half the roof [5]. The cover that saves money and the cover that pays out are two products on one page.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A replacement-cost roof claim is paid twice. The first payment is the worn value, and the rest is released only after the repair is proven, inside a window the policy has to grant.
- **Why It Matters:** Wind and hail are the largest cause of home losses, the average claim paid topped $20,000 in 2023, and the second check is often lost by owners who take the first one and close the file.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The roof rider:** A clause that swaps replacement-cost cover for actual cash value decides whether a second payment exists at all [4].
  - **The recovery clock:** Whether the policy states the window in writing, since California's floor is 12 months, 36 after an emergency, plus more for proven delays [2].
  - **The 15-year line:** Roof age at renewal, and whether the inspection route is used before a carrier demands a new roof [3].
- **The Catch:** The rules in law bind two states while other policies answer the same question in contract wording, and the average roof figure a regulator cites carries no date [4].
