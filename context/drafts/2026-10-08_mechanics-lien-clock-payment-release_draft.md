A California homeowner has 15 days after a remodel ends to record one form, a Notice of Completion [1]. File it, and a subcontractor's window to lien the house drops from 90 days to 30 [1]. Miss it, and a supplier the owner never hired keeps a 90-day clock running after the last check clears [2].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Homeowners treat a remodeling contract as the whole story: sign it, pay it, done. The lien rules sit in state law. They run behind the contract, not inside it. A subcontractor or a supplier who is not paid by the general contractor can file a claim on the house itself, even when the owner paid the general contractor in full.

The clock is set by statute, and it varies by state. California gives a claimant 90 days from completion [2]. Arizona gives 120 days, or 60 with a completion notice on file [4]. Utah makes a subcontractor or supplier file a preliminary notice within 20 days of starting work, or lose the right to lien [3].

The one lever a homeowner holds looks the same almost everywhere: file the notice, and the window shrinks. California trims a subcontractor's time from 90 days to 30, and a prime contractor's from 90 to 60 [1].

What strikes me is that the owner's protection is not the size of the payment. It is not the wording of the contract either. It is two paper acts: record the completion notice on time, and hold the next payment until each claimant signs a release.

## By the numbers

- **15 days — The owner's deadline:** A California homeowner has 15 days from completion to record a Notice of Completion [1]. That filing cuts a subcontractor's or supplier's lien window from 90 days to 30 [1].
- **90 days — The default clock:** With no notice on file, a claimant must record the lien within 90 days of completion, acceptance, or first use [2]. A suit to enforce it must follow within 90 days of recording [2].
- **120 days — Arizona's longer window:** Arizona allows 120 days to record a notice and claim of lien after completion [4]. That falls to 60 once a completion notice is recorded [4].
- **20 days — The early notice:** Utah makes a subcontractor or supplier file a preliminary notice within 20 days of starting work to keep the right to lien [3]. California runs the same 20-day window [2].

## What I'd watch:

The people who handle this daily watch two dates, not the contract price. Title officers, construction lawyers, and the better general contractors all live by these. Here is where I am looking next.

- **The completion notice, filed early.** Recording it is the cheapest defensive act a homeowner has [1]. California keeps the timing tight at 15 days [1]. I keep coming back to how many owners skip it because nobody told them it was theirs to file.
- **A release at each payment, not just the last.** A conditional release protects the owner until the check clears [1]. An unconditional release means the payment is made and the claim is gone [1]. California's licensing board says the owner may hold the next payment until the prior releases are in hand [1].
- **The fund behind the contractor.** Maryland caps recovery from its Home Improvement Guaranty Fund at $30,000 per claim [5]. One contractor's total is capped at $250,000 [5]. New York City pays up to $20,000 through a simple claim process, and up to $40,000 through the full one [6]. Both ceilings sit below a typical roof or kitchen job, so the fund is a backstop, not a plan.
- **Who is even allowed to lien.** Laborers and the direct contractor usually skip the early notice [2]. Subcontractors and suppliers do not [2]. The set of people who can cloud a title is wider than the set the owner hired.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that the rules cut both ways, and that is the part worth sitting with. The same 15-day filing that protects a homeowner also exists so a diligent supplier gets paid. Shorten the clock too hard, and builders grow wary of small jobs [1][4].

The day-counts vary, and they stay in motion. Arizona's 120 days is not California's 90 [1][4]. The money in a state fund is set by the local statute, not by a national number [5][6]. A homeowner in one state cannot copy a number from another.

There is also a gap between what the law allows and what most owners do. Holding the next payment until releases arrive is legal [1]. Yet most people pay the last invoice in full and hear about the lien months later. That is how a routine roof turns into a five-month, four-figure cleanup.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** On a home remodel, the money already paid does not end the risk. A subcontractor or supplier unpaid by the general contractor can still lien the house, and the deadline runs on a state clock the owner can shorten [1][2].
- **Why It Matters:** The owner holds two controls, both cheap and both timed. Record the completion notice (15 days in California) to cut a subcontractor's lien window from 90 days to 30, and release each payment only against a signed lien release [1][2]. Skip either, and a stranger's unpaid invoice lands on the title.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The completion notice:** Whether owners file it inside the 15-day window, since it is theirs to file and nobody else's [1].
  - **Releases at every payment:** Whether each check goes out against a conditional release, and whether the next payment waits on the prior ones [1].
  - **State funds:** What a guaranty fund actually pays — $30,000 per claim in Maryland, up to $20,000 in New York City — against a job that usually costs more [5][6].
- **The Catch:** The day-counts differ by state (Arizona's 120 days is not California's 90), the fund caps are set locally and sit below a typical remodel, and shortening the clock too far pushes builders away from small jobs [1][4][5][6].
