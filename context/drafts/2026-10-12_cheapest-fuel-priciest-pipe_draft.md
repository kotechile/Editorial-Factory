<!-- lead -->

Natural gas is the cheapest way to heat a home in the United States this winter: about $640 for the season, down 9% from last year. Heating oil will cost the average household $2,115, up 21%. In the same month, California passed a law that pays homeowners to stop using gas. The pipe that carries it runs $20,000 to $30,000 a house to replace. The fuel is cheap. The connection is not. [1] [2]

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The winter bill now sorts by fuel, not by use, and the pipe under the lawn decides which row a household lands in.

The Energy Information Administration (EIA) published its 2026–27 Winter Fuels Outlook on October 6. About half of all households heat mainly with gas or propane. They will spend less than last winter. The other side of the ledger is harsher. Electricity works out at $1,196, up 4%. Heating oil lands at $2,115, up 21%. Its price of $5.26 a gallon runs 34% above last winter. [3] [4]

That is a spread of roughly $1,475 between two households in the same national weather. The EIA expects temperatures about the same as last winter overall, with a milder Northeast and a colder West. [3]

Heating oil is a small club: about 3% of homes, mostly in the Northeast. It is also the group with the least room to move. East Coast distillate stocks sat 32% below their five-year seasonal average in September. Forecasters expect them to stay 20% to 30% below average all winter. [1] [4]

I keep coming back to what that spread really prices. A household cannot change its fuel inside one season. The bill is set by equipment someone installed years ago, and by a pipe the owner may never have thought about.

California has just made that pipe a line item. The Home Energy Choice Act, signed September 30, requires a utility to offer a household a choice when a gas service line is due for replacement: take the new line, or take cash to electrify instead. The payment is designed to be smaller than the cost of the line. The gas customers who stay come out ahead. [10] [2]

The math is public. The Natural Resources Defense Council (NRDC) reckons that giving 1,000 households $17,500 each to electrify would save the state's gas customers about $20 million, against replacing their lines. A large California gas utility replaces about 15,000 service lines a year. One line typically runs $20,000 to $30,000, billed to every gas customer over the life of the pipe. Nationally, utilities replace about 700,000 service lines a year and spend upward of $7 billion doing it. [2] [5]

## By the numbers

- **$640 vs $2,115 — the winter fuel spread:** the average gas-heated home against the average heating-oil home, with gas down 9% and oil up 21% [3].
- **$5.26 a gallon, up 34% — the oil squeeze:** heating-oil prices are climbing while East Coast stocks sit 32% below the five-year seasonal average [1] [3].
- **$20,000 to $30,000 — one gas line:** the typical replacement cost for a service line, paid by every gas customer, with about 15,000 replaced each year [2].
- **$17,500 × 1,000 — the buyout math:** NRDC's estimate of a $20 million saving if households take the cash instead of a new line [2].

## What I'd watch:

Three things decide whether this turns into a real household option or stays a line in a rate case.

- **The framework, not the law.** The California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) still has to write the incentive levels and the notice rules. Utility programs need approval by January 1, 2028. The law caps programs at 1% of a utility's customers and expires January 1, 2035. Notice is the open question: one clean-energy research group has recommended telling households one to five years ahead. [2] [5]
- **Whether the precedent scales.** Con Edison has run a similar exchange since 2024. Its own filing put the avoided cost near $28,000 for each household that electrifies instead of taking a new line. That avoided cost is what makes the model work, more than the size of the payment. [5]
- **The rest of the cost stack.** The federal credit for efficient home appliances, worth up to $2,000, closed to 2026 installs. Heat-pump shipments still held up through the first half of the year, and 2026 is tracking near the sector's best. Prices are moving the other way, with October increases of 3% to 6% and one supplier at 15%. Borrowing for a $20,000 decision is not cheap either: the average home equity loan sat at 8.46% on October 1, the prime rate is 7.00%, and the next rate decision lands October 27–28. [6] [7] [8] [9]

## The catch

The law is narrow, and the number that gets quoted is not the one it sets.

Most households will never see this letter. The program runs only where a pipe is already scheduled for replacement. It covers at most 1% of a utility's customers, has no written framework yet, and sunsets in 2035. The $17,500 figure is a nonprofit's example, not an amount anyone has been promised. [2]

The cost shift the utilities objected to is real, too. San Diego Gas & Electric and Southern California Gas argued that as customers leave, the ones who stay carry more of the system. The law answers that by holding the payment below the avoided replacement cost. That shares the saving rather than removing it. It works while pipes are being replaced. It says less about the bills still standing in 2040. [2]

I would keep the fuel ranking in its frame as well. This is a one-winter forecast. The EIA warns that comparing fuels is a rough guide. Housing stock, equipment and other end uses differ enough that a cheaper fuel does not guarantee a cheaper bill. [3]

The part I keep circling is the default. Until this law, the pipe was replaced and the customer paid. Now a household can be paid to walk away, but only if it hears in time. [2]

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The winter heating bill now turns on which fuel a home is already connected to — about $640 for gas, $2,115 for heating oil — and California became the first state to pay homeowners to abandon the gas line that delivers it.
- **Why It Matters:** The connection, not the commodity, is the expensive part. A gas service line costs $20,000 to $30,000 to replace and is billed to every gas customer, which turns a fuel choice into a capital decision.
- **What I'd Watch:** how the framework gets written, and whether the math travels to other states.
  - **Ratepayer alternative:** a program that pays a household to electrify instead of rebuilding its gas line, where the payment is smaller than the avoided cost.
  - **Avoided cost:** what a utility does not spend when a customer leaves, which is what makes the payment cheaper than the pipe.
  - **Notice window:** how far ahead a household learns its line is up for replacement — the difference between a choice and a deadline.
- **The Catch:** the program triggers only where a pipe is already due for replacement, is capped at 1% of a utility's customers, has no written framework, and ends in 2035; the quoted $17,500 is an example, not an offer.
