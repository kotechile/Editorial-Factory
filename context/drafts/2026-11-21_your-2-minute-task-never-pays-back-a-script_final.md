A 2-minute weekly task pays back 104 minutes a year. A script that needs a fix once a quarter burns 120 minutes of upkeep alone. The script does not break even. It loses year after year while looking exactly like progress.

This is not a corner case. The oldest measured breakdown of software work found that 48.0% of staff hours went to keeping old systems alive, not building new ones [2]. A home script inherits that shape with none of the budget.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

This article prices hobbyist automation for anyone tempted to script a small recurring task: upkeep, not build time, decides the trade, and the break-even bar sits at about 4.2 minutes a week over five years.

<!-- tension -->

## Upkeep is the real price

I have watched the upkeep line stay empty in every project plan I have ever written for myself. The hours you spend building a script are the cheap part. The hours it charges forever are what decide the trade. In the 1978 survey of 69 data processing shops, keeping and improving old systems took 48.0% of annual staff hours [2]. The measured share of that work was perfective — 60.3%, small improvements to what already exists [2]. Your script wants the same attention. It just bills you on weekends.

Now run the numbers. A 2-minute weekly task returns 104 minutes a year. A script that eats 30 minutes a quarter consumes 120. The upkeep alone outruns the task, so there is no payback date at all. Even a script that never needs a touch has to amortize an 8-hour build, which takes 240 weeks — about 4.6 years — before a 2-minute weekly task repays it. Give the script a five-year life with quarterly upkeep, and the task must be worth at least 4.2 minutes a week to cover the build and the fixes.

## By the numbers

- A 2-minute weekly task returns 104 minutes a year, against 120 minutes of quarterly upkeep.
- The break-even bar is 4.2 minutes a week once an 8-hour build is spread over five years.
- Zapier Professional at $19.99 a month is $239.88 a year, or 5.0 hours at the 2025 median wage of $47.66 [1].

## The rule that settles it

Once the hours sit on one line, the call makes itself. Take the minutes you spend keeping the script alive each year, add the build spread over its life, divide by 52. If the task sits below that line, do it by hand and keep the weekend. If it sits above, automate — for scale or to remove a step you always fumble, not for fun.

The paid tier sits on the other side of the same line, priced in hours rather than dollars. $239.88 a year is what Zapier Pro costs at $19.99 a month [3]. That is 5.0 hours of your own time at the median wage [1]. A pipeline whose upkeep runs past 5 hours a year costs more than the subscription it replaced — the same move that priced the maintenance hour in [Your Standby Bill Hides 62 Watts](/your-standby-bill-hides-62-watts/).

What I keep noticing is how dull the verdicts are. Most small tasks deserve "hand." A few big ones deserve a script with no moving parts. Brittle chains across many services deserve the $19.99. Same rule, three inputs — that is my read, not a measurement.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## What the rule cannot carry

The rule prices time, and time is not the only reason people automate. A script that runs unwatched overnight buys reliability the minute count does not see — that is a limit of the model, not a new claim. The 48.0% figure is also fifty years old and describes big IT shops, not homelabs; I use it because no newer measured count says its shape is wrong [2].

The wage has its own limit. $47.66 is a national median for a paid role [1]; nobody pays a hobbyist for Saturday hours. So the rule prices the cost of time, not cash. That is the point. The hours come out of the same weekend the projects do, and a script that eats more of the year than the task it replaced is a hobby dressed as a win.
