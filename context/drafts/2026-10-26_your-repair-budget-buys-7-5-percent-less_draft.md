<!-- lead -->
Spending on home repairs is holding near $525 billion a year and rising 1.5 percent, while the materials behind those jobs are up 9.7 percent. That is a quiet squeeze on your repair budget's reach: it buys about 7.5 percent less work than it did a year ago.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Two agencies measured the same squeeze from opposite ends this month, and neither one divided its number by the other.

Harvard's housing research center published its remodel forecast on October 22. It sees spending growth of just 1.5 percent by the third quarter of 2027, down from 2.4 percent at the end of this year, with yearly spending moving from $524 billion to $527 billion across those four quarters [1]. The government's wholesale price report for September, out on October 15, priced the other end: goods that go into home building up 9.7 percent in a year, and copper wire and cable up 23 percent [2][3].

One counts dollars spent. The other prices what those dollars buy. What strikes me here is that nobody prints the quotient, and the quotient is the number a household lives inside.

The shipment counts tell the same story from a third side. Gas warm-air furnaces shipped 287,710 units in August, down 4.3 percent from a year earlier and 5 percent lower for the year so far, while home water heaters fell 1.9 percent on gas and 1.6 percent on electric [7]. Cooling equipment rose 11.4 percent in the same month, which is what a summer of fixes looks like beside a year of fewer new units [7].

The reason this lands now is that the fix is winning on price. Retail spending on building goods has been flat to negative and remodel permits have stalled, which is the shape of owners holding old gear together instead of resetting it [1].

## By the numbers

- **1.5% — Spending growth:** the October forecast for growth by the third quarter of 2027, down from 2.4 percent at the end of 2026 [1].
- **9.7% — Home-building goods:** wholesale prices for the goods in residential work, at 355.2 in September against 323.9 a year earlier [2].
- **23.0% — Copper wire and cable:** the metal that runs through panels, pipe and coils, at 485.5 against 394.7 [3].
- **7.5% — Fewer jobs per dollar:** spending growth of 1.5 percent divided by goods growth of 9.7 percent, this desk's math on the two rows above [1][2].

## What I'd watch:

The part I keep circling is the spread inside the basket. Steel mill products are up 27.6 percent and softwood lumber 14.2 percent, while plumbing fixtures and fittings are up 5.1 percent [4][5][6]. A wiring-and-coil job and a faucet swap do not carry the same exposure, and one "materials are expensive" quote flattens both into a single line.

- **The trade mix:** copper and steel sit in the trades a panel, a condenser or a service line touches, which are the jobs with the least room to swap in something cheaper [3][4].
- **The volume tell:** furnace and water-heater shipments are falling while cooling units climb, which reads as owners paying to keep a system alive rather than to reset its clock [7].
- **The forecast's own slope:** spending eases from 2.4 to 1.5 percent, but on the same 9.7 percent goods basis the 2.4 percent rate already works out to about 6.6 percent fewer jobs per dollar [1][2]. The squeeze is not arriving; it is building.
- **The labour line:** wholesale prices stop at the factory gate, so permits, truck rolls, labour and margin sit on top of that 9.7 percent and a real quote runs wider [2].
- **The next print:** the two series to re-read are the January remodel release and the monthly wholesale report. I'm watching whether permits stay flat while spending holds.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

A wholesale index is not a quote. It prices what mills and factories received, before a contractor adds labour, permits and margin, and the home-building basket includes new work, which is not the mix inside a retrofit [2].

The forecast is also a forecast. Every figure in the October release is a projection across the next four quarters, and its authors note that the Census Bureau revised monthly retail sales in September, which moved earlier estimates by less than one percent [1].

My read is that 7.5 percent is the right shape and the wrong detail. It is an average across a basket, not a price for one roof, and a household that put in a furnace two years into a fifteen-year life should ignore it, because early replacement throws away real service life. The number bites on an asset already near the end of its clock, where the choice is a price this year or a larger one after a failure.

That is also why the reserve matters more than the purchase. A replacement date is the one line in a household budget that can be set years ahead, and the math behind a [capital sinking fund](/capital-sinking-fund/) is just that future bill divided by the months between now and then [1].

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Spending on home repairs is set to grow 1.5 percent while the goods those jobs use are up 9.7 percent, so the same money buys about 7.5 percent less work each year.
- **Why It Matters:** The clock on a roof, a furnace or a water heater runs in years, not in budgets. Putting a job off does not make it go away; it lands later at the higher price, often as an emergency.
- **What I'd Watch:** Which part of the basket is moving.
  - **Copper-heavy trades:** wiring, pipe and coils, where the index is up 23 percent and swapping in a cheaper part is hard.
  - **Shipment counts:** falling furnace and water-heater units against rising cooling units show fixes crowding out new units.
  - **The forecast slope:** spending growth easing from 2.4 to 1.5 percent while goods prices hold near 10.
- **The Catch:** A wholesale index leaves out labour and permits, its basket includes new build, and the 7.5 percent is an average that means nothing for a young asset.
