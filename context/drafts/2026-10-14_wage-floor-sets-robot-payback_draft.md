New York will require $17.00 an hour in New York City, Long Island and Westchester County from 1 January 2027 [6]. The national median for stockers and order fillers — the job the same federal wage table also lists as order picker — is $17.95 an hour [3]. The legal floor and the market rate for the same work sit 95 cents apart, and most warehouse payback models are built on the second number.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

An automation case has one input that is published before anybody signs a purchase order, and that input is the wage the machine replaces. The federal government prints a median for each occupation every year, and state labor agencies print the legal floor for a dated window [1][5][6].

The floor is not one number. It is $7.25 an hour in the states that follow the federal rate, and Oregon's tiers run from $16.80 in the Portland metro area to $14.55 in its non-urban counties through June 2027 [5]. New York's $17.00 rate starts in January 2027 [6].

The same picking machine therefore lands on a different payback in each state, before a single hour of throughput is counted.

My read: the common error is not choosing the wrong robot. It is choosing the wrong wage. One blended warehouse pay figure hides the two facts that decide the case — which job the machine displaces, and which state it stands in.

The third input is the one nobody publishes. How much of the workforce turns over is a local measurement, and the closest thing to a national number is a survey of operators [7].

## By the numbers

- **$22.32 an hour — The priciest job on the floor:** Industrial truck and tractor operators, the forklift jobs the same table also calls lift-truck operators, earn a median of $46,420 a year [1]. Packers sit at the other end of the ladder at $17.44 an hour [4], so the two ends of a warehouse job list are about 28% apart.
- **$17.00 an hour — The highest legal floor:** New York's minimum for New York City, Long Island and Westchester County takes effect on 1 January 2027, with $16.00 in the rest of the state [6]. The 2027 inflation step was blocked: state law allows annual inflation increases, but it also lists conditions, called off-ramps, that can stop one [6].
- **$16.80 an hour — Portland metro's floor:** Oregon's rate for July 2026 through June 2027, against $15.55 in its standard counties and $14.55 in the non-urban ones, re-set every July 1 as inflation allows [5]. The federal floor on the same page is still $7.25 [5].
- **55% of operations — Turnover at 15% or less:** That is the share of surveyed operations reporting annualized employee turnover at or under 15%, with 29% between 16% and 30% [7]. During peak periods, 30% or more of the same survey's workforce is temporary [7].

## What I'd watch:

What I am watching next is what operators do with the published tables once they have them.

- **The order of the roadmap:** The wage table already sets a sequence. Displacing a $22.32 truck-operator hour saves more than displacing a $17.44 packing hour, and the two picker roles sit between them at $19.35 for material handlers and $17.95 for order pickers [1][2][3][4]. I want to see whether retrofits keep going to whichever aisle is easiest to fence off.
- **The state-by-state page:** One blended wage cannot survive a $7.25 floor and a $16.80 floor inside the same network [5]. Multi-site operators are heading toward one payback page per state, and I would want to read the assumptions at the top of each.
- **The budget written in hours:** The survey's median projected capital and technology budget is $425,000, against an average of $2.16 million [7]. At the $19.35 hand-mover median [2], that median budget is about 22,000 hours — roughly ten years of one full-time picker at 2,080 hours a year. I would rather read a capital request in picker-years than in robot units.
- **The peak hour:** If 30% or more of a peak workforce is temporary [7], the hours automation is bought to cover are often the cheapest hours on the site. Whether a payback model counts those hours at the floor or at an agency rate is worth knowing before the number is signed.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The floor only binds the bottom of the pay band. Most buildings pay above it, and the medians in the federal table are national [1][3]. The floor is a guardrail on the cheapest hours, not the cost of the hour a machine actually replaces.

The floors also move at different speeds. New York's 2027 increase was stopped by one of the law's off-ramps, so a floor can sit still for a year [6]. Oregon re-sets each July 1 and prices its non-urban tier $2.25 below the Portland metro rate, while the federal floor has stayed at $7.25 [5].

The turnover figure is a distribution across many operations, not a forecast for one building. The extremes are real. A field case behind this desk has a Midwest distributor at 48% annual turnover while its starting wage went from $16.50 to $22.00 and its manual picking cost per unit rose 38% [8].

The part I keep circling: the floor is published and dated, the turnover number is neither, and both land in the same savings line.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The wage an automation case rests on is published twice — a median by occupation from the federal wage table, and a legal floor from each state on a dated schedule [1][5][6].
- **Why It Matters:** The floor runs from $7.25 to $17.00 an hour across states [5][6], and the warehouse job ladder runs from $17.44 to $22.32 [1][4]. So one blended wage overstates or understates the payback depending on where the building stands and which job the machine replaces.
- **What I'd Watch:** How operators turn the published wage tables into capital requests.
  - **The roadmap order:** Sequencing automation by the wage each job carries, rather than by which aisle is easiest to rebuild [1][4].
  - **The floor path:** Reading each state's dated rate window and its indexation rule, including the conditions that can pause an increase [5][6].
  - **The picker-year budget:** Expressing a capital request as the picker-hours it equals, so $425,000 reads as roughly ten years of one picker [2][7].
- **The Catch:** The floor binds only the cheapest hours and the medians are national, so the published tables set the range for a decision rather than the number inside it [1][5].
