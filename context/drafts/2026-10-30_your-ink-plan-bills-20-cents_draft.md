Hewlett-Packard (HP) bills the overage page, the one you print past your ink plan's cap, at $0.20, because the plan stops paying once you top its monthly count [1]. That is the same rate HP charges on the smallest plan it sells, and about 50 times what one bottle of black ink costs on a tank printer [1][2].

Six prices for one printed page sit on those two vendors' pages, and the printer on your desk sets none of them.

| What you buy | Sticker price | Pages included | Each page |
|---|---|---|---|
| Instant Ink, 10 pages a month | $1.99 | 10 | $0.199 |
| Instant Ink, 100 pages a month | $8.49 | 100 | $0.0849 |
| Instant Ink, 700 pages a month | $32.99 | 700 | $0.0471 |
| Pay As You Print, 20-page set | $5.49 | 20 | $0.2745 |
| Extra pages past a plan's cap | $2.00 | 10 to 15 | $0.133 to $0.20 |
| EcoTank 522 black bottle | $17.99 | 4,500 | $0.0040 |

Epson's EcoTank machine, the printer those bottles feed, lists at $239.99, with a $50 promo that runs only to 31 October 2026 [2]. A tank flips the deal: you buy the machine once and the page is nearly free after that.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

My read starts with what an ink plan really sells, because the monthly fee is not a price for ink.

Each tier is a page quota with a fee attached, so the rate per page falls as the quota rises. The 700-page tier charges less than a quarter of what the 10-page tier charges for the same page, and the yearly plan's largest quota works out to $0.0424 [1].

The overage is where the design shows. Past the cap, pages come in sets of ten to fifteen for $2, which puts the extra page at $0.13 to $0.20, the smallest plan's rate charged to a customer who may be on the largest [1].

The same logic runs the other way at the bottom of the table. Buying ink with no plan at all, in 20- or 75-page sets, costs more per page than any tier HP sells, so the option sold as freedom is the dearest way to print [1].

A home whose printing spikes carries all the risk, while HP gets a forecast it can bill against and a printer that reports ink levels back [1].

## By the numbers

- **$0.0112 — One full-colour page on a tank:** a black page plus three colour inks, each bottle at list price, comes to about a cent, 3.8 times under the cheapest ink-plan page [1][2].
- **$0.0424 — The cheapest page HP sells:** the 8,400-page yearly tier at $355.99 works out under a nickel, and it is still nearly four times the tank's colour page [1][3].
- **$0.0425 — What a bigger tier costs:** moving from the 100-page to the 300-page tier buys 200 more pages for $8.50, which is 3.1 to 4.7 times cheaper per page than an overage set [1].
- **$239.99 — The machine that ends the meter:** at 75 pages a month the tank's three-year total is $239.99 against $305.64 in fees, and at 300 pages a month the gap widens to $284.34 [1][2].

## What I'd watch:

I watch three things, because each one moves the yearly total more than the plan's own rate does.

- **The quota you forecast:** a home printing 120 pages a month on the 100-page tier pays overage on 20 pages every month, and the step up to the 300-page tier costs about a fifth as much per page as those overage sets [1].
- **The rollover ceiling:** unused pages bank at three times the monthly allowance, 150 pages on the 50-page plan, and the yearly plan banks nothing at all [1]. A quota bought for a busy quarter does not carry over a quiet one.
- **The ink in the box:** Epson's two-year claim rests on 125 pages a month, so the bottles inside the box cover about 3,000 pages before the first purchase [2]. A heavy printer hits the bottle sooner than the calendar suggests.

## The catch

The part I keep circling is the tank's cent-a-page, because it comes from a standard text pattern run under the maker's own method, not a count of anyone's real documents. Epson's note says yields fall when printing is infrequent or leans on one colour, so a photo-heavy month drinks colour bottles far faster than a text month [2][3].

The comparison also assumes a purchase. An owner who already has an eligible HP printer holds that capital, and for that reader the fair contest is the plan against the pay-as-you-print sets, which the plan wins at every tier [1].

Neither figure carries paper, and neither carries the failure that ends most inkjet lives: a printhead that dries out in a machine nobody uses. Epson's advice to print often is not a marketing line but the condition under which its own yield figure holds [3].

For the same sum run against a habit instead of a quota, the desk's [cost-per-cup calculator](/calculators/coffee-arbitrage/) prices one machine against a weekly routine.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** A printed page's price is now set by the page count you commit to, not by the printer you own.
**The number:** $0.20 a page past the cap, against $0.0424 on the largest yearly plan and about a cent on a tank.
**The risk:** Those rates come from a standard text pattern, so infrequent or photo-heavy printing costs more on either machine.
