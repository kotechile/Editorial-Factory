A homeowner in our field notes was quoted $11,200 and a seven-month permit wait to take the house from 200 amps to 400 [6]. A smart subpanel cleared the same hold-up for $4,200 [6]. The gap between those two numbers is not a discount. It is the difference between sizing a house on the worst case and sizing it on what the house really draws.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Every "you need more amps" verdict starts with the math, not with a reading. Codes say a home's calculated load must stay under about 80 percent of the panel's rating [1]. Cross that line and the standard answer is a bigger service.

That math assumes the worst case. The dryer, the range, the air conditioner and the car charger all get counted as if they run at the same moment. Codes work that way on purpose, because the cost of being wrong is a fire. The odd part is that the same math decides who pays $8,000 to $10,000 for a new panel and who does not.

The load most owners are adding right now is smaller than the fear suggests. A typical home charger of the 240-volt kind, wired to its own circuit, pulls 30 amps and delivers 7.2 kilowatts [2]. Against a 200-amp service that is a rounding error on paper, and mostly a scheduling problem in practice.

I keep coming back to how much of this decision is paperwork. The device does the work. The calculation decides whether you are allowed to buy it.

## By the numbers

- **80% — The line that decides it:** A home's calculated load has to stay under about 80 percent of its panel capacity. Cross it and the standard answer is a service upgrade [1].
- **$8K-$10K — A panel swap, fitted:** That is the all-in price of trading a standard panel for a smart one, against roughly $1,000 per controlled circuit for load management on the panel you own [1].
- **7.2 kW — The charger you add:** A typical home Level 2 unit runs 30 amps on a 40-amp circuit, and the same class spans 2.9 to 19.2 kilowatts by model [2].
- **$199.99 — The reading you need:** A mains plus 16-branch monitor bundle lists at $199.99, sampling 24,000 times a second at plus or minus 2 percent accuracy [4].

## What I'd watch:

The hardware is not the interesting part. The open question is who holds the number when the electrician's math and the owner's meter disagree.

- **The room already on the books:** Emporia's own worked example has a house drawing 80 amps of a 100-amp service, which leaves about 20 amps for a car charger [3]. The charger slows down. It does not fail to exist.
- **The rate a fixed install starts at:** Set once on the worst case, a charger is "often limited to 24-40A (5.7-9.6 kW)". A charger tied to a managing panel holds up to 48 amps, or 11.5 kilowatts, and eases off when the house needs the power [5].
- **The question worth asking:** Whether the hardware is a listed energy-management system. The industry explainer says inspectors accept those when they are properly listed and installed, and that listing is what lets a managed load be discounted in the math at all [1].
- **The services that already work:** Homes can go fully electric on services as small as 150 amps once real usage patterns and load management are counted [1]. An upgrade still earns its place when the service is unsafe or worn out, or when the big loads really do run together most days [1].

What I'm watching next is the sequencing. A load manager and a meter cost less than the quote does, and their readings are the only evidence in the room that is not a worst-case guess.

## The catch

The 80 percent figure is a code rule, not a reading of your house. The pages that state it explain the rule. They do not test homes against it [1]. My read: it is the gate to clear, never proof of what a service can carry.

The prices need the same care. The $8,000-to-$10,000 panel figure, the $1,000-per-circuit estimate and the $780 device price all come from a webinar run by the firm that sells the load manager. So they are list prices and vendor answers, not market averages [1]. The manager's job is also narrower than the pitch. It sheds or slows the load you put behind it, which is fine for a charger that fills overnight and annoying if your peak overlap happens every day.

Two more limits sit in the hardware. A subpanel adds breaker slots, not ampacity (the amps a service can carry), so it does not make a service bigger [1]. And the meter that sizes the job is a consumer monitor at plus or minus 2 percent, not a utility revenue meter [4]. That is good enough to settle a decision. It is not good enough to argue with a utility.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A "you need a bigger panel" verdict comes from a worst-case calculation. Codes only send you to an upgrade when the calculated load crosses about 80 percent of panel capacity [1].
- **Why It Matters:** The gap is five figures. A smart-panel swap runs about $8,000 to $10,000 fitted. A load manager that controls the same circuits lists at $780, and a mains-plus-16-branch monitor at $199.99 [1][4].
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether owners bring their own peak reading to the quote talk, or accept the worst case.
  - **The 80 percent line:** The code threshold a load calculation has to clear before an upgrade becomes the standard answer [1].
  - **The 100-amp example:** A house drawing 80 amps of a 100-amp service leaves about 20 amps of room, enough for a charger at a lower rate [3].
  - **The listing question:** Whether the load manager is a listed energy-management system, since that listing is what inspectors accept and what lets a managed load be discounted [1].
- **The Catch:** The threshold is code math, not a measured result. The prices are vendor list prices. A subpanel adds slots, not ampacity. And the monitor you would size with is a consumer device at plus or minus 2 percent [1][4].
