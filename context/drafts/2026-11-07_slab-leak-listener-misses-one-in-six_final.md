The meter is still turning late at night with every tap and appliance off, and the bill has climbed for two months because water is leaking under the slab. That is when a homeowner is asked to pay for someone to walk the floor with headphones and name the spot to cut. The listening test is real work, but in the one published test of the method an acoustic listener missed about 15% of the small holes it was pointed at [2]. That blind spot is why the free meter check and a $100–$250 pressure test come first: they decide whether the listen has anything to hear [6].

## Why a quiet floor is not a clean pipe

Water escaping a crack in a pressurized pipe makes a low, hollow tone. That tone travels down the pipe wall and into the ground above it, and it is almost always present in pipes carrying 30 psi — pounds per square inch — or more [4]. Two things then decide whether an ear can pick it out. Water pooling around the leak site acts as an acoustic insulator, so a leak that has soaked the ground for weeks gets quieter rather than louder [4]. The tone also competes with sewer flow, traffic rolling over the pipe, and whatever else runs nearby [4].

My read starts with that physics, because it explains a result that looks contradictory. A 2023 experiment on a real pipeline ran 324 simulated leak tests. It caught 84.6% of 5 mm holes, 97.7% at 7 mm and 98% at 10 mm, with a 1.2% false-alarm rate — how often it flagged a leak that was not there [2]. That is an accurate instrument. It is also an instrument measured on a pipeline in a study of oil and gas transport, not on the narrow line under a house [2].

The public water benchmark makes the same point from the other side. Its reference dataset came from a laboratory rig of 152.4 mm polyvinyl chloride (PVC) pipe, 47 metres long, with a milled leak area of about 2 square millimetres [3]. The rig recorded 280 signals while traffic and tools ran in the background [3]. A six-inch main in a lab is not a half-inch line under a slab, and the smaller the pipe and the deeper the pour, the less of that tone reaches the surface.

## By the numbers

- **18,250 gallons a year:** What a steady household leak adds up to over twelve months [1].
- **84.6% correct:** Detection on the smallest leak hole the published pipeline test used [2].
- **$650 ceiling:** The top of a published guide range for a slab diagnostic, before any repair [5].

## The cheap tests come first

The cheapest test costs nothing except two hours of patience. Read the meter, use no water for two hours, then read it again; a dial that moved means water is escaping somewhere [1]. A family of four that burns more than 12,000 gallons in a winter month gets the same answer faster [1].

The second test is the one that tells a listener where to point. Pressure testing on the supply side runs $100 to $250 and confirms whether a pressurized line is losing water at all [6]. That distinction matters more than it sounds. A drain leak flows only while someone is using water, and the listening gear hunts a hiss that exists all the time. So a drain problem shows up under a drain-side hydrostatic test — filling the line with water to see if it holds — and shows up as nothing to a microphone at two in the afternoon.

Only then does the listening fee make sense, and the published guide prices for it disagree. One guide puts slab detection at $175 to $400, billed before the repair scope is set [6]. Another puts the same diagnostic at $350 to $650 and says a technician can localize a wall or slab leak inside a square foot in 20 to 40 minutes [5]. The spread is worth asking about, because the work is the same and the difference is how a shop prices a trip.

What I'd watch is the monitoring step, because it comes one step earlier. A flow monitor answers whether water is leaving at all, and the [threshold you set decides what it catches](/water-leak-monitor-rate-threshold/). That is a number a microphone never reports.

## The honest limit of a listening fee

My read, held loosely: the water is the cheap part of this decision, and the sales pitch always leads with the wrong number. The heaviest-decile household leak loses 50 gallons a day [1]. Even at a painful municipal rate that is a modest annual bill. Slab repair itself is published at $1,500 to $4,500, and the fee is 4% to 43% of that bill [5]. The cheap end is easy to defend, and the dear end needs a second quote.

No source publishes the share of residential slab locates that come back wrong, so that missing number is the honest limit of what a listening fee can promise. That published result belongs to a pipeline test, not a house call [2]. What the published work does support is narrower and more useful. A clean listen is not evidence of no leak, because the same practitioner source says outright that leaks do not always make noise and that pooling water can hide the one that does [4]. A quiet result is a reason to test the other system.
