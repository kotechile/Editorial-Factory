<!-- lead -->
The coil was giving up 11 degrees when a technician called the compressor dead and quoted $7,500 to replace it. The owner asked for a second look, which found a sticking expansion valve and a filter packed shut. Both were replaced for $180, and the same system then moved nineteen degrees. The number in the middle of that story is the split, the gap between the air going into the cooling coil and the air coming out. It is what separates a repair from a replacement, and it is often measured in a spot where it cannot work.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The trade has no single target for that gap. One training site puts a healthy cooling split between 14 and 23 degrees, calls the old fixed 20-degree rule "too simple to be a hard and fast rule", and says the spread comes from blower type and load [1]. A thermostat maker puts the same idea at 16 to 22 degrees and notes that the ideal range "varies depending on who you ask" [2]. An owner holding a report with one number on it is holding a reading with no agreed edge.

Both bands assume the probe sat across the coil. The training site is blunt about the shortcut that most reports use: "You can't get an accurate delta T from return and supply grilles because heat gains and losses occur in the ducts" [1]. Leaky ducts alone add 15-20% to the energy a system burns, even in a new house, which is about the size of the gap a register reading can carry [3].

Which way the number misses says more than the edge it misses by. A split above about 22 degrees points to air starvation: a dirty filter, a fouled coil, a blower wheel caked in dust, too few supply ducts, a return that is too small [2]. A split below about 16 degrees opens a list of eight suspects, and a bad compressor is sixth, behind low charge, a stuck metering device, too much air, wet air and a blocked liquid line [2]. My read is that the list is the whole story. The compressor is the priciest item on it, and the only one that turns a repair visit into a replacement quote.

## By the numbers

- **14 to 23 degrees — The split band:** That is the range a trade training site gives for cooling, with a warning that a fixed 20-degree rule is too simple because blower type and load move the number [1].
- **350 cfm per ton — Air before charge:** The inspection standard wants indoor coil airflow above 350 cubic feet per minute per ton before the superheat method can be used, and charge proved by superheat or subcooling [3].
- **15 percent — Low charge is common:** Field studies find both new and working systems low on refrigerant, often by about that much, and a unit can arrive that way on the day it is installed [5].
- **$72 to $144 — Cost of a 10–20% loss:** That is what a weaker system adds to a summer of cooling, priced against the 2024 average household bill of $719 [5].

## What I'd watch:

What I'm watching is the paper that comes with a verdict, because the numbers a technician writes down say more than the line under them. A split logged with the probe spots, an airflow figure and a charge reading turn a sales call into a diagnosis. One temperature on a work order does neither.

- **The coil-side number:** A split read before and after the coil, far enough out for the air to mix, is the only version that compares with either published band [1].
- **The airflow proof:** Charge cannot be judged until airflow clears 350 cfm per ton, which is the order the standard sets and the step a rushed call tends to skip [3].
- **The charge figure:** Superheat for a fixed metering device, subcooling for a thermostatic expansion valve, written on the ticket rather than said out loud [3].
- **The replacement floor:** A new split-system condenser made on or after January 1, 2023 has to clear 13.4 SEER2, and a heat pump 14.3 SEER2 with 7.5 HSPF2, so a quote is testable against the federal floor before the price is [4].
- **The wasted kilowatt-hour:** At the 2025 average home price of 17.30¢ per kilowatt-hour, a system a few points off its rated efficiency pays that gap back every month it runs [6].

## The catch

Wet air is the honest complication. When indoor air holds more moisture, the coil spends its capacity pulling that water out, and the split drops for a reason that has nothing to do with a fault [1]. One summer reading is a snapshot of one afternoon's load, which is why the thermostat maker sells logging the number over time rather than a single glance [2]. A split inside the band is not a clean bill of health either, since a mild day can hide a charge fault that a hot one would show.

Where I've landed is that the split is a triage tool, not a verdict. It narrows eight suspects to two or three and it makes a compressor recommendation answerable, but the case for a new compressor is a set of numbers read together: airflow, charge, and the split across the coil. The useful thing an owner can hold is not a target temperature. It is the demand that the reading came from the right place, and that the other two numbers exist at all. The tool the trade trusts for its own readings, [a thermal image that points rather than answers](/your-250-imager-needs-15-degrees/), makes much the same point about instruments.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The reading that decides between a repair and a compressor replacement is a temperature split taken across the coil, and the published bands differ enough that where the probe sat matters more than the number itself.
- **Why It Matters:** A compressor is the priciest part on the low-split suspect list, so a reading taken at a vent can turn a small metering repair into a four-figure quote without anyone lying.
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether a service ticket carries the three numbers a diagnosis needs, and how the federal efficiency floor shows up on a replacement quote.
  - **Temperature split:** The gap between the air going into the cooling coil and the air coming out, read at the coil rather than at a register.
  - **Superheat and subcooling:** The charge readings taken at the refrigerant lines, one for a fixed metering device and one for a valve.
  - **Airflow floor:** The least air volume per ton of cooling that has to be moving before a charge reading means anything.
- **The Catch:** Wet air lowers the split on its own, one reading catches one afternoon, and a number inside the band does not clear a system of a charge fault.
