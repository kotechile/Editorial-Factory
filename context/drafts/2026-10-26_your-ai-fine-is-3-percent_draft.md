<!-- lead -->
Take a European firm with €2 billion of sales that fails its duties as the operator of a high-risk AI system. Its fine ceiling under the EU AI Act is €60 million, four times the €15 million printed beside that duty in the text that sets it [1]. That is the 3% band, not the 35 million on the board slide. Each band is written as the higher of a euro amount or a share of worldwide sales [1].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

What strikes me here is that the tiers follow the duty, not the damage. The penalty section sets three of them. Banned uses under Article 5 cost €35 million or 7%. Duties on the firms that run these systems, including the deployer duties in Article 26 and Article 50's rules on telling people what they are dealing with, cost €15 million or 3%. And wrong or incomplete answers to a watchdog cost €7.5 million or 1% [1].

The clock is as load-bearing as the tiers. The penalty chapter has applied since 2 August 2025, and the bans since 2 February 2025, with two more arriving on 2 December 2026 [2]. The high-risk duties that generate most of a governance program's work start later still: 2 December 2027 for systems listed in Annex III, and 2 August 2028 for products covered through Annex I [2]. Europe built the fine before it switched on the duties.

"Whichever is higher" is the phrase that moves the number. Below €500 million of sales the euro figure caps a firm, and above it the share runs. The small-business rule pushes the same logic from the other side, capping a small or medium-sized firm at the lower of the two [1]. The regime is drawn so the cash follows the balance sheet.

So the headline figure is not wrong. It is simply the wrong one for a large firm. €35 million is the smallest a 7% fine can be, and 7% is held back for a closed list of banned uses. A bank whose underwriting agent cannot explain a weekend of declined loan files is not on that list. It is on the deployer list, at 3% [1].

## By the numbers

- **3% — Deployer band:** deployer duties under Article 26 and Article 50's disclosure rules sit in the band of up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher [1].
- **7% — Banned uses only:** the €35 million figure attaches to Article 5's closed list, not to the wider duty to run a system safely [1].
- **€500 million — Crossing point:** the sales level at which 3% passes €15 million and 7% passes €35 million, so the euro amounts stop binding (arithmetic on [1]).
- **4% — Privacy band:** a personal-data failure tied to the same system can reach €20 million or 4% of worldwide turnover, whichever is higher, under a regime the AI Act leaves alone [5][4].

## What I'd watch:

My read is that the budget talk changes shape once the number is worked out rather than quoted. At €2 billion of sales the three bands read €140 million, €60 million and €20 million, and the privacy band reads €80 million (arithmetic on [1] and [5]). None of those totals needs a watchdog to have decided anything. They are what the text says the top is.

- **The two bans arriving on 2 December 2026.** The amended application clause pushes points (ba) and (bb) of the banned list to that date. The closed list the 7% band hangs on is still growing [2].
- **Model providers on the same share.** The European Commission can fine a provider of a general-purpose model up to 3% of yearly worldwide sales or €15 million, whichever is higher [3]. A group that ships its own model meets the same 3% from a second side.
- **The privacy reading running alongside.** Article 2 says the AI Act does not affect the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [4], which is how one incident ends up priced twice, once at 3% and once at 4% [1][5].
- **The record the duty rests on.** A deployer's duties include watching the system run, assigning human oversight and keeping the system's own logs for at least six months [6]. [An agent's logs are only evidence if they can be replayed](/agent-audit-trail-not-evidence/), and that is where a 3% exposure is usually found.

## The catch:

The bands are tops, not prices. The penalty section tells a national watchdog to weigh the nature, gravity and length of the failure, any earlier fines, and the size and sales of the operator before it sets an amount [1]. Member states write the procedural rules themselves [1]. I have not read a single decided case against these tiers, because there is not one to read yet.

Two more limits matter. Below €500 million of sales the euro figure really is the top, so a mid-sized firm quoting €15 million is quoting its own number and not a smaller one. And a share of sales measures the firm, not the harm: the same failure costs a big group more than a small one for reasons that have nothing to do with the agent.

Where I have landed is that the honest board number is a range, with the crossing point written beside it, and not a single figure lifted from a summary. The text is short. The sums take a minute.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Europe's AI rules price failures in bands, and each band is the higher of a euro amount or a share of worldwide sales [1]. The €35 million that summaries quote belongs to the banned list alone, while a deployer's own duties sit one tier down at €15 million or 3%.
- **Why It Matters:** For any firm above €500 million of sales the share wins, so the exposure is a number worked out from the balance sheet rather than a cap that can be absorbed. At €2 billion of sales the deployer band alone runs to €60 million [1].
- **What I'd Watch:** I am watching how the first cases price these tiers, and three details decide it.
  - **The crossing point:** the sales level at which a share of worldwide sales beats the euro figure, which is €500 million in both the 3% and 7% bands and €750 million in the 1% band.
  - **The growing banned list:** two more banned uses apply from 2 December 2026 [2], so the top band covers more conduct each year.
  - **The parallel privacy reading:** the AI Act leaves the data-protection regime intact [4], so one incident can be priced at 3% and at 4% [1][5].
- **The Catch:** The bands are maximums that a national watchdog then scales to the firm, and no case has yet shown how that discretion will be used. Below €500 million of sales the euro figure binds, and the headline number becomes the real one again.
