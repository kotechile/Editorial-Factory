<!-- lead -->
A 5% hurricane deductible on a $400,000 home is $20,000. Your insurer pays nothing until the damage clears that line [1]. The average home claim in 2023 came to $20,062 [2]. So on an average storm the deductible risks your claim: the carrier owes $62.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The deductible stopped being a flat fee. It became a share of the house.

Florida spells out the ladder. A carrier must offer hurricane deductibles of $500, 2%, 5% and 10% of the dwelling limit, and the owner picks one [1]. California runs the same math for earthquakes, capping the deductible at 15% of the coverage on the home [3].

The part I keep circling: the percentage moves with the asset. The loss does not. A house whose insured value rose carries a bigger deductible for the same storm, on the same roof and the same furniture.

Florida tries to make the trade visible. The carrier must work out the dollar figure and print it on the declarations page at issuance and at every renewal [1]. A policy with its own hurricane deductible must also carry an 18-point bold warning that the clause may "result in high out-of-pocket expenses to you" [1].

## By the numbers

- **$8,000 — 2% of a $400,000 limit:** the cash held back before a hurricane claim pays anything, set by the limit and not by the damage [1].
- **$20,062 — average claim paid:** the typical home claim in 2023, a year when 5.33 of every 100 house-years produced one [2].
- **2.8% — wind and hail rate:** the share of insured homes with a wind or hail loss in a typical year of 2018-2022, the largest single cause in the data [2].
- **15% — quake ceiling:** the most a California quake policy may charge as a deductible, as a share of the coverage on the home [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

I look at two numbers on the declarations page now. Neither one is the premium.

- **The dollar figure, not the percentage:** Florida makes the carrier print it. It is the only number that says what a claim must clear before the policy acts like insurance [1]. A reader in another state can do the same sum with the limit on their own page.
- **The credit against the extra risk:** going from 2% to 5% on a $400,000 house adds $12,000 of risk the owner carries. At the 2.8% wind and hail rate, that extra cash costs about $336 a year in expected claims. A carrier credit below that is a bad trade for the owner [1][2].
- **The California million line:** the 5% and 10% quake options do not exist above a $1 million limit. A pre-1980 non-slab home with no verified retrofit starts at 15%, which is $150,000 on a $1,000,000 limit [3][4].
- **Which peril the percentage covers:** the Florida ladder prices hurricane losses only. A burst pipe or a kitchen fire still meets the other deductible on the policy. A large hurricane deductible buys down nothing on a water claim [1].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that this is honest math sold with framing that hides the trade.

The $336 figure is a bench test, not a quote. It uses the rate for the whole wind and hail class. A named-storm deductible fires only in a named storm, so the real cost is lower and the bar a credit must clear is lower too [2]. It also assumes a claim at the average. A total loss runs past any deductible and costs the owner the same spread either way. A $9,000 repair costs the owner nothing more under a 2% deductible and all of it under a 5% one. The deductible is a bet on the size of the next loss as much as on its odds.

Nothing on the public record says what a carrier charges to buy the percentage down. That number lives in the filed rate plan and on the quote. No statute prints it [1][3]. An owner who takes the larger deductible without that credit is guessing at the price of their own risk.

The state rules are also state rules. Florida's ladder and its ceiling for homes under $500,000 apply in Florida. That ceiling carries its own exit. An owner may sign a statement turning down cover for the first dollars of hurricane damage, with the lender's approval, and go higher [1]. California's 15% caps the deductible on the cover an insurer must offer at minimum. It does not set what the policy costs [3][4].

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Hurricane and quake deductibles are now written as a percentage of the dwelling limit. The cash an owner puts up before the carrier pays now scales with the value of the house, not the size of the loss.
- **Why It Matters:** At normal limits the percentage is larger than the average claim. A 5% deductible on a $400,000 home is $20,000 against a $20,062 average claim, so a mid-sized loss lands almost fully on the owner.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The printed dollar value:** Florida makes the carrier work it out on the declarations page. That turns a vague percentage into the figure a claim must clear [1].
  - **The buy-down credit:** whether the carrier's credit for a smaller deductible beats roughly $336 a year, the expected cost of the extra cash at risk [1][2].
  - **The peril behind the percentage:** a hurricane deductible prices hurricane losses only. The water and fire deductibles on the same page are separate [1].
- **The Catch:** the break-even is a bench test built on an average claim and a whole-peril rate. No public source says what a buy-down costs, and the state ladders differ by state and by peril.
