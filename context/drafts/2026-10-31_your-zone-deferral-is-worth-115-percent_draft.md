<!-- lead -->
A foreign-trade zone is sold on duty you pay later, and the later saves less than the pitch promises, because the cost of money is the small part. Bank prime was 7.00 percent on 29 October [1]. Hold a duty bill for 60 days and you keep 1.15 percent of it, hidden inside a number nobody repeats. On a million dollars of duty that is $11,507, about a tenth of one percent of the goods behind it at a 10 percent duty rate.

What a zone really changes is the value your duty is charged on, and the line it is charged at. The calendar is the smallest of the three levers.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Goods in a zone sit outside the customs laws until they are sent into the country [5]. That one fact is the product. My read: the duty question leaves the border and lands on a choice you make later.

Two rules set the number, and neither is about time. The value duty is charged on becomes "the price actually paid or payable for the merchandise in the transaction that caused the merchandise to be admitted into the zone" — your parts price, with the work and profit added inside the zone left out [2]. The rate is yours to pick. Privileged status fixes the foreign goods' own line, at the rate in force on the day you file. Goods you leave alone are treated as the finished item on the entry [2].

Put the two against a plain border entry and the gain shows. Import the finished good and duty lands on its full price, at its own line. Bring the parts in and make the good in the zone, and the base falls to the parts price, while the filing lets you pay the parts line or the finished line [2]. Say the parts sit at 6 percent and the finished good at 2 percent. The gap you can elect is four points of the parts value.

## By the numbers

- **7.00 percent — Cost of borrowed money:** Bank prime on 29 October 2026, the rate a credit line charges to carry an unpaid duty bill [1].
- **3.88 percent — What the cash earns:** The effective federal funds rate on the same release, the low end of the same sum; a million dollars of duty held two months earns $6,378 there [1].
- **0.3464 percent — Fee on the same value:** The fee charged to the importer on a formal entry, inside a per-entry floor and ceiling that Customs and Border Protection (CBP) adjusts each year [4].
- **5 years — Bonded clock:** Goods may neither be put nor stay in a bonded warehouse past five years from the day they land [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The firms that get paid by a zone ran the tariff lines before they signed the lease.

- **The gap, before the site:** Put each part's line beside the finished good's line. Where the finished line is lower, the base and the rate both help. Where it is higher, a zone widens the bill.
- **The filing date:** Privileged status takes the rate in force on the day you file, not the day the goods land [2]. Goods under a quota are put at the higher, non-quota rate once that status is granted, which gives away the quota benefit [2].
- **The export file:** Zone goods that fall under United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) drawback and change in condition cannot go to Mexico or Canada without a duty set on them, due before the 61st day after export unless you show the duty paid there [5]. A plant in Monterrey fed from a zone is a duty question, not a shipping step.
- **The entry count:** The fee is charged per formal entry on the same value, so weekly entry does real work on a large import book [4].
- **The bonded clock:** Anything parked in a bonded warehouse hits a five-year wall from the day it lands [3], and a delay that runs into it turns into a payment.

## The catch

This piece cannot hand you the rate that decides it. The gap lives in your own tariff lines, and where a part and its finished good share a line, the zone's math falls back to the delay you were pitched.

The math also leaves out what a zone costs to run: activation, a stock control and records system, a bond, and staff to keep those records straight. Those costs are fixed, while the saving rises with duty, so a small importer with a thin gap is buying paperwork.

One more limit. A delay only counts if the duty is paid in the end, and goods that leave the zone for export pay nothing at all. My read, held loosely: a zone pays where the parts cross several lines and the finished good sits on a cheaper one. Everyone else is buying a calendar. ## At a glance

- **The shift:** A zone moves the duty question off the border and onto your bill of materials.
- **The number:** 60 days of delay is worth 1.15 percent of the duty at bank prime.
- **The risk:** Where parts and finished good share a line, the zone saves only the calendar.
