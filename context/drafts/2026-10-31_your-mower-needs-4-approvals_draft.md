<!-- lead -->
Four robot mower models hold the only federal approval given to a consumer yard machine for the 2027 American market, and all four belong to one company. Husqvarna lost 334 million krona last quarter, after foreign-made robots went onto a national-security list in July, and your next mower needs one of those approvals before a store can sell it. That list is public.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Husqvarna sells into both markets, so its report reads as a split screen. Sales fell 5 percent organically in the quarter, and the forest and garden arm, which carries the home mowers, fell 10 percent [1]. The company gave three reasons for the mowing weakness: bad weather, "competitive headwinds", and dealers holding too much stock in Europe [1]. It answered with "targeted price increases", the usual reply of a leader squeezed on price [1].

America looks different. On 28 July the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) put foreign-made "advanced robotic devices" on a list of gear that cannot get the approval needed to be sold there [2][3]. A maker can escape that list by getting a Conditional Approval from the Department of War, and the FCC posts each one it grants [2]. The rule catches a mower: a moving machine over 4.4 pounds, with a sensor, a network link of at least 200 kilobits a second, and software that steers it [3]. New models are blocked, while models approved before the change keep selling [3].

Read the register and the pattern is plain. It lists seven robot approvals, and one covers a yard machine: Husqvarna's 305v iQ, 310v iQ, 420v iQ and 440v iQ, cleared on 9 September for a 2027 launch in the US [2][4]. The rest are floor cleaners and factory vehicles. Four of the seven came on or after 16 October, so the queue moves quickly, just not through the mower aisle. What I keep circling is the timing: in the month the leader admitted it cannot hold price in Europe, its four platforms were the only yard machines with permission in America.

## By the numbers

- **334 million krona — Quarterly loss:** the group operating result fell to a loss of 334 million krona from income of 141 million a year earlier, a -3.7 percent margin against 1.5 percent [1].
- **4 of 7 — Yard machines on the register:** the FCC lists seven robot approvals, and Husqvarna's four mowers are the only consumer yard gear among them [2].
- **65 percent — US content bar:** a mower dodges the list if it counts as US-made, meaning domestic parts above 65 percent of cost through 2028 and 75 percent from 2029 [3].
- **$800 to $2,500 — What buyers pay:** the mowers in Consumer Reports' current ratings run from about $800 to more than $2,500, against $400 to $800 for its top battery walk-behind mowers [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The register changes by public notice, so the next entry is the signal. An approval for a second mower brand would mean 2027 brings a real choice rather than four variations on one design.

- **The next name on the list:** a new brand appears there before it appears in a store [2].
- **A premium on the four:** a market with one approved supplier is the easiest place to hold a price [1].
- **Stock of approved models:** everything approved before 28 July can still be imported and sold, and that is where this season's deals sit [3][5].
- **Any move against old approvals:** the FCC can also stop the import of gear it already approved, through a process in its own rules, used in June 2026 for older covered gear [2].

I am not calling this the end of the incumbent. The group still expects 3 billion krona of gross savings by the end of 2028, with about 935 million booked so far [1]. America is also where it already sells the most professional robot mowers, with more than 500 golf courses on its machines and 24 percent of group sales booked there in 2025 [4]. What I am watching is narrower: whether a second mower brand ever appears on a list that holds one.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

Consumer Reports tells readers the FCC "banned the sale" of foreign-made robots including robot mowers, then adds, correctly, that the ban does not touch mowers already owned or already approved [5]. The FCC's own words are narrower: new models cannot get approval, while approved models keep flowing to stores [3]. If you own one, or buy one this season, none of this reaches you.

So the scarcity sits in next year's models, not in the aisle today. And I keep coming back to what the list does not say: it records approvals, not applications, so nobody outside the FCC knows how many brands asked and were turned down. My reading rests on a public list that shows one yard machine, and no source in this run publishes a competitor price, so the claim that the price war will not arrive on schedule is a judgment about a queue, not a forecast. The rated acreage on a spec sheet is already a best-case figure, and [the gap between rated and usable area](/your-1-acre-mowers-04-acre-trap/) is the other place a buyer's math goes wrong.

## At a glance

**The shift:** New foreign-made robot mower models need their own US approval, and the register holds one consumer yard brand.
**The number:** Seven approvals, four of them since 16 October, and only Husqvarna's four mowers.
**The risk:** Waiting for a cheaper 2027 model is a bet on a queue you cannot see.
