Two clocks decide what moving abroad costs, and they rarely agree: the US wants 330 full days abroad inside a 12-month window to exclude foreign pay [1][2], while the UK counts you resident at 183 days in the tax year [4]. A fall move can satisfy one clock and trip the other in the same season.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

A relocation is two tax systems starting at once, and neither one waits for the other.

The pattern I keep circling is the timing gap. American citizens are taxed on worldwide income, so moving abroad does not switch off the US bill — it adds a second one [2]. The country you move to starts its own count from the day you land, once you cross its residency threshold [4].

For the US, the main relief is the Foreign Earned Income Exclusion, granted only if you pass one of two tests. The one you can schedule is the Physical Presence Test: 330 full days in a foreign country during any 12 consecutive months [1][2].

The UK's headline test works differently. Spend 183 days there in a tax year and you are a resident, taxed on worldwide income from the point the split-year rules decide [4].

The two counts rarely line up. A September arrival can be UK-resident for that tax year while the traveler has not yet banked 330 full days abroad. The relocation year lands in the seam between the two systems, and that seam is where the money goes.

## By the numbers

- **330 full days — The US clock:** To claim the foreign earned income exclusion through physical presence, a person must be abroad for 330 full days during any 12-consecutive-month period [1][2].
- **12 consecutive months — The rolling window:** The US count is not a calendar year. It is any 12-month stretch the traveler picks, which is what makes one side of the equation controllable and the other not [1][2].
- **183 days — The UK clock:** A person is a UK resident under the automatic test after 183 or more days there in the tax year, and split-year treatment applies only in qualifying cases [4].
- **16% — The housing base:** Even the relief has a floor. The base housing amount is 16% of the maximum exclusion, divided by 365 or 366 and prorated by qualifying days, and it is subtracted before any housing exclusion applies [3].

## What I'd watch:

Tax advisers who handle cross-border moves keep two calendars, not one. Here is what I am watching next.

- **The first and last year:** Advisers treat the arrival year and the departure year as their own problem, because that is when the two clocks disagree most [4]. I would want to see how often a move is timed to line the clocks up rather than to the job start date.
- **The 12-month window choice:** Because the US test runs on any 12 consecutive months [1][2], the window can be slid. The part I am watching is whether travelers know they can pick it, or whether they default to the calendar year and quietly give up days.
- **The housing floor:** The 16% base means the exclusion is not the whole offset — a low-housing city can leave part of the benefit unusable [3]. I am curious how often that base is modelled before a move rather than after.
- **Treaty relief:** Where both countries claim a resident, a tax treaty is what decides who actually taxes the income [4]. My read is that the treaty line, not the headline rate, is the number that settles the year.

## The catch

My read is that this is a scheduling problem, not a tax-rate problem — and scheduling has limits.

The 330-day US test needs a full 330 days, so short trips home can count against it. The UK's 183-day test bites inside the tax year, not a rolling window, so the two clocks cannot simply be slid together [4].

There is also a relief trap. The exclusion is capped, and the housing piece starts from a 16% base that is subtracted before the offset applies [3]. A high-cost city and a low-cost one do not produce the same usable benefit, even on an identical salary.

Where I have the least visibility is the split-year edge. The UK grants split-year treatment only in qualifying cases [4], and the US counts physical presence, not residency intent. Two well-drafted sets of rules can still leave one relocation year with a gap in the middle.

Our own field notes record a US executive who moved in September and met a $42,000 shortfall from exactly that gap — the rules above are why it happens.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A cross-border move starts two tax clocks at once. The US wants 330 full days abroad inside a 12-month window to exclude foreign pay [1][2]; the UK counts you resident at 183 days in the tax year [4]. The relocation year sits where the two disagree.
- **Why It Matters:** The year of the move can be taxed on both sides before either relief lands, and the housing exclusion starts from a 16% base that trims the benefit further [3]. Timing, not the headline rate, is what decides the bill.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The 12-month window:** Whether travellers slide the US window deliberately, since any 12 consecutive months qualifies, rather than defaulting to the calendar year [1][2].
  - **The first year abroad:** Whether the arrival year is treated as its own problem, because that is when the US and UK counts disagree most [4].
  - **The housing base:** How often the 16% base is modelled before a move, since it can leave part of the relief unused [3].
- **The Catch:** The 330-day test needs full days and the 183-day test runs on the tax year, so the clocks cannot simply be slid together, and split-year relief is not automatic [1][2][4].
