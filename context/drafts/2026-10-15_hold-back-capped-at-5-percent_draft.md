<!-- lead -->
Retainage, the money a remodel contract lets an owner hold back, is capped at five percent, not 15, in the states that write the rule down. Oregon says an owner may keep no more than five percent of the price of the work completed, and its home improvement contracts sit under the same sentence [1].

Minnesota sets the same five percent limit on a building and construction contract, then adds a clock: the owner must hand back all of the hold-back within 60 days after substantial completion [2].

The money kept back is the only part of the budget an owner still controls once the crew is on site. Where no state writes that ceiling down, the number is whatever the contract says.

In California, the new five percent cap does not even reach the house: a residential project of four stories or fewer is left out of it [4].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Retainage is the slice of each progress payment that an owner keeps until the work is done and signed off. It is the owner's real leverage once the crew is on site. It is also the money the crews behind the general contractor are waiting on.

What strikes me is how thin the floor under that number is. Four states read closely here give four answers, and only two of them write the owner's own hold-back into law. Oregon caps it at five percent of the finished work, and it says home improvement contracts are covered [1]. Minnesota caps it at five percent, and it lets an owner cut the hold-back on any monthly payment [2].

California put a five percent cap on private work for contracts signed on or after January 1, 2026, then carved the ordinary house out of it [4]. Virginia's five percent rule lives in the state's public procurement act, so it binds public bodies rather than a homeowner's kitchen [5].

That gap is the point. A cap in a law sets both ends of a deal. It tells the owner what may be kept, and it tells the contractor what cannot be taken. Where no cap applies, the number in the contract is the whole story.

## By the numbers

- **5 percent — Oregon's hold-back ceiling:** An owner may withhold as retainage "an amount equal to not more than five percent of the contract price of the work completed," and partial payments are allowed on contracts for construction and home improvement [1].
- **60 days — Minnesota's release deadline:** The owner must release all retainage no later than 60 days after substantial completion, and the hold-back on the contract may not top five percent [2].
- **1 percent a month — the interest an Oregon owner owes:** Interest on the final payment starts 30 days after the contractor finishes and the owner accepts the work, and it runs until the final payment is sent [1].
- **$500,000 — Oregon's escrow trigger:** Above that contract price, the money kept back must go into an escrow account that pays interest [1].

## What I'd watch:

The moves around these numbers say more than the numbers do. Owners and builders are re-reading the release language, and the two states with a written cap are the ones where the clause has a backstop.

- **Whose cap applies:** Oregon's sentence names home improvement contracts. Minnesota's covers a contract for the design, construction, alteration, improvement, repair or maintenance of real property [3]. California's cap says out loud that a home of four stories or fewer is left out [4].
- **The escrow line:** Oregon pushes a hold-back above $500,000 into an account that pays interest, and the interest runs from the date the payment request was approved [1]. Below that price, the money simply sits with the owner.
- **The reduction rule:** Minnesota lets an owner cut the hold-back, or drop it, on any monthly payment when the work is going well. If the owner cuts it, the contractor has to cut retainage for its subcontractors at the same rate [2]. That second half is the one that keeps the supplier chain paid.
- **The acceptance window:** Oregon starts its interest clock at acceptance. The party that gets the completion notice has 15 days to accept the work or list what is left. If nobody answers, interest starts 30 days after that window closes [1].

I'd want to know how many owners realize the clock starts on their silence, not on their objection.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

My read is that these caps are real but narrow, and reading them as a national rule would be a mistake. Two of the four states write the owner's hold-back into law. One leaves the house out. One covers public work only.

The other half of the catch is arithmetic. Five percent of the work completed is not five percent of the whole contract. Neither figure is a warranty, either. A hold-back does not fix bad work, and it does not stop a subcontractor or a supplier from claiming against the property on its own separate clock.

The part I keep circling is how much of this is settled before the first payment. A contract that names the ceiling, the release trigger and the person who signs off on the work has something no statute can hand over: a date that everyone agreed to.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** States that write an owner's retainage into law cap it at five percent of the completed work, and California's new retention cap for private projects, in force for contracts signed from January 2026, leaves the ordinary house out.
- **Why It Matters:** The hold-back is the owner's leverage after the crew arrives, and the ceiling on it decides how much of the money stays put. Oregon also puts a price on keeping it too long, at one percent a month once acceptance is 30 days old.
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether the release language names a trigger the owner controls, and how the reduction rules travel down the chain.
  - **The ceiling:** The most an owner may keep, stated as a share of the completed work rather than of the whole contract.
  - **The release trigger:** What has to happen before the money is owed back, such as an inspection sign-off or a written acceptance.
  - **The chain:** The rule that a contractor must cut retainage for its subcontractors when the owner cuts it for the contractor.
- **The Catch:** Where no cap applies, the contract is the whole rule, and a hold-back settles neither bad work nor the separate claim a supplier can file against the property.
