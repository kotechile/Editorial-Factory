<!-- lead -->
Your upgrade's quoted price is fixed. The loan behind it resets every month, and a quarter-point move adds about $9 to the median payment — a silent reset that never shows up on the quote.

Intercontinental Exchange (ICE) put a number on the loan side in its October Mortgage Monitor. The median outstanding second-lien home-equity line of credit (HELOC) stands at $44,000, at a median rate of 7.4% [1]. Those lines track the prime rate and reprice monthly, which is why the payment moves at all.

In the same weeks, heating and cooling makers sent another round of October price letters. Most land in the 3% to 6% band, and a few go far higher [2].

Neither document hides anything. The silent part is that the two never sit in the same folder.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Two clocks are running on one purchase. The equipment price moves when a manufacturer issues a letter. The loan moves whenever the prime rate changes.

The Federal Reserve just changed it. The central bank raised its target range a quarter point to 3.75% to 4% on September 16 [3]. The vote was 12 to 0, and the statement cited elevated inflation. Bank of America lifted prime to 7.00% the next day, from 6.75% in December [4].

What strikes me here is how cleanly the two halves separate. The efficiency rating on a new heat pump is fixed the day it goes in. The cost of the money is not fixed at all. Neither is the price of the replacement you may need in a decade.

The loan data shows borrowers already shifting toward floating money. Adjustable-rate mortgages (ARMs) took more than 11% of rate locks, their largest share in nearly four years [1]. Active first-lien ARMs number 3.1 million, the most in five and a half years.

**By the numbers:**

- **$44,000 — Median HELOC balance:** the stock of second-lien lines, at a median 7.4% [1]. Fully drawn, that is about $3,256 a year in interest.
- **$9 a month — Cost per quarter point:** ICE's own arithmetic for the median HELOC payment [1]. Four quarter points add about $36 a month, with no new money borrowed.
- **3% to 6% — October equipment letters:** the typical rise this month [2]. Aeris Metals went to an average of 12.9% on linesets, and Robertshaw to as much as 15% on affected products.
- **6.6% — Remodeler input costs:** the second-quarter rise in nonlabor inputs, up from 3.9% the quarter before [5]. That is eleven straight quarters above zero.

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The next Federal Open Market Committee meeting runs October 27 and 28 [6]. Whatever comes out of it lands on the median HELOC within a billing cycle. That reset is contractual, not negotiated.

- **The ARM share of new locks:** above 11% now [1]. The 2020 vintage matters most. About 74,000 seven-year ARMs written that year reset in 2027, with a median payment jump near $1,066 a month, or 36%.
- **The effective dates on the letters:** Panasonic's 3.7% to 4.0% starts October 19, and Resideo's adjustment arrives October 31 [2]. A quote written across either date is a different number for the same box.
- **Fixed against floating:** the national average HELOC rate was 8.195% on October 5 [7]. A ten-year home equity loan averaged 8.164%. Nearly the same price, for a contract that cannot move against one that can.
- **Cash buyers:** they are the only group whose upgrade price is fixed on both sides. I keep coming back to that gap, because it is the whole case for paying cash when the reserve allows it.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that this is a story about structure, not a crisis. Nine dollars is small next to a $44,000 balance. A household with slack in its budget will absorb a few resets without noticing.

The scale moves with the line. At $100,000, a quarter point is about $21 a month [1]. Four of them come to roughly $83. Same contract, four times the exposure.

Two caveats need keeping apart. The 7.4% is the median of lines already open, many priced when margins were thinner. New lines advertise 8.195% [7]. The marginal borrower pays more than the median book suggests.

The letters also price equipment, not labor. The installed price is not the list price. What the letters do prove is direction and timing: the box got dearer this month, on dates a buyer can read.

One case in the desk's own file points at the deeper limit. A heat pump sized without a balance-point check produced a $920 electric bill in a single cold snap. That is one case, not a rate. Still, the savings side is fixed at install, and everything else in the equation can move.

Home values are still rising, at least. The median existing-home price was $417,500 in September, up 1.3% for a 39th straight month [8]. Equity can absorb a bigger loan. It cannot make the payment, which comes out of income every month.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Both prices inside a financed home upgrade moved in October. Manufacturers sent another round of list increases, mostly 3% to 6% [2]. The loan that pays for the equipment resets monthly off a prime rate the Fed raised in September [1][3].
- **Why It Matters:** The only fixed numbers at signing are the equipment price and the efficiency rating. The cost of money floats. A payback estimate that holds the rate steady overstates the return, and the error grows with each quarter point.
- **What I'd Watch:** The rate path and the price letters both carry dates, which makes the next moves readable.
  - **The October Fed meeting:** a quarter point there adds about $9 a month to the median HELOC payment within one billing cycle.
  - **The letter dates:** a quote written across an effective date is a different number for the same equipment.
  - **The ARM reset wave:** about 74,000 seven-year ARMs from 2020 reset in 2027, competing for the same household cash.
- **The Catch:** The $9 figure is ICE's median estimate, not an observed change, and it scales with the balance. New lines also price above the 7.4% median of the existing book.
