The broker in Lisbon, Dublin or Amsterdam sells you the country's own index fund, and the United States does not see an index fund. It sees a company abroad that earns dividends and interest, which is enough to make it a passive foreign investment company — the code's label for a fund whose income is passive — and what it bills you is not the capital-gains rate you would pay at home. Each slice of the gain is taxed at the top rate in force for the year it falls in, 37% in the tax agency's latest table, and then interest is added for the years the tax went unpaid. The rule is decades old, and it lands on the purchase rather than the sale, because the label is decided by what the fund holds and not by what you do with it.

<!-- tension -->

## Why the wrapper stays invisible

Nothing at the counter tells the fund you are about to buy apart from the one your neighbours buy. A European index fund holds shares, collects dividends and does little else, so it meets the test on one of two counts: most of its income is passive, or most of its assets produce passive income [1]. The asset test is what catches a plain share fund, because shares count as assets held to produce income even when the fund never sells them.

Once the label sticks, the default rule replaces the capital-gains treatment a home investor gets. Each year's payouts are measured against a moving line, and the part above it is an excess distribution: the fund's payouts in the year, less 125% of the average of the three years before [2]. A sale gets no separate rule. The whole gain on a sale counts as an excess distribution, so the entire profit joins the sum at once [4].

Then comes the part few readers have seen described. The excess distribution is spread evenly across every day the fund was held, and each slice is taxed at the top rate in force for the year it falls in [3]. A ten-year hold becomes ten annual bills at ten top rates, none of them the long-term capital-gains rate. Interest is added on each slice, at the rates the agency uses for tax paid late [3].

My read is that the wrapper is the cheapest thing to change and the last thing anyone checks, because the paperwork at purchase looks the same either way.

## By the numbers

- **75% — Income test:** a fund abroad is caught when 75% or more of its gross income is passive, or when at least 50% of its assets produce passive income [1].
- **125% — Payout line:** a payout above 125% of the three-year average is an excess distribution, and only the excess is taxed this way [2].
- **8621 — One form per fund:** the return is filed for each fund held, directly or not, and once a year — so five local funds is five returns, sold or unsold [4].

## What advisers do with it

The first move is to stop treating this as a fund choice. Advisers describe three routes: accept the default, elect to mark the fund to market, or elect the qualified-elect treatment. Marking to market taxes the yearly rise as ordinary income at the same top rates, adds no interest, and needs the fund's shares to trade on a qualified exchange [6]. The qualified-elect route needs the fund itself to hand the investor a yearly statement of earnings, which most European index funds do not give out.

The second move is to price the wrapper, not the holdings. The same fund held inside a local pension or an insurance wrapper that is not itself the taxed object behaves differently from the fund held in a brokerage account. Order also matters, because the choice to mark to market is made on the return for the year of purchase, so a decision left until the following spring is already late. The [expat financial and international tax evaluator](/calculators/expat-evaluator/) models that ordering against the exclusion and credit questions.

What I keep circling is the count of forms. A reader who buys four local funds in one year, holds them for a decade and sells nothing files forty returns in that decade, and each one needs the fund's year-end value and the year's payouts. That is a bill paid in hours, and it arrives years before the tax bill does.

## The catch

An exemption exists, and it is smaller than it reads. No reporting is needed when the funds are worth $25,000 or less at year end, or $50,000 on a joint return, but only if the investor also took no excess distribution and booked no gain on a sale [4]. The relief therefore ends in the year it would have mattered, because a sale is what creates the gain; a reader who has sat under the line for years meets the full rule in the year they sell.

Two limits on the argument, and the first is what I had to check twice. A fund that fails both tests is not caught at all, so a fund holding buildings directly, or trading actively, can sit outside the label. And the rate moves: the table's 37% is the top rate for 2018 through 2025, while the 20% capital-gains rate it displaces is a separate rate on a separate base, with interest still to add [4][5]. The gap is a direction, not a total, and anyone quoting the difference as the whole cost has left out the interest.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** The wrapper, not the fund, decides whether the US taxes a gain at capital-gains rates.
**The number:** 37% is the top rate each slice is taxed at, before interest.
**The risk:** The $25,000 reporting exemption ends in the year of the sale.
