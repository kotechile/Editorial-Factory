<!-- lead -->
Private mortgage insurance (PMI) can bill $245 a month long after the risk it covers has shrunk, because the dates that end it come from the loan you signed rather than the house you now own [4][2]. One of those dates you can move, and only by asking. The other arrives on its own, whatever the balance on your statement says [1].

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** The end of PMI is a date set by your original loan, not by the equity you have built.
- **The number:** 78% of the original price, read off the schedule, not your balance.
- **The risk:** Extra principal moves the request date and leaves the automatic one alone.

<!-- tension -->

## Two clocks on one loan

The rule arrived in 1998 and was tightened in 2000, so it covers every conventional loan with PMI written since. At closing the servicer, the firm that collects your payment, must give you the amortization schedule. That is the table that splits each payment between principal and interest, and it comes with three dates [3]. The first is when you may ask to cancel on that schedule. The second is that you may ask sooner, based on what you have actually paid. The third is when the cover ends without anyone asking at all.

Only the first two involve a request. To cancel at 80 percent of the price you paid, you send the servicer a letter and keep a clean payment record. You also stay current, show the home has not fallen below that price, and confirm that no second loan sits behind the first [2]. That last pair is where most letters die, because the test is about decline, not about the gain you think you have.

The automatic date needs no letter. It sits at 78 percent of the original value, is read off the original schedule, and the statute tells the servicer to ignore the outstanding balance on the day it lands [1]. Paying extra does not pull it forward, because that date never looks at what you have paid.

Two limits sit behind it. The cover cannot run past the first day of the month after the midpoint of the loan's term while you are current, which is 180 months on a 30-year note [2]. And a loan the lender classed as high risk when it was written can carry the insurance past both dates, if the closing papers said so [2].

## By the numbers

- **60 days:** the late payment that disqualifies a request, counted across a two-year lookback [1].
- **30 days:** the point past which no premium may be charged once a request is in [2].
- **45 days:** the window a servicer has to return unearned premiums after the cover ends [2].

## What the gap costs

I ran the arithmetic on a $400,000 house bought with 10 percent down, which is a $360,000 loan at 7.00 percent, an assumption rather than a quote. The balance passes the earlier line in month 101 and the automatic date lands in month 115. Add $200 a month and the earlier line arrives in month 67, while the automatic date stays at 115 [1][2].

So the household paying extra has cleared a line the law already recognises, and the premium keeps billing for four more years unless somebody writes. The servicer has no duty to volunteer the earlier date, and the disclosure that names it sits in a closing file most owners have not opened since they signed [3].

- **Check the schedule first:** The closing file holds the original table and the date it was set to reach the earlier line, which is the number to compare with today's balance [3].
- **Count the lookback:** A single late payment inside the past two years can reset the request, so the payment record matters as much as the balance [1].
- **Clear the second loan:** A home equity line, or any loan registered behind the first, blocks a cancellation until it is paid off or moved behind the new lender's claim [2].

I would check that last item before writing anything, because a household that opened a credit line for a remodel can sit well past the balance line with no route through [2].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that the rule is narrower than the pitch. A house that gains value gives its owner no lever here, because both dates read the price paid at closing; appreciation matters only if the servicer chooses to accept a new appraisal under its own policy, and nothing in the statute makes it do that [1][2].

A decline cuts the other way, and that asymmetry is the part to carry away. A falling market can block the letter at the earlier line, since the borrower has to show the home has not dropped below the price paid, while the automatic date keeps to its schedule because it never looks at value at all [1][2].

So the move is dull. Find the closing file, read the original schedule, and compare today's balance with the earlier line on the first page. If the balance is past it and the payment record is clean, the request is a letter, and the [Down Payment and PMI Calculator](/calculators/down-payment/) runs the same comparison on your own numbers before you send it. After that, the servicer owes the refund within 45 days of acting [2].
