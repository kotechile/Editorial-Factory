<!-- lead -->
Samsung Electronics America is suing CMA CGM, the French carrier formed by Compagnie Maritime d'Affrètement (CMA) and Compagnie Générale Maritime (CGM), for at least $186 million [1][2]. The claim, filed with the Federal Maritime Commission (FMC) and served September 1, says Samsung paid more than 121,000 delay fees it never should have owed [2][3].

<!-- tension -->
**Why it matters:** This is the biggest test yet of whether a shipper can win back years of unfair fees under the Ocean Shipping Reform Act of 2022 (OSRA-22). That law, plus a 2024 FMC rule, set a hard standard: a carrier bill must arrive within 30 days, name the right party, and trace to a delay the shipper actually caused [5].

**The big picture:** Most importers treat these fees as a normal cost of doing business. Samsung is proving the cash comes back when the delay was the carrier's fault, not the shipper's.

The dispute turns on "store-door" shipments — jobs where the carrier was on the hook to truck or rail each container all the way to its final stop, not just across the ocean [4]. Samsung says those moves began breaking down around 2020, when truck and chassis shortages and crowded ports snarled U.S. supply chains [2].

The carrier blamed those same problems when Samsung questioned the bills, then refused to waive them [2]. Samsung also says the carrier sometimes switched prepaid store-door moves to "container yard" moves, leaving Samsung to arrange and pay for the inland haul itself [2][4].

**By the numbers:**
- **$186M:** Samsung's total claim against CMA CGM [1][2].
- **$148M:** The share in allegedly improper demurrage, detention, and rail-storage charges alone [2].
- **121,000+:** How many demurrage, detention, and related charges Samsung says it paid [3].
- **$3.75M:** The charges on one batch of containers held at an inland rail ramp for over two weeks by a chassis shortage the carrier acknowledged [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
**The playbook:** Turn your own detention and demurrage bills into a dispute pipeline, not a write-off.

- **Audit every bill against the FMC rules.** Check the 30-day window and that the bill names the party who actually booked the move. A bill that misses either is defective [5].
- **Log which delays were yours.** These fees are only lawful when the delay traces to you. If a chassis shortage or a jammed port held your cargo, write it down — that record is what wins a dispute [4].
- **Automate the disputes.** Detention disputes are a bounded, repeatable job. Software that flags bad bills and files claims can claw back money without adding staff [6].
- **Don't wait for a nine-figure case.** The money hides in the long tail of small fees most teams never contest. One disputed $590,000 charge was enough for the carrier to hold 40 of Samsung's containers at a New York-area terminal in April 2022 — the leverage runs both ways [2].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
**The catch:** This is a slow fight that lives or dies on your records. The FMC's first ruling is not due until September 1, 2027, and a final one until March 15, 2028 [6]. A 2025 court ruling also struck down one part of the FMC's billing rule, so some of the standard still has gray areas [5]. You only win if you can prove, in writing, that the delay belonged to the carrier.

<!-- tldr -->
- **The Reality Check:** Shippers usually pay container delay fees without question, but under OSRA-22 a carrier can only bill you for delays you actually caused — and one major shipper is now demanding $186 million back on that basis.
- **The Winning Moves:** Build a dispute process around the FMC's billing standard.
  - **Audit bills:** Check that each bill arrived within 30 days and names the right party.
  - **Log the delays:** Record chassis shortages, port jams, and failed inland moves as the carrier's fault.
  - **Automate claims:** Use software to flag and file bad charges at scale.
- **The Fine Print:** The fight is years long and needs strong proof — the first ruling is not due until 2027, and courts recently set aside part of the rules, so clean notes are your best defense.
