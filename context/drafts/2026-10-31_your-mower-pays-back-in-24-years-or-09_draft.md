Your robot mower's payback is not a product number. The same $2,799.98 quarter-acre machine earns out in 2.4 years against a Northeast mowing bill and in 0.86 years against a Southeast one, because the season it replaces runs 24 weeks in one place and 45 in the other [1]. That is the trap: payback is one division, and the number you divide by is a calendar.

| Region | Mowing season | Weekly visit | Annual total |
|---|---|---|---|
| Northeast | ~24 weeks | $49–$77 | $1,176–$1,848 |
| Midwest | ~26 weeks | $48–$70 | $1,248–$1,820 |
| West / Northwest | ~34 weeks | $45–$68 | $1,530–$2,312 |
| Southeast | ~45 weeks | $46–$72 | $2,070–$3,240 |
| Southwest | ~46 weeks | $42–$64 | $1,932–$2,944 |

Those are published quotes for a typical quarter-acre lawn, drawn from bookings between September 2025 and September 2026 in a guide dated 26 October 2026 [1]. Mowing runs $45 to $75 a visit there, with most households paying $60 [1]. Anyone pricing a robot against that bill is comparing a one-off purchase to a seasonal service, which is why the same machine looks like a bargain in Georgia and a stretch in Maine.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Mowing is sold by the visit and budgeted by the year, so the two units never quite meet. A quote looks like a monthly bill to anyone who thinks in subscriptions, but a northern lawn needs that visit a couple of dozen times and a southern one about four dozen, which turns one price into very different annual bills [1]. Most payback write-ups treat the contract as if it ran all twelve months.

My read is that the math in those write-ups is fine and the calendar is wrong. A one-year payback built on twelve months of mowing is, in the Northeast, closer to two and a half years wearing a southern schedule. That distance is the whole gap between buying the machine and leaving it.

The monthly average makes the error harder to see. Marketplaces publish a per-month figure for lawn care that folds in feeding, leaf removal and bed weeding, and spreading it over a year gives a number bigger than a whole northern mowing bill [1]. A robot only removes the mowing.

The wage under the visit explains why the price does not fall. The federal labor survey puts the median pay for landscaping and groundskeeping workers at $18.82 an hour in its 2025 data [2]. At that rate a $60 visit covers about 3.2 hours of a worker's time, before fuel, machines, cover or the drive between jobs.

## By the numbers

- **2.8× — One mower, two seasons:** the quarter-acre machine's payback runs 2.38 years against the lowest Northeast bill and 0.86 years against the highest Southeast one, a spread set only by how many weeks the grass grows [1].
- **$451.68 — A season of your own mowing:** 24 weeks at the source's own "1+ hour per mow" estimate, paid at the median wage [1][2]; the same sum over a 45-week season gives $846.90.
- **6.2 versus 3.3 — The price in seasons of labor:** the quarter-acre list price equals 6.2 Northeast seasons of your own mowing at that wage, and 3.3 Southeast seasons, which is the honest way to price the time rather than the money [1][2].
- **3.2 hours — What one visit buys:** $60 divided by $18.82 [1][2]; at the top of the range a $75 visit covers 4.0 hours.

## What I'd watch:

The quotes that hold up arrive with a visit count attached, and that count is the first thing worth reading.

- **The season line:** operators who quote a flat rate usually know their local season to the week, and the annual figure follows from it. A quote with no visit count is a price, not a bill.
- **The quarter-acre scope:** published per-visit prices are quoted for a quarter-acre lawn [1], while the machines in this class — the ones [the yard robots hub](/categories/yard-robotics/) sorts by rated area — start at a quarter acre and climb. On a bigger lot the bill grows with the visit price while the machine's price barely moves, so a large northern lot can pay back as fast as a small southern one.
- **The sale window:** Kress was selling its quarter-acre KR161 at $1,499.99 against a $2,799.98 list price, in a sale dated to 31 December 2026 [3]. A sale like that halves every payback number here, so the price in any comparison is a dated input, not a property of the machine.

## The catch:

The data behind every figure above belongs to one booking marketplace, and it exists to sell bookings. A local operator who mows four lawns on one street may bill quite differently, and no survey of contract prices is being consulted here.

The totals are mowing only. Feeding, aerating and leaf cleanup sit outside them, and a robot does not do that work — which cuts both ways, because the full-service monthly average that payback claims like to quote makes the saving look bigger than the mowing line does [1]. I find it hard to square a comparison that subtracts a full-service bill from a machine that only cuts grass.

The scope is the other limit. Every price above is a quarter-acre price, so the one-acre machines in this class are being priced against a bill for a quarter of their rated area, and the northern numbers are the cautious end of the range rather than a national one [1][4]. On a lot that is sloped, gated or cut into zones, the machine may not cover the area at all.

There is also a cost on the machine side that the contract does not have: blade sets, and in time a battery. I could not find a battery price or cycle count that a reader could check for either machine, so that line is left out rather than guessed.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** Payback claims are moving off "a year of mowing" and onto the number of weeks a region's grass actually grows.
**The number:** The same machine's payback varies nearly threefold between the shortest and longest mowing seasons in the country.
**The risk:** The published visit prices are quarter-acre prices, so your own quote moves the answer more than the machine's price does.
