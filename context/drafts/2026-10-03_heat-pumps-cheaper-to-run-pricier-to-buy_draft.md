<!-- lead -->
In the same month an independent study put the heat-pump savings for Maryland's oil-and-propane households at up to $1,600 a year, the HVAC manufacturers pushed through another round of price increases — and Carrier's CEO told analysts he "can and will continue to raise price." The two numbers a homeowner needs to make a heat-pump decision are moving in opposite directions at the same time.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

RMI, the energy-research nonprofit, found that roughly 330,000 Maryland single-family homes still heat with delivered fuels — 14% fuel oil and 4% propane — and that switching those homes to a heat pump saves between $1,200 and $1,600 a year on average, with the sharpest savings on the Eastern Shore and in Western Maryland [1]. The running-cost case for switching has never looked better.

What strikes me here: the buy side told a different story in the same window. Carrier CEO Dave Gitlin told the Morgan Stanley conference on September 15 that higher prices are necessary to offset tariffs, fuel, copper, steel and aluminum, then said it plainly: "We can't raise price 8% every four months, but we can and will continue to raise price" [2]. Trane's CFO said the company had already pushed two residential increases this year — up to 5% each on April 1 and July 1 — and called pricing "a lever" it has to pull to balance cost against inflation [2].

## By the numbers

- **$1,200–$1,600/yr — Running-cost savings:** what RMI calculates a Maryland delivered-fuel household saves switching to a heat pump, with the biggest gains on the Eastern Shore and Western Maryland [1].
- **~330,000 — Delivered-fuel homes:** Maryland single-family households on fuel oil (14%) or propane (4%) — the population with the most to gain from the switch [1].
- **5–15% — September price increases:** the range of HVAC manufacturer hikes that landed in September, from Jones Stephens (5–15%) to OmegaFlex, M&M and Snappy (up to 6% each) [3].
- **~7.5M — Carrier's raised forecast:** U.S. residential units Carrier now expects this year, up from 6.5–7 million, with last year's repair-over-replace trend "largely subsided" [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The people closest to this are acting like both numbers are real. The manufacturers' September increases didn't land once — they hit on the 1st, 3rd and 21st, a rolling series rather than a single adjustment [3]. And the demand side is what lets them keep pushing: Carrier's CEO said last year's repair-over-replace trend has "largely subsided," so homeowners who deferred replacing an aging unit are coming back to the market [2].

The part I keep circling: the break-even math has stopped being a static calculation. If the running-cost savings are growing at the same moment the ticket price is climbing, the answer to "does it pay for itself?" depends on which arrow moves faster between your first quote and your signed contract.

- **The savings are regional, not universal:** Maryland's $1,600-a-year figure is a delivered-fuel story — oil and propane homes, concentrated on the Eastern Shore and Western Maryland — not a natural-gas or electric-resistance story [1].
- **The replacement is bundled:** a heat pump provides heating and cooling in one system, so households with aging air conditioning and a failing furnace are replacing two systems at once [1].
- **The incentive lever is EmPOWER:** RMI points to Maryland's energy-efficiency program as the mechanism to steer incentives toward the delivered-fuel households with the most to gain [1].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read: none of this means "wait." It means the two headline numbers are no longer enough to decide on their own. The savings figure is an average across electric providers, and your actual break-even depends on your local rate and your own unit's replacement timing [1]. The price increases are real but uneven — September's 5–15% range landed on different product lines, from service parts to specific product groups, not uniformly across every heat-pump model [3].

I could be wrong, but I'd treat "heat pumps pay for themselves" the same way I'd treat "heat pumps are getting too expensive" — both are true, and neither is the whole sentence.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** An independent study says switching to a heat pump saves Maryland's oil-and-propane households $1,200–$1,600 a year — while HVAC manufacturers are simultaneously raising equipment prices 5–15% and saying more increases are coming.
- **Why It Matters:** A heat-pump decision is a break-even calculation, and both of its inputs just moved in opposite directions. The savings that justify the switch got documented at record levels at the same moment the upfront price those savings must pay back got more expensive.
- **What I'd Watch:** whether the running-cost savings or the equipment price moves faster between quote and contract.
  - **The payback race:** the question shifts from "does it pay for itself?" to "does it pay for itself faster than the price inflates?"
  - **The rolling hikes:** September's increases landed on three separate dates, so the cost is a moving target rather than one jump [3].
  - **The EmPOWER lever:** Maryland can steer efficiency incentives toward the oil-and-propane homes with the most to gain [1].
- **The Catch:** Both numbers are averages. The $1,600 savings figure depends on your local rate and fuel type, and the 5–15% price hikes landed unevenly across product lines — so the real answer is still a local one.
