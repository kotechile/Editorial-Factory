<!-- lead -->
Your labor line churns about 40% a year, and every exit bills the business. Workers in warehousing leave their jobs at roughly 40% a year. That is the 2026 estimate from the Bureau of Labor Statistics' Job Openings and Labor Turnover Survey (JOLTS) [1]. One exit bills about $21,800 in warehousing. Gallup's rule charges 40% of a worker's pay to refill the seat [2]. At a 200-person distribution center, that churn refills about 80 seats a year. A robot fleet has to beat this stream, not the wage bill.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
For supply chain leaders building an automation case: the industry's separation rate runs about 40% a year, one exit costs around $21,800, and new hires work at 60–70% of standard for weeks. Price the labor line from replacement cost before you sign a fleet quote.

<!-- tension -->
## The Floor Empties Every Year
The wage line looks calm, and the churn hides behind it. Warehousing paid an average of $26.76 an hour in April 2026, about $54,500 a year [2]. The same data family also counts people leaving, and it reads roughly 40% a year [1]. So a budget built on wages sees a stable floor and misses the refill.

Refilling a seat costs more than the wage printed on it. Hiring and screening alone run $800 to $1,500 per hire [1]. A new associate works at 60–70% of standard in month one, and trainers stop picking to train [1]. Overtime covers the open seat at time-and-a-half. CognitOps prices a full warehouse exit at 50–150% of yearly pay once all of that is counted [1]. Its own example is blunt: a mid-size DC with 200 people and 40% churn absorbs $1.5 million a year or more [1]. A fleet is budgeted against the wage. The leakage sits in that churn.

## By the numbers
- **$21,800 per exit:** Gallup's 40%-of-salary replacement rule applied to warehousing's $54,500 annual average [2].
- **$1.5 million a year:** replacement costs at a mid-size DC running 40% turnover on 200 people [1].
- **60–70% of standard:** what a new associate produces in month one, while trainers stop picking [1].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## How Operators Model the Line
The teams closest to this math split the exits by type first. A seasonal spike that heals itself and a quit problem that compounds need different levers [2]. Then they price both levers on the same line item. A retention program — career paths, better schedules, small role-based bonuses — costs $2,000 to $5,000 per associate a year [1]. Exit costs run several times that. On a 200-person floor, that program and a robot fleet chase the same operating budget, and only one sits in the capital deck. I would also carry the wage and the exit cost into the case together, the way the wage-floor payback argument on this desk does [3](/wage-floor-sets-robot-payback/). The federal wage table behind this desk's floor argument, O*NET [4], puts the median pay for the job the floor is staffed from at $19.35 an hour. So a case priced at the wage alone undercounts the line by the churn above it.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The Catch
The two published exit-cost rules disagree, and they sit on different bases. Gallup's rule charges 40% of pay, about $21,800 at warehousing's average [2]. A Center for American Progress estimate charges about 20% of pay for a worker earning under $50,000 [1]. One share rides an industry average, the other a lower wage band. So the spread is honest, and no single number is the exit cost. A retention program also does not delete the line a fleet targets; it steadies the churn, which is a smaller claim than a robot pitch makes. The net cost of a labor line is the real baseline. Price both levers against it, and buy whichever moves it for less.
