A product that costs $100 ex-works in China lands in the United States at $136 — while the same product, made in Mexico at a $108 factory price, lands at $110 [3]. The cheaper sticker price lost by 19% once freight and tariffs were counted.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The unit price printed on a purchase order is an optical illusion.

For two decades the offshore math was simple: a technician in China cost a few dollars an hour, so the sticker price beat everyone. That arithmetic has inverted, and it inverted on the line items nobody puts on the quote.

What strikes me is that the labor gap flipped. China's average manufacturing worker now costs $6.69 an hour fully loaded, against $5.56 for a Mexican entry-level operator [1]. Add the tariff stack — Chinese goods face 17.5–35% on top of their base duty, while a USMCA-qualified Mexican product enters at zero — and the gap widens before the container leaves the port [1]. A cheaper Mexican comparison using a different wage series still lands at $4.90 versus $6.50, a 25% gap [2].

**Why it matters:** This is the total-landed-cost (TLC) question — the full cost of a product from origin to your dock. The founder's standing rule is that offshore unit labor is a trap when ocean freight, port demurrage, quality-audit travel and customs duties add 25–40% to the base cost. The field case that keeps getting quoted is a steel casting bought in Southeast Asia at $18 that landed at $24.80, against $21.50 nearshored to Monterrey.

## By the numbers

- **$6.69 vs $5.56 — Fully-loaded wage:** China's average manufacturing worker versus Mexico's entry-level operator, before any tariff is added [1].
- **17.5–35% — China tariff stack:** The added duty on Chinese goods (Section 301 plus Section 122 plus the base rate) against 0% for USMCA-qualified Mexican goods [1].
- **~19% — Landed-cost gap:** A worked example lands at $110 from Mexico versus $136 from China, despite the Mexican factory price being $8 higher [3].
- **4.6% — Import growth:** US manufactured-goods imports still rose last year even as reshoring investment tripled, so the sourcing call is not as obvious as the wage chart suggests [4].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The people closest to the sourcing decision are rebuilding their cost models around landed cost rather than renegotiating the sticker price.

- **Rebaselining on landed cost:** Operators are loading freight, duty, customs brokerage, inland drayage and carrying cost into one number before comparing two factories. The Mexico-side firms now market a full cost-stack figure — roughly 36% lower unit costs once tariff, logistics and labor are combined [2].
- **Checking tariff exposure by product:** The China gap is not flat. The effective US tariff on Chinese goods runs 29.5–33% for a tariff-exposed line, but some low-tariff goods barely gap at all [3]. The TLC math only bites where duty and freight are heavy.
- **Watching the wage trend, not the level:** China's manufacturing wages roughly doubled from 2014 to 2023 on an ~8% annual rate, then flatlined in 2024 [1]. What I'd want to know is whether that pause resumes, because it is the single input that reopens the offshore case.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The landed-cost gap is real but it is not a universal verdict.

Kearney's Reshoring Index is still negative — it improved from −115 to −91, and US manufactured-goods imports actually rose 4.6% last year [4]. My read is that the TLC advantage tilts hard toward Mexico for tariff-exposed, freight-heavy products, but China still wins on long, complex runs where its supplier density and productivity offset the wage gap [3]. The honest tool is a TLC calculator fed with your own freight and duty rates, not a headline comparison.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A product made for $100 in China lands at $136 after freight and tariff, while the same product made for $108 in Mexico lands at $110 — the decision is decided by total landed cost, not the factory price [3].
- **Why It Matters:** China's fully-loaded labor ($6.69/hr) now exceeds Mexico's ($5.56/hr), and a 17.5–35% tariff stack on Chinese goods versus 0% under USMCA widens the gap before the shipment leaves the port [1].
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **Landed-cost rebaselining:** Whether sourcing teams fold freight, duty, brokerage, drayage and carrying cost into one comparison number [2].
  - **Tariff exposure by product:** Whether low-tariff, high-value lines stay in China while heavy, tariff-exposed lines shift to Mexico [3].
  - **China's wage trend:** Whether the 2024 pause in Chinese wage growth resumes, since that single input reopens the offshore case [1].
- **The Catch:** Kearney's index is still negative and imports rose 4.6% last year, so the Mexico advantage is real but product-specific — China still wins on complex, high-volume runs [4].
