<!-- lead -->
Drawback refunds up to 99% of the duty an importer paid on goods that are later exported or destroyed, and much of that money is never claimed. The number rewrites the math behind a factory move: a reshoring case that credits the shift with "escaping" a tariff is only escaping the share that stays in the United States.

That is not a loophole. It is a program from 1789, and it sits in the file of every importer now re-quoting a supply chain.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Most landed-cost models treat duty as money gone. Drawback treats it as money lent. U.S. Customs and Border Protection (CBP) pays back certain duties, taxes and fees when imported goods are exported or destroyed [1][3].

The refund has a cap, and it is high. A rule in title 19 of the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) says the sum "will not exceed 99 percent" of the duties, taxes and fees paid [2]. The law behind it sets the same 99 percent [3].

Then there is substitution. An importer can match a duty-paid import to an export of the same eight-digit tariff code, even when the imported goods never left [3]. The match can reach back five years from the import date [3].

My read: this is why a reshoring case so often overstates itself. The duty it promises to kill is, on the exported share, a duty the importer could already recover.

What changed is the size of the prize. Today's stacked tariffs are big, so 99 percent of a big number is big. Section 301 and Section 201 duties — the extra tariffs on Chinese goods and on specific imports — are claimable for drawback [4].

The share that is not recoverable is the share that stays home. Goods that land and sell in the United States never leave, so they never qualify.

## By the numbers

- **99% — Refund cap:** drawback pays back up to 99% of the duties, taxes and fees paid on goods that are exported or destroyed [2][3].
- **5 years — Matching window:** a duty-paid import can be matched to an export of the same eight-digit code for up to five years from the import date [3].
- **19 U.S.C. 1313 — The authority:** the drawback law, with rules at title 19 of the CFR; Section 301 and Section 201 duties are claimable [1][4].
- **Section 232 — The carve-out:** the steel, aluminum and copper tariffs, plus some emergency-powers duties, are generally excluded [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The operators I'd expect to move first can already prove a matching export trail. My read: the paper, not the tariff schedule, is the real gate.

- **The export share:** only the exported or destroyed part of an import carries a refund. I'd want to know what share of a given part actually leaves.
- **The paper trail:** drawback is a proof standard. A claim needs the entry record, the export or destruction record, and a match between them [1][4].
- **The program test:** eligibility is decided tariff by tariff. Section 301 is generally in; Section 232 and some emergency-powers duties are out [4][5].
- **The clock:** the window runs five years from the import date [3]. A claim filed late is a claim never filed.

The field friction is familiar. A casting quoted at $18 a unit offshore can land at $24.80 once freight, demurrage and audit travel are counted, as the desk's own field notes have it. In another case, a customs reclassification produced a retroactive $1.2M duty bill. Both are the kind of hit a refund window cushions.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

Drawback reaches only the exported share. A reshoring case built on domestic demand gets no help, because those goods never leave.

The newest tariffs are the least likely to qualify. Section 232 metals and some emergency-powers duties sit outside the program [5]. That exclusion is one vendor's reading of current practice, dated June 2026, not a fixed rule [5].

I could be wrong, but the shape of the trap is what sticks. The office that models a move often nets out duty savings the firm could have refunded without moving a machine.

A reshore still buys real things: shorter lead times, a second plant, less exposure to one port. The part worth arguing about is the duty saving, and that is smaller than most models say.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Drawback refunds up to 99% of the duty paid on imported goods later exported or destroyed. Substitution lets a duty-paid import be matched to an export of the same tariff code for up to five years.
- **Why It Matters:** A reshoring case that credits the move with escaping a tariff is overstating the saving. The recoverable share of that tariff never needed a new factory.
- **What I'd Watch:** Where the recoverable duty really sits in a supply chain.
  - **The export share:** only the part of an import that is exported or destroyed carries a refund; goods sold in the United States do not qualify.
  - **The proof chain:** a claim needs the entry record, the export or destruction record, and a match between them — without all three, there is no refund.
  - **The program test:** eligibility is decided tariff by tariff, with Section 232 metals and some emergency-powers duties generally excluded.
- **The Catch:** Drawback reaches only the exported share, the newest tariffs are the most likely to be carved out, and that exclusion is current practice as of June 2026, not a fixed rule [5].
