<!-- lead -->
The same winter now bills a household $686 or $2,977, and the only thing that picks is the fuel the house already burns [1]. On November 10 the National Energy Assistance Directors Association (NEADA) released its winter forecast and priced the season by fuel [1]. A natural-gas household pays $686, exactly as it did last year. A heating-oil household pays $2,977, which is 70.2 percent more than last winter [1]. The average behind those numbers is $1,002, up 7.1 percent, and it is nearly useless to plan with, because it blends bills that sit 4.3 times apart.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
NEADA's latest winter forecast prices heating four ways, and the gap between the cheapest and the dearest fuel has never been wider. What a homeowner should do this season depends almost entirely on which fuel the invoice names.

<!-- tension -->
## How the spread happened
What I keep circling is how fast NEADA's own winter forecast swung. NEADA's September cut put the season at $1,030 per household, up 8.7 percent [3]. The November revision walks the average back to $1,002, yet re-prices one fuel violently [1]. Oil jumps 70.2 percent while gas holds at zero and electricity adds just 6.0 percent [1].

The mechanism is supply, not the thermostat. Heating oil tracks global diesel markets, and NEADA names the Strait of Hormuz as the reason it refuses to call this decline a trend [1]. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) shows the same caution in its weekly data: residential heating oil slipped 1.5 percent to $6.14 a gallon in the week ending November 2, and still sat about 69 percent above the same week a year earlier [2]. Electricity climbs for a separate reason — rising rates, data-center demand and grid upgrades — and that line is up 32.1 percent since the winter of 2021–22, against 110.1 percent for oil [1]. So the oil household faces a bill that can pass a major repair, while the electric household's season costs $69 more than last year's [1].

## By the numbers
- **$2,977:** the projected season bill for a household that heats with oil [1].
- **$686:** the projected season bill for a natural-gas household, flat against last winter [1].
- **$23 billion:** what households behind on utility payments already owe — one in six of them [1].

## Where the season lands on you
Here is what I find hard to square: the same winterize checklist gets sold to every homeowner, while its payoff differs by a factor of eighteen. NEADA's directors are planning for the bad case, asking Congress for an added $3 billion for the Low Income Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP), the federal bill-help fund [1]. Their reasoning is blunt: states should assume prices stay high all season [1]. The Northeast, where the oil-heated homes cluster, takes the sharpest regional hit, $1,532 per household, up 16.9 percent [1].

For the household, the fuel list orders itself. An oil-heated home now faces a season whose bill rivals a repair, so the cheap acts earn real money: the annual service visit, sealed leaks, a strict thermostat schedule [1]. The case for leaving oil also needs fresh pricing, because the replacement path was itself repriced in the November manufacturer price lists [1]. A gas household's bill is flat, so the savings a heat pump was sold on do not exist on that invoice yet [1]. What I would watch next is the EIA weekly series. If the $6.14 gallon holds through December, the oil forecast stops being a scare number and becomes the budget. ## The catch in the number
What I would not do is treat $2,977 as a price you can fight. It is a projected seasonal expenditure — an assumed volume of oil at an assumed price — and NEADA's own release shows how soft those assumptions are, citing prices that fell after its basis week [1]. The honest comparison sits on your own meter: last winter's actual gallons at this winter's price. The 2026–27 lesson is narrower than the headline. The fuel mix you inherited decided most of this winter for you, and every other choice on the table is small against it.
