<!-- lead -->
Your nonrenewal letter isn't final, and the count is the part that pays. Florida gives a homeowner a 120-day runway before a home policy can end, and the letter has to say why [1]. California sets 75 days [2]. Washington sets 60 days to cancel [4]. Each number runs back from the date the policy would stop, so a carrier that mails late keeps the old policy alive at the old price.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The letter rarely follows a claim. A carrier re-scores a regional wildfire model, and a household that paid for twenty years becomes a line in a file. The part I keep circling: the bill is the cost you see, and the ending is the one that can reach the mortgage.

Lawmakers have treated that risk as a fairness problem rather than a pricing one, so the exit is written as a sequence. A carrier must give a reason, hand it over inside a set window, and live with the result when it misses. The result is not a fine, but more of the same cover at the same price for as long as the letter came up short.

Most owners read the notice as the end and start shopping that afternoon. The law treats it as a starting gun, and the days between the letter and the end date are the only edge in the file.

## By the numbers

- **120 days — Florida notice:** A home policy cannot be nonrenewed, cancelled or ended without written notice at least 120 days before the date it stops, and the letter must give the reason [1].
- **75 days — California notice:** The letter must go out at least 75 days before the policy ends, and a late one leaves the old policy in force for 75 days from the day it was mailed [2].
- **60 days — Washington cancellation:** Written notice must arrive at least 60 days before the date cover stops, and it must state the carrier's real reason for cancelling [4].
- **60 days — California cancellation bar:** Once a home policy has run 60 days, a mid-term cancel holds only on a short list of grounds, from nonpayment to a physical change that makes the house uninsurable [3].

## What I'd watch:

I read the three laws as one question, and the question is a date. The rate talk in the letter matters less than the postmark on the envelope.

- **The reason line:** Florida and Washington want the reason itself, and Washington says the real reason, while California wants the specific reason or reasons [1][2][4]. A letter with no reason is not the letter the law describes.
- **What California adds:** The letter must print the state insurance department's web address, its phone number and its Los Angeles street address, which is the state pointing the reader at a place to complain [2].
- **The fast lane:** A missed payment cuts the clock to ten days in Florida and in Washington [1][4]. Every other exit in these states gets weeks.
- **The fix for a late letter:** In Florida the cover stays in force at the same price until a new policy starts, or until a stretch as long as the missed notice has run after the letter finally lands [1]. In California the old policy runs on, same terms, for 75 days from the mailing [2].
- **Offer or exit:** California splits the two, and a carrier must send one or the other at least 45 days before the policy ends [2]. That 45 is a second clock, and a letter that meets it can still miss the 75.

## The catch

My read is that this is real edge, but less than it looks. Three states sit in this evidence set, and their numbers should never be blended into a national rule. Florida uses 120 days, California 75, Washington 60 on a cancel, and each figure sits inside its own wording.

A missed clock buys time at the same price, not free cover. Florida holds the policy only until a new one starts [1], and California holds it for 75 days from the mail date [2]. Neither state turns a botched letter into a free year, and neither stops a carrier from setting the next price where it likes once the window is met.

There is also a known way around the clock. Florida lets a home insurer cancel or nonrenew on 45 days' notice when the state insurance office finds an early exit is needed to protect policyholders, say on the carrier's finances or its hurricane cover [1]. A state can shorten the runway the law otherwise gives.

What none of this touches is price. These rules govern how a policy ends, not what the next one costs, and a household facing a 29.1 percent jump in a state pool has a different problem from one holding a bad letter. I would treat the clock as the piece of an insurance exit an owner can check without a lawyer.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A nonrenewal or a cancel is a process with a notice window set by state law, not a letter that ends cover on the day it prints. Florida asks for 120 days, California 75 on a nonrenewal, Washington 60 on a cancel.
- **Why It Matters:** An ending can reach the mortgage, and a carrier that mails late keeps the old policy alive at the old price. That gap is the only edge an owner holds in the file.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The reason line:** Whether the letter gives the reason, since Florida and Washington require it and California requires the specific reasons [1][2][4].
  - **The late-letter fix:** Whether the carrier mailed inside the window, because a late letter adds weeks of cover rather than voiding the policy [1][2].
  - **The ten-day lane:** Whether the letter cites a missed payment, the one exit that skips the long clock in Florida and Washington [1][4].
- **The Catch:** These rules cover timing, not price, and Florida's insurance office can approve an earlier exit on 45 days' notice [1]. Three states appear here, and their figures do not travel.
