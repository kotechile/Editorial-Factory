<!-- lead -->
Your $30 exception bill is wrong, and the error is not in the decimal place.

A vendor's own worked example puts one order exception at 45 minutes of a customer-service rep's day. The 2025 median wage for an order clerk is $22.20 an hour. Forty-five minutes of that wage is $16.65 [4]. The other $13.35 is not explained on the page that prints the $30, and I will not invent it for them [2].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

An order that needs a human is expensive. The cost never lands on one line of the income statement.

Go Autonomous sells exception automation. It puts the split at 20% to 40% of order volume and 60% to 80% of total order-management cost [1].

My read: that is a vendor's claim. The page shows no sample and no method. The shape of it is still worth sitting with.

The same page runs a made-up shop at 400 orders a day. A 25% exception rate is 100 exceptions a day. At 45 minutes each, that is 75 hours of rep time before anyone touches freight, credit or the customer [1].

And the labour is the small part. The vendor argues that visible rep hours are only 30% to 40% of the total cost [1]. The rest is revenue that moves later, orders that get re-keyed, and customers who take the next order somewhere else.

That last piece has a peer-reviewed echo. The Journal of Retailing, as cited by Moxo, found that fulfilment failures delay the next customer order by 7.22% on average [2].

I did not read the paper. The number arrives second-hand and should be held that way.

The practical effect is blunt. If one order in four breaks, your service level is not set by your warehouse or your carrier. It is set by the queue that repairs the break.

## By the numbers

- **20% to 40% — Share of orders:** the vendor's estimate of order volume that needs manual repair, against 60% to 80% of total order-management cost [1].
- **45 minutes — Handle time per exception:** the vendor's worked example, which turns 100 daily exceptions into 75 hours of rep time [1].
- **$16.65 — Wage per touch:** 45 minutes at the 2025 median order-clerk wage of $22.20 an hour [4].
- **$54.50 — Cost at the 75th percentile:** what benchmarking by APQC (the American Productivity & Quality Center) shows a purchase order costs to process, against about $14 at the 25th percentile and $42 on average [3].

## What I'd watch:

The vendor's scoring card is more useful than its headline cost figure. It tells you what failure looks like from the inside [1].

The card sets three marks. An exception rate above 20% of order volume. Resolution time averaging over 4 hours. Reps opening 3 or more systems to close one case.

I would want those three numbers from my own desk before believing any automation case.

- **Which class recurs.** The five types the vendor names are pricing mismatch, quantity discrepancy, master-data gaps, authorization thresholds and delivery constraints [1]. My read: two are data problems that stop when the data is fixed. Two are policy problems that need a threshold, not a person. One is a capacity problem that no queue can absorb.
- **Whether headcount moves the rate.** Adding reps shortens the queue and leaves the rate where it was. That is the vendor's argument and it is also mine, so I hold it loosely.
- **The spread across firms.** The same benchmarking data runs from about $14 to $54.50 for one transaction [3]. A fourfold gap on one process is a design gap, not a wage gap. It also means one published per-touch cost cannot be yours.
- **Where the money moves next.** A delayed confirmation pushes the invoice. The invoice pushes the cash. On a 400-order day, 100 exceptions is a lot of invoicing sitting behind a queue [1].

The tempting move is to point an agent at the queue and count the cases it closes. I would want to know which classes it closes. The mix is the whole argument.

## The catch

The load-bearing ratios here come from a company that sells the fix. There is no sample size, no named client and no method on that page. The $30 has even less behind it: one line, no working [2].

The APQC figures are real benchmarking. But they measure a purchase order, not a sales order. I have used them as the nearest published per-transaction number, not as your number [3].

A purchase order and a sales order share a desk and not much else.

Then there is the control problem. A credit hold and a price override are money decisions wearing an operational costume. An agent that clears a hold by releasing it has overridden a credit policy. That is a control failure before it is a saving — my position, not a finding.

Autonomy belongs on the classes where the rule is fixed and the money at risk has a ceiling. The rest need a threshold that someone signed.

None of this makes the queue cheaper by itself. It only shows you which part of it you are paying for twice.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The cost of a manual order exception is quoted as a flat vendor number, about $30. The part you can check is a wage times a handle time: 45 minutes at $22.20 an hour is $16.65 [2][4].
- **Why It Matters:** Exceptions are 20% to 40% of order volume and, on the vendor's own numbers, 60% to 80% of order-management cost [1]. That queue is what your service level is really reporting — not the warehouse, and not the carrier.
- **What I'd Watch:** Three signals that decide whether automation earns its budget at your desk.
  - **Exception rate:** the share of orders that need a human. Above 20% is the vendor's own mark for a structural problem [1].
  - **Handle time:** minutes per exception. Forty-five is the vendor's example. Yours is the number that matters [1].
  - **Class mix:** which of the five types recurs. Data fixes stop, policy fixes need a threshold, capacity does not stop [1].
- **The Catch:** The ratios and the $30 come from a vendor with no published method. The APQC benchmark measures purchase orders, not sales orders. And an agent allowed to clear credit holds is a control failure before it is a saving.
