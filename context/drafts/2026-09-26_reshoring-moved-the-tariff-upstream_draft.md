Coca-Cola just committed $10 billion to U.S. infrastructure through 2030, and its CFO wants you to know it is not a tariff hedge [1]. John Murphy told Fortune the company already keeps 98 cents of every dollar spent on its beverages inside the U.S. economy — "leaving little room for reshoring" [1]. The same week, roughly $3.1 billion of the U.S.–Canada plastics trade landed under an all-in duty of about 53–55% [2]. That is the part worth circling: the biggest reshoring headline of the quarter comes from a company that says it has nothing left to reshore, while the tariff quietly migrated upstream into the packaging.

<!-- tension -->
## The Tariff Moved Upstream

**The big picture:** Reshoring has been sold as a finished-good decision — where to assemble the bottle, the circuit board, the appliance. The policy reality has moved on. On July 20, the U.S. ordered an extra 50% tariff on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods under Section 338 of the 1930 Tariff Act, a provision unused for a century that overrides USMCA free-trade protections [2]. For plastics, Rabobank estimates roughly $3.1 billion of the ~$15 billion U.S.–Canada trade is now exposed [2].

**Why it matters:** Section 338 stacks on top of the base duty, pushing covered items to ~53–55% all-in — even goods that qualify for duty-free USMCA treatment [2]. So a company that moved final assembly to Ohio still buys closures, film, and molds that cross a tariff-torn border. Rabobank's framing is blunt: "Tariff whiplash is the baseline. For plastics, the operating challenge is no longer the rate; it is the volatility" [2]. What strikes me is how that reframes the whole reshoring playbook. The tariff is no longer a cost you dodge by moving the factory — it is a moving layer that chases your inputs.

**By the numbers:**
- **$10 billion:** Coca-Cola system's U.S. infrastructure investment planned for 2026–2030, most of it bottling-partner capex [1].
- **98 cents:** the share of every Coca-Cola beverage dollar that already stays inside the U.S. economy — the CFO's reason "there's little room for reshoring" [1].
- **~$3.1 billion:** the slice of the ~$15 billion U.S.–Canada plastics trade now exposed to the Section 338 tariff [2].
- **~53–55%:** the all-in duty on covered plastic items once Section 338 stacks on the base rate [2].

## Where This Bites

**Where this bites:** The operators closest to the story are re-costing the bill of materials, not the factory.

- **Input landed cost, not SKU cost:** Rabobank found the tariff can change faster than a purchase order can be repriced, so committing to a fixed price on resin or packaging is now a bet against the tariff regime [2]. The people I hear from are re-running landed cost at the resin, mold, and closure level.
- **The "domestic" claim is not a shield:** Coca-Cola's 98-cent figure is the exception that proves the rule — most manufacturers are nowhere near it, so "Made in the USA" assembly can still carry a tariff-heavy input stack [1][2].
- **HTS-code hygiene over headline-chasing:** converters are still sorting out which packaging lines the tariff actually covers, because Section 338's annexes are narrow today but can be extended without a formal investigation or comment period [2].
- **The reshoring wave is real, but the margin is thinner than it looks:** 36% of OEMs say they have reshored or are actively doing it, and 63% are planning U.S. capex for it [3] — yet that capex buys capacity, not tariff immunity.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The Catch

**The catch:** This is not a claim that reshoring is pointless. Section 338's current annexes are narrow — Rabobank says most resin and packaging formats sit outside the list today, so "many converters see no immediate duty change" [2]. The exposure is real but concentrated, and the tariff's legal basis is largely untested [2].

My read: the durable shift is not the specific 50% on polyethylene bags. It is that the planning problem has changed shape. A decision engine that stops at the finished-good landing is now looking at the wrong number. The load-bearing question has moved to what your Tier-2 and Tier-3 inputs cost, tariff-adjusted, across a border that keeps rewriting its own rules.

I've been reading the reshoring coverage all week, and one pairing keeps sticking with me.

On Sept. 15, Coca-Cola committed $10 billion to U.S. infrastructure through 2030 — and its CFO, John Murphy, told Fortune it is "a growth play, not a tariff hedge," because 98 cents of every dollar the system spends on its beverages already stays inside the U.S. economy. There's almost nothing left to reshore.

The same week, Rabobank reported that roughly $3.1 billion of the U.S.–Canada plastics trade is now under an all-in duty of about 53–55% — a 50% Section 338 surcharge that stacks on top of the base rate and overrides USMCA free-trade terms.

My read: reshoring didn't kill tariff risk. It moved it upstream, into the packaging, the resin, and the molds that go into the thing you assemble in Ohio. "Made in the USA" assembly can still carry a tariff-heavy input bill.

I could be wrong — Rabobank notes Section 338's current annexes are narrow, so most converters see no immediate change. But the list can be extended without a formal investigation or comment period, and a tariff that changes faster than a purchase order can be repriced is a planning problem, not just a cost problem.

What I'm watching next: whether sourcing teams re-cost at the resin, mold, and closure level — the bill of materials — instead of stopping at the finished-good landing. Curious how others are handling it.
