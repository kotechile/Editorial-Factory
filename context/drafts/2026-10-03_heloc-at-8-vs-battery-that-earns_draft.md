<!-- lead -->
The same week the average home-equity line of credit (HELOC) hit 8.194% [1], California signed two bills that let customer-owned batteries get paid for pushing power onto the grid when it is under strain [3]. Two numbers just started pointing in opposite directions for anyone deciding where a home-improvement dollar should go.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Borrowing against a home has rarely been this expensive relative to what a financed improvement returns. The 30-year fixed mortgage hit 7.30%, its highest since November 2023, and refinance applications fell 9% in a week and sit 56% below a year ago [2]. I've been watching the rate side of this all week, and the quieter number is the HELOC: at 8.194%, a remodel funded with borrowed money has to clear an 8% hurdle before it pays for itself [1].

Meanwhile home values are barely keeping up with — actually, lagging — inflation. The Federal Housing Finance Agency (FHFA) index rose 2.6% over the past year, and the S&P Case-Shiller national index rose 1.9%, against roughly 3.4% inflation [4]. A kitchen redo that used to "come back" at resale no longer reliably does, because the house itself is appreciating about zero in real terms.

Then there is the other side. On September 30, Governor Newsom signed SB 913 and SB 905, which let networks of customer-owned batteries and flexible appliances relieve the grid during the handful of hours each year that drive the most cost [3]. SB 913 directs the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) to set a valuation method for customer batteries that export power during grid stress, so they can qualify for resource adequacy (RA) — being counted, and paid, for the power they can actually dispatch [3].

My read: the cost of capital and the return on a home asset moved in opposite directions in the same week. That re-ranks the HELOC-versus-cash decision this whole beat is built around.

## By the numbers

- **8.194% — Average HELOC rate:** the national average home-equity line of credit, per Mortgage Research Center data reviewed by Fortune on Oct 1, with 10-year home-equity loans at 8.164% and 15-year at 8.503% [1].
- **7.30% — 30-year fixed mortgage:** the highest since November 2023, up from 7.12% the prior week; refinance applications fell 9% week-over-week and sit 56% below a year ago [2].
- **300,000+ — California home batteries:** the number of customers with solar-charged batteries, growing by 2,000 a week, now eligible under SB 913 to be valued for what they export during grid stress [3].
- **+2.6% / +1.9% — Home price gains:** FHFA and Case-Shiller annual appreciation, respectively, both below the ~3.4% inflation rate — meaning homes are losing value in real terms [4].

## What I'd watch:

What the people closest to this are doing, rather than what I would tell anyone to do:

- **California battery owners are already enrolling:** more than 300,000 customers hold solar-charged batteries, and 2,000 more are added each week — the fleet SB 913 now routes toward resource-adequacy payments is the largest in the country [3].
- **Aggregators are positioning for June 30, 2027:** SB 913 gives the CPUC until then to write the export-valuation methodology, so the companies that bundle home batteries into virtual power plants (VPPs) are watching how the next governor's appointees write those rules [3].
- **Borrowers are shifting to ARMs to dodge the fixed rate:** adjustable-rate mortgages hit 10.3% of applications, their highest share since October 2025, as borrowers look for a cheaper front-end rate than the 7.30% fixed [2].

The part I keep circling: the decision this forces is not really "remodel or battery." It is whether a dollar borrowed at 8% into a home that is not appreciating in real terms is a better use of capital than a dollar put into an asset that now earns from the grid. I'd want to see the June 30, 2027 valuation rules before I believed the battery side fully penciled out — but the direction of the two curves is hard to miss.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

I could be wrong about how fast this bites. SB 913 is a bill signed, not a check written: the actual export values, fleet measurement rules, and enrollment mechanics all get decided by the next governor's CPUC appointees in the first half of the new term [3]. A battery only earns if those rules are written to pay it, and a homeowner outside California gets none of this revenue today — the VPP economics here are California-specific.

And the "cash beats borrowed" read has its own limits. Plenty of homeowners do not have the cash to fund a battery outright, and paying down an 8% HELOC is itself a guaranteed, tax-free 8% return in a way no battery revenue stream is. The honest version of this story is not "batteries beat remodels" — it is that the math has tightened in a way that makes financing a purely cosmetic improvement much harder to defend.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Home-equity borrowing repriced to near three-year highs — HELOC 8.194%, 30-year fixed 7.30% — in the same week California signed SB 913 and SB 905, which let customer batteries get paid for exporting power when the grid is stressed. Borrowing to improve a home got more expensive exactly as one home asset gained a revenue stream.
- **Why It Matters:** With home appreciation (2.6% FHFA / 1.9% Case-Shiller) now below ~3.4% inflation, a remodel financed at 8% no longer reliably recaptures value at resale. The marginal home-improvement dollar is shifting from borrowed, non-earning improvements toward cash-funded assets that earn from the grid.
- **What I'd Watch:** whether California's next set of utility regulators writes export values that make the battery math work, and whether other states copy the model.
  - **CPUC export valuation:** the rulemaking, due by June 30, 2027, that sets how much a customer battery is paid for power pushed onto the grid during stress.
  - **VPP aggregation:** companies bundling thousands of home batteries into one dispatchable resource that bids into the wholesale market.
  - **HELOC and mortgage rates:** whether the 8%-plus borrowing cost holds, or eases back and reopens the case for financed remodels.
- **The Catch:** SB 913 is signed but not yet implemented — the payouts depend on rules the next governor's appointees write, and the revenue is California-only for now. Paying down an 8% HELOC is also a guaranteed return that no battery revenue can promise.
