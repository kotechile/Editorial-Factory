<!-- lead -->
A senior engineer kept working from Spain, and the contract authority his employer gave him built the company a permanent establishment — treaty law's term for a branch-like presence that makes a foreign company taxable where it sits. The back-dated corporate tax, fines and forced social-security bills ran to about $110,000 on this desk's field note. The tax problem your remote work creates is not always yours. It can land on your employer, and the tests that decide it are not the residency clock every guide repeats.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
This article is about the tax and social-security risk your employer takes on when you work abroad. It concerns remote workers and their companies. Four tests decide it — three on days or contract authority, one on a 25% activity share — and none is the personal residency clock.

<!-- tension -->
## The clocks your employer answers to
Every expat guide talks about you: whether 183 days somewhere makes you a resident who files a personal return there. The [state residency exit carries its own clock set](/move-abroad-still-a-resident-546-days/), and even that piece stops at the personal layer. What I find hard to square is how often a move gets cleared on the personal clock alone. Your employer faces a different question: does the company itself carry on business in the host country? The answer turns on who signs contracts and who sells to local buyers, and four tests decide it.

The narrowest test protects a short stay. US law does not count a travelling employee's services against the company that sent them, so the visit does not make the company taxable in the US. The carve-out applies when the employee stays in the US no more than 90 days in the tax year and the pay for those services stays under $3,000 [1]. That is a statutory de minimis — a smallness line below which the law ignores the activity. It is as generous as the US side gets.

The host country's test is wider. HM Revenue and Customs (HMRC), the UK tax authority, states in its treaty guidance that the services carve-out normally needs a person present more than 183 days in any 12-month period, and the services must be sold to third parties [2]. Read the second half closely. An engineer who works only on the company's own product may never meet it. A founder who invoices local customers meets it the day the first payment clears.

The third test catches senior people, and it counts no days at all. Treaty law builds a dependent-agent presence when someone in the host country habitually exercises an authority to sign contracts for the enterprise [3]. HMRC's manual paraphrases the clause as a person who "habitually plays the principal role leading to the conclusion of contracts". An engineer on a tourist visa holds no such authority and stays invisible. A vice-president who signs vendor deals carries it from day one.

A fourth regime sits under the income-tax layer. European social-security coordination asks which country covers a worker active in two or more member states, the test counts shares, not days. When less than 25% of your activity stays in your home country, that low share signals you work mostly elsewhere [4]. Fall below the line and you may not be insured where you live. The coverage follows the work, not the address.

## By the numbers
- The US carve-out needs a stay of 90 days or less in the tax year [1].
- The services test needs presence beyond 183 days in any 12-month window [2].
- The social-security share sits at 25% of home-country activity [4].

## Where this bites
The advisers who handle this daily check two facts about a remote hire before anything else. One: what can the employee sign? Two: does the host country see customers of the company? Both are design questions, and reorganising signing authority costs less than a back-dated corporate bill. The 25% share is measured on turnover, work time, services and income together, so a part-time worker can drift across the line without any wrong turn. What I watch for is the quiet upgrade of a remote contractor into someone who "can approve deals" — that phrase, in human-resources language, is treaty language too.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch
None of these tests protects a company from its own employee's seniority. The 183-day services test and the 90-day US carve-out both count days. The contract-authority test counts none — it recognises power instead. That gap is the honest limit of every digital-nomad visa pitch. The visa solves your right to be there. It says nothing about the presence your role can build for the company paying you. What I find sobering is that the failure behind this desk's field note involved no fraud and no clever structure — only an executive title, a sales season, and a test nobody checked.
