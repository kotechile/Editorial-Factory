Home insurance now beats the property tax bill in two-thirds of U.S. counties, by an average of 14 percent, according to a Bloomberg Intelligence study published on October 19. Tomorrow, voters in seven states decide 12 property tax measures. Florida's Amendment 3 and Oklahoma's State Question 847 are the big ones. None of them touches the premium. [1] [2] [3]

Both bills land on the same household budget, and only one has kept pace with inflation. Property taxes have risen only slightly more than inflation since 2020, the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy reported on October 21, even as home prices climbed more than 50 percent. [4]

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The revolt is real. It is aimed at the slower of the two lines.

What moved the premium is weather and how insurers price risk, not any vote. Hail and winter storms are driving rates up in the Midwest and Mountain West, led by Colorado, Utah and Minnesota. In the Southeast, premiums now run 2.5 to 4 times the tax levy. Over a decade, homeowner policies are up 45 percent after inflation. [1]

What the ballot offers is a transfer, not a cut. A town sets its levy by the revenue it needs, so shielding part of a home's value pushes the rest of the bill onto renters, second homes and commercial property. The Tax Foundation says as much about this year's measures, and it is why the group prefers a levy limit, the North Carolina shape, to a bigger exemption. [2]

Florida is the cleanest test. Amendment 3 lifts the homestead exemption for non-school taxes to $150,000 next year and $250,000 in 2028, while the $25,000 school exemption stays put. In the Lincoln Institute's example, a $275,000 home would owe city or county tax on $25,000 of value. The state's exemption now tops out at $50,000, and Adam Langley of the Lincoln Institute puts a workable statewide figure at $20,000 to $40,000. Florida's version is odd in one way: it mandates the high number with no state money to make towns whole. [2] [4]

What I keep circling is the mismatch. A voter can move the tax. Nobody votes on the premium, and the premium is the line that answers to risk.

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## By the numbers

- **14% — Insurance over tax:** At the county level, owners pay 14 percent more for insurance than for property tax; add weather-driven auto cover and the gap reaches 30 percent. [1]
- **75% — Counties where the pair wins:** With car cover included, the combined bill tops the property tax in 75 percent of U.S. counties. [1]
- **79% — Nebraska's decade:** The state's homeowner policies rose 79 percent after inflation, against 45 percent nationally. [1]
- **1.75% — Oklahoma's new cap:** State Question 847 cuts the yearly cap on assessed homestead value from 3 percent to 1.75 percent, and on other property from 5 percent to 4 percent. [2]

## What I'd watch:

Three things decide whether the relief shows up as money.

- **The county, not the country.** Two-thirds of counties is an average of averages. The 14 percent gap is small where taxes are high and wide where they are low, which is why the Southeast looks nothing like New Jersey. [1] [4]
- **The levy behind the exemption.** A bigger exemption changes who pays, and town budgets still have to clear. Property taxes fund 28.4 percent of local spending nationally, more than half of it in New Hampshire, Connecticut, New Jersey and Maine, and under 15 percent in Arkansas, Alabama and Nevada. The pressure lands unevenly. [4]
- **The repair backlog.** The capital that would lower the premium is going elsewhere. A LendingTree survey published October 26 found 34 percent of owners delayed a repair in the past two years. Of those who still need work, 32 percent cannot afford it, 23 percent say quotes are too high and 3 percent blame the difficulty of getting a loan. The median project costs $2,113 after inflation, up 76.1 percent since 2015. A professional job runs $4,103; the same work done by the owner runs $845. [5] [6]

I'd want to know whether insurers credit the work. A roof and a water shut-off are the two items that turn up in claims, and [the measured cost of one slow leak](/silent-15400-home-loss/) is the kind of loss the premium is priced against.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The comparison is a county average, and averages hide the two cases that matter. In a high-tax Northeast county, the tax bill still dwarfs the premium. In a low-tax Sun Belt county, the premium can run three times the tax, and no exemption closes that gap. [1] [4]

There is also a timing problem. All 12 measures are undecided as of today. A homestead exemption that passes in Florida does not lower a levy; it moves one. If town budgets hold, the relief for a $275,000 home arrives as a smaller share of a bill that keeps growing. The owner who expected a cut sees a smaller increase. [2] [4]

I could be wrong, but the revolt may be healthier than its target. Property taxes are the one housing cost a voter can move, and 45 of 58 such measures passed from 2020 through 2025. That record says less about the size of the bill than about how badly people want a lever they control. [3]

## At a glance

**The shift:** Twelve property-tax measures reach ballots tomorrow; insurance, now the larger bill in two-thirds of counties, is on none of them.
**The number:** 14 percent — the average premium's lead over property tax.
**The risk:** An exemption moves a levy rather than cutting it.
