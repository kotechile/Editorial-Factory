<!-- lead -->
The fast rung of the new federal exclusion costs 15.9 percent of the gain, because it leaves only half of that gain out of income. The other half is taxed at a maximum 28 percent, and a 3.8 percent surtax on net investment income lands on top once income clears its threshold [4][5]. Your shares reach that 3-year rung only if a 30-day election put the whole grant on one clock on the day it was issued [2][3].

The table behind that sum is new. Pub. L. 119-21 rewrote section 1202 in July 2025 and swapped one rule for three: a shorter hold now buys part of the break, and the full break still needs the old five years [1].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture

For shares acquired after July 4, 2025, three years of holding buys half the exclusion, four years buys three quarters, and five years still buys all of it [1]. Shares bought before that date keep the older deal, which needs five years for the whole break and gives nothing at three [1]. The rewrite is short, and the money it moves is large.

What I find hard to square is the price of the fast rung. A reader who sells at three years has a bill about half the size of an ordinary long-term holder's, not none of it, because the taxable half is taxed at up to 28 percent rather than the 20 percent most long-term gains carry [4]. That 28 percent is a cap, so a reader in a lower bracket pays less. It is still a cap on this gain, not the usual rate.

Then there is the part that shuts the door. To qualify, the company's gross assets must stay under $75,000,000 before the shares are issued and right after, with the money the sale itself raised counted [1]. A large round can therefore close the window on options exercised the next month, since the cash and the shares arrive together.

## By the numbers

- **$15,000,000 — per-issuer cap:** the gain one person can leave out of income from a single company is capped at fifteen million dollars, cut by whatever was already excluded from that company in earlier years [1].
- **10 times — the other cap:** the limit is the greater of that dollar figure or ten times the adjusted basis of the shares sold, and basis is set at original issue, ignoring later additions [1]. Ten times basis only beats the dollar cap above $1,500,000 of basis, which is a sum on the two figures the statute states.
- **$75,000,000 — gross-assets ceiling:** read off the balance sheet before the issuance and again right after it, counting the money the round raised [1].
- **30 days — filing window:** the election goes to the Internal Revenue office where the person files a return, with a copy to the employer, and the rule lets it be filed before the transfer [2].

## What I'd watch

My read is that this is three choices wearing one tax label, and only the last is about the Internal Revenue Service. The people closest to this material, founders who bought restricted stock early and engineers weighing an early exercise, tend to argue about the rate and skip the clock.

- **The vesting calendar:** with no election, each tranche's holding period starts when it vests, so the last shares of a four-year monthly grant reach the five-year rung 108 months after the grant. With an election, the whole block starts at transfer and reaches it at 60 [3]. The filing buys four years of clock, priced at ordinary income on the spread at grant.
- **The balance sheet, not the market:** headcount and valuation say nothing about whether the shares qualify; the test is gross assets at the moment of issuance [1]. The round that makes a grant feel valuable is often the one that ends the run for anyone who has not exercised.
- **The original-issue test:** shares bought from a colleague in a secondary sale or a tender are not acquired at original issue, so they never qualified, however long they are held [6]. That is a different question from the one most secondary buyers ask, which is only about price.
- **The other clock:** a reader who leaves before exercising faces the window priced in [Options Trap: The Bill After You Leave](/post-termination-option-clock-tax/), and the two windows are not the same thing. One decides whether the shares exist at all; the other decides which rung they reach.

## The catch

What I keep circling is that the part rung is a trade, not a gift, and the trade can lose. Waiting from three years to five is worth 15.9 points of gain, so holding pays only if the position keeps about 84 percent of its value over those two years. A private company can lose all of it, and one reader's washed-out common stock in a down-round sale is the shape of that risk rather than a forecast.

The next caveat is the list of excluded trades. Section 1202 leaves out service firms in health, law, engineering, architecture, accounting, consulting and several others, plus any business whose main asset is the skill or name of its staff [1]. An engineer holding shares in an engineering services firm has to check what the company actually did, because a job title decides nothing.

The last is that an election is not free. It turns a maybe into a tax bill at grant, and if the shares are later lost while still unvested the rule treats that as a sale at a loss equal to what was paid [2]. A loss is not a refund, and the tax already paid on that grant does not come back on its own.

## At a glance

**The shift:** Section 1202 pays a part exclusion at three years for shares issued after July 4, 2025, where it once paid only the full one at five.
**The number:** 15.9 percent of the gain still goes to tax at that first rung.
**The risk:** the shares qualify only if the company's gross assets were under the ceiling on the day they were issued.
