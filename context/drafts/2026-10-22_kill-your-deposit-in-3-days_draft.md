<!-- lead -->
A remodel contract signed at your kitchen table comes with three days to cancel and get the deposit back. The same contract signed at a contractor's showroom, after you visited that showroom first, comes with none. The difference is not the price, the crew, or the paperwork. It is the address where the sale happened.

That gap matters because the deposit is the largest sum a homeowner hands over before seeing a single day of work. Three states write a ceiling into law. California caps a down payment at the lesser of $1,000 or 10 percent of the contract amount [1]. Maryland caps it at one third [2]. Massachusetts allows one third, or more when special-order materials cost more [4]. Everywhere else, the contract decides.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The deposit exists because a builder needs money for materials and a slot on the calendar. It is also where a bad actor takes the money and leaves. That is why some states cap it while leaving the rest of the payment schedule alone.

What strikes me is that the cap and the cancellation right guard different halves of one decision. The cap limits how much can leave your account. The window decides whether you can pull it back.

Federal law sets that window, and it sets it by geography. The rule covers a sale agreed somewhere other than the seller's fixed place of business, at $25 or more if it happened at your house, or $130 or more anywhere else [5]. A sale made after earlier talks at a permanent retail location is carved out [5]. So the contractor who meets you at home sells you a contract you can undo. The one who walks you through a showroom and then hands you the paper does not.

The rule is also old and quiet. Its text has not changed since January 2017 [5]. The door-to-door salesmen it was written for are gone. The geography test they left behind still decides a kitchen remodel.

## By the numbers

- **$1,000 or 10% — California's ceiling:** A down payment may not exceed the lesser of the two, on a home improvement contract between an owner and a contractor [1].
- **One third — Maryland's ceiling:** The contract cannot require more than one third of the total price at signing, and no payment at all is allowed before the signature [2][3].
- **5 business days — Maryland's door-to-door window:** The required contract notice gives until midnight of the fifth business day to cancel, or the seventh day where the buyer is at least 65 [3].
- **Ten business days — the refund clock:** Once the seller has your cancellation notice, any payments must come back inside ten business days [5].

## What I'd watch:

The mechanics around these rules say more than the ceilings do. I've been reading the notices these contracts are meant to carry, and the tells are specific.

- **Who picks the room:** The contractor sets the meeting. One showroom visit before the paperwork is the single fact that can remove the federal window, and nothing in the contract has to mention it [5].
- **The notice beside the signature:** A covered sale must carry a cancellation statement in bold type of at least 10 points, next to the signature line [5]. A contract with no such block is not the sale the rule describes.
- **The payment ladder:** California bars any payment past the value of work performed or material delivered, once the down payment is behind you [1]. A schedule of large sums tied to dates rather than stages does not fit that section.
- **The special-order carve-out:** In Massachusetts the deposit ceiling rises to the real cost of custom or special-order materials that had to be ordered early [4]. A kitchen with a custom cabinet run is where that clause earns its keep.
- **The financed version:** In Maryland, a contract secured by an interest in the house must state a three-business-day right to rescind, in writing [3]. That notice is a contract requirement, not a courtesy.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

My read is that both guards are narrower than they look, and neither one makes a deposit safe.

The caps are state law. California, Maryland and Massachusetts write ceilings down. The pages I read say nothing about the other states, and I found no primary source that counts them. In a state with no ceiling, the owner has a contract, not a statute.

The federal window is narrower still. It turns on where the sale was agreed, and its carve-outs are ordinary: an earlier visit to a fixed retail location, a repair visit you asked for [5]. It also starts on the date of the transaction, not the date you read the fine print.

And a cap is not a promise. It limits what can leave before work starts. It does not get the money back, does not put the owner ahead of an unpaid supplier, and does not turn a bad builder into a good one. The refund duty sits with the seller, and those ten business days are the seller's clock, not yours.

That is why the room, the signature line and the payment ladder carry more weight than the percentage does. The percentage caps the loss. The other three decide whether the loss can be undone.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Two state statutes cap the deposit on a home remodel, at the lesser of $1,000 or 10 percent in California and at one third in Maryland and Massachusetts, while the federal cancellation window turns on where the contract was signed rather than what it buys.
- **Why It Matters:** The deposit is the biggest payment a homeowner makes before any work exists. A cap limits how much is exposed, and a three-business-day window is the only reversal most buyers get.
- **What I'd Watch:** Where the sale is agreed, what sits beside the signature line, and how payments are tied to work.
  - **The room:** A sale agreed at the house carries the federal window. One agreed after a showroom visit may not.
  - **The notice block:** The rule asks for a cancellation statement in bold type of at least 10 points beside the signature.
  - **The payment ladder:** California bars payment past the value of work performed or material delivered.
- **The Catch:** A cap is not a guarantee. Three states write a ceiling down, the pages say nothing about the rest, and no statute gets a paid deposit back for you.
