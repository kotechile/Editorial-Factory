<!-- lead -->
Your console rose instead of falling after memory and storage costs climbed, and the three current machines now cost $500 more together than they did at launch. The vendors passed that bill along rather than absorbing it. What decides the repair-or-replace sum is no longer the machine's age. It is whether the part still exists and whether you can fit it.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Consoles got dearer after launch, so replacement now costs more than at release.
- **The number:** $500 more for all three current consoles than at launch.
- **The risk:** The cheap part is vendor-controlled, and supply ends before the hardware does.

<!-- tension -->

## The generation that got dearer

Consoles used to shed price as they aged. This generation added it, in three separate announcements. Sony lifted its US list to $649.99 for the disc PlayStation 5 on 2 April, and called the move a response to "continued pressures in the global economic landscape" [1]. Microsoft raised its 512-gigabyte consoles by $100 and its 1-terabyte models by $150 on 1 August, and said what drove it: console storage and memory costs had risen more than 2.5 times over, with another doubling expected by the fall of 2027 [2]. On 1 September, Nintendo raised the Switch 2 from $449.99 to $499.99, fifteen months after launch [3].

iFixit put the three side by side this week and priced the result at $500 above the launch total [4]. The raise covers a cost, not a margin. Microsoft says consoles are "typically not sold at a profit", and that it sells them for less than they cost to make, which I read as the industry conceding that the arithmetic has flipped [2]. Every repair-or-replace sum has two sides, and the side that moved up is the one that used to fall.

Nobody moved the other side. The thumbstick that drifts on a PlayStation controller is a $14.99 part from iFixit [5]. A boxed New Nintendo 3DS XL, the top model in a handheld family the company has since dropped, sells for $450.00 today, eleven years after it reached stores [6]. It cost $199.99 when it was new [4].

## By the numbers

- **$649.99 US list:** Sony's own price for the disc PlayStation 5 [1].
- **More than 2.5x:** Microsoft's figure for the rise in console storage and memory costs behind its increase [2].
- **$14.99:** iFixit's price for the DualSense joystick, the part that drifts first [5].

## What fails, and what it costs

The failure list is unglamorous. Drifting sticks, dust, loud fans and consoles that run hot are what iFixit names as the usual suspects, and most of them improve once the machine is opened and cleaned [4]. The stick is the expensive one, and on paper it is a rounding error against the console it sits in. On paper is the problem. iFixit rates the joystick replacement difficult, at one to four hours, because the module is soldered to the controller's board [5]. That is a soldering iron, a desoldering pump and a steady hand, or a repair shop's hourly rate on top of the part.

The part itself is not the barrier for everyone. iFixit ran repair workshops across Europe this autumn and found people who "had the motivation, and they were gaining the knowledge", and who still could not afford the replacement part [7]. I keep coming back to the soldering iron, because that labour is the one cost no vendor sets. Our [repair-or-replace calculator](/calculators/tech-debt-repair/) runs the arithmetic on the two numbers an owner actually holds: the quote and the price of a replacement. Microsoft's answer to its own price rise is a certified refurbished console at up to $100 off list [2]. Sony's Black Friday promotion this month discounts games rather than hardware [8].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

None of this makes a console an investment. Microsoft's increase recovers a cost on hardware it already sells below cost, and a company that loses money on the box has no reason to make repairs cheap [2]. I don't read the resale premium as a general rule either. It belongs to a discontinued collectible: $450.00 for a boxed New 3DS XL and $838.78 for a sealed one, on a handheld with no loose-unit figure in this record [6]. The premium is partly for the box.

What the two ends share is the part and the schedule behind it. Nintendo stopped repairing that handheld line in March 2025, when it ran out of components [4]. The console you already own is worth keeping because its replacement got dearer. That is also why its parts will get dearer and scarcer, at a pace set by the people who made it rather than by the price the market now puts on it.
