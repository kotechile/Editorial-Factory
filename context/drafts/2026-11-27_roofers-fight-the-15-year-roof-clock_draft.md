<!-- lead -->
The shingle makers just accused their own insurers' research arm of bad science. The fight is about the roof over your head. The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association (ARMA) said the study behind a new 15-year limit rests on "non-standard testing protocols, unrepresentative grab-sampling, and incomplete probabilistic modeling." The limit takes effect on January 1, 2027. After that, a roof older than 15 years cannot keep the FORTIFIED designation — the certificate behind premium discounts in states that reward it. The certificate, not the roof, is what expires.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
This piece is about the fight over the new 15-year limit on FORTIFIED asphalt roofs. It matters to homeowners whose premiums ride on that certificate. The rule does not order anyone to replace a roof. But Florida shows what insurers do with age rules.

<!-- tension -->
## Narrow rule, wide fear
The sequence runs one way. The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (IBHS) is a research group funded by insurers. It issued the bulletin on 2026-09-29. Its words are plain: "designation eligibility is limited to 15 years from the initial FORTIFIED designation date," or from the shingles' own install date, if they predate the program [1]. The limit applies only to applications filed on or after that day. It does not shorten any term issued before then [1].

ARMA answered in public on 2026-11-09. The trade group called the conclusions "fundamentally compromised." It asked insurers not to use the bulletin for broad screening rules [4].

So the standard's owner and the shingle sellers are at war. What I find hard to square is how narrow the rule is, how wide the fear runs. IBHS insists the limit "does not establish asphalt shingle service life" and forces no replacement at any age [1]. The program's own page says the upgrades "remain in place" even after a home ages out [2]. ARMA is not reacting to what the bulletin says. It is reacting to what insurers do with documents like it.

Florida is the proof both sides reach for. Carriers there began dropping roofs older than ten years, solely for age. That was the early-2000s claims crisis. Some shingle warranties ran 30 years [4].

The state answered with a law: no non-renewal on a roof under 15 years old solely for age, if an inspection finds five years of life left [4]. Only Florida, Texas and Virginia have such rules. Insurance Journal counts them with the Texas insurance department [4].

## By the numbers
- **15 years:** the age limit on FORTIFIED asphalt-shingle roofs under the September bulletin [1].
- **100,000 designations:** the milestone IBHS announced in May, over half earned in the prior three years [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## Where this bites
The people closest to the story are blunt about the stakes. Security First, one of Florida's largest property carriers, prices on roof age and upkeep. Its president, Melissa Burt DeVriese, called age "the single-largest rate factor." Her company offers no premium discount for FORTIFIED homes [4]. The reason is the building code. FORTIFIED work is close to what Florida's code already requires for roof decks and coverings. The certificate buys less where the code carries the load [4].

For a homeowner with an aging asphalt roof, the new rule couples two clocks. The renewal clock now runs against the discount clock. In most states, no statute stops an insurer from folding age into its risk rules. What I'd watch is the quiet one: whether any carrier cites the bulletin the way Florida carriers once cited age. ARMA's letter is, in effect, a public record that they might.

The paperwork matters as much as the date. A roof with no recorded install date gets one term of up to five years. The program then bars renewal — its re-designation step — until the shingles are replaced [1]. The install invoice is the one document that keeps the clock honest. The discount math behind [your 49% discount isn't all roof](/your-49-discount-isnt-all-roof/) already showed a state mandate that looks like a windfall is partly a reinsurance story. This limit is the same discount, now with an expiry.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch
Both sides are arguing past the certificate. IBHS is right that the bulletin mandates nothing. No roof must come off at the age limit, and metal or tile roofs face no such limit [1]. ARMA is right that the split may not survive contact with insurers. It did not in Florida. The institute works for those same insurers. The shingle makers are arguing with their biggest customers. One Florida contractor made the case simply. His own 32-year-old shingles weathered Hurricane Milton with loose shingles as the only damage. He wants the shingle's quality, not its age, to drive the risk question [4]. What I keep coming back to is the test his argument will face first: a certificate expiry, not a storm.
