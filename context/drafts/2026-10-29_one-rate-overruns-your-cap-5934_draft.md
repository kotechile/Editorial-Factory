<!-- lead -->
Your €115,000 salary sits $203 under the foreign earned income exclusion at the whole-year exchange rate the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) printed for 2025. At the rate it printed for 2021, the same euros overrun your cap by $5,934, because the cap is a dollar figure and your pay is not. The printed average is one number for a whole year, while the rule for a single payday is the rate on that day [1]. Either way, a rate you never chose decides how much of the exclusion your job uses.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The tax code writes its limits in dollars. The IRS says every amount on a return goes in dollars, at the rate in force when you receive, pay or owe the item [1]. Income in another currency is therefore measured, not merely reported, and the ruler moves on its own schedule.

The IRS table shows how far it moves. Its euro column reads 0.886 for 2025, 0.951 for 2022 and 0.846 for 2021, and those figures run the other way from what most people expect, because they are euro per dollar, so you divide by them [2].

One return then uses more than one rate. Pay takes the rate on the day it lands. Foreign tax you paid takes the rate on the day you paid, and a refund keeps that day's rate, not the rate when the money came back [5]. Tax you count as it arises, not when you pay, usually takes the year's average, unless one of three doors closes [5].

My read is that the filing software hides this. It asks for a figure, turns it into dollars, and never shows which rate it used. The reader then sees dollar income that moved without a raise, beside a credit that shrank without any change in foreign tax.

## By the numbers

- **0.886 — 2025 euro average:** The IRS table gives 0.886 euro per dollar for 2025, against 0.951 for 2022 and 0.846 for 2021. So €100,000 is $112,867 at the 2025 figure and $105,152 at the 2022 one, a 7.34% move in the dollar tax base with no change in pay [2].
- **1.1130 — today's reference rate:** The European Central Bank (ECB) quoted the dollar at 1.1130 per euro on 29 October 2026, which puts €100,000 at $111,300, or $1,567 below what the 2025 average gives [3].
- **1.1209 — the Federal Reserve's rate:** The H.10 release of 26 October 2026 put the euro at 1.1209 dollars on 19 October, well within a cent of the ECB's figure, so the two official sources agree about one market [4].
- **30% over 36 months — the fast-inflation test:** A currency counts as fast-inflating once prices across the 36 months before the tax year's last day rise 30% in total, and tax counted as it arises in that currency has to use the year-end rate [5].

## What I'd watch:

- **The refund rule:** a refund of foreign tax keeps the rate from the day you paid, so a filer who overpaid in a strong year carries that year's rate into a later one [5].
- **The three doors:** tax counted as it arises drops to the payment-day rate when it is paid late, or paid in an earlier year. It drops to the year-end rate when the currency fails the price test [5].
- **The one-way election:** a filer on that basis can pick the payment-day rate instead. That choice then runs through later years until the agency agrees to undo it, so it is not a yearly toggle [5].
- **The paper trail:** Form 1116 asks for a written note on how you got the rate, which is the only place an auditor sees the choice [5].
- **The odd split:** a bonus paid in one currency and taxed in another is where the paid-versus-counted gap bites hardest, because the two legs take different rates.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The printed averages are one figure for a year, not permission for every payment. A salaried reader paid evenly through the year has a fair case for the average. One whose income lands in a single bonus has a weaker one, because the rule points at the rate on the day [1].

The cap is also reset each year rather than fixed, so the dollar figure that makes this math work belongs to last year's return, and this year's will differ [6][7].

None of it is a hedge. A rate change moves the tax base, not the buying power, and a filer who converts more cheaply still spends at local prices. My read, held loosely — the useful habit is to settle the method once, note the rate you used, and keep it. [The expat tax and relocation model](/calculators/expat-evaluator/) runs both rates side by side, and the gap is what the paperwork must defend.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The exclusion and the other limits on a US return are dollar amounts, while cross-border pay arrives in another currency, so the exchange rate fixes the dollar figure before any rule about the cap applies.
- **Why It Matters:** A euro salary can use up the whole exclusion or overshoot it without moving by a cent, and the credit for foreign tax shifts with the same rate, so the filing decision starts with which rate to use.
- **What I'd Watch:** the places where the chosen method shows up on the return.
  - **The refund rule:** a refund keeps the rate from the day the tax was paid, so an old year's rate can follow a filer forward.
  - **The three doors:** tax paid late, paid in an earlier year or priced in a fast-inflating currency each falls back to a different rate.
  - **The election:** picking the payment-day rate binds later years until the agency agrees to undo it.
- **The Catch:** the printed averages are whole-year figures rather than a licence for every payment, the cap is reset each year, and a stronger conversion does not buy more abroad.
