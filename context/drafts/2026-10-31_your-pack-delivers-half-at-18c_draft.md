<!-- lead -->
Your pack delivers about half its rated capacity at -18°C, and the drop comes from the cell, not the machine. A battery rated at 100 percent capacity at 27°C (80°F) usually gives only 50 percent at -18°C (0°F) [1]. Cold slows the chemistry inside the cell and cuts what it can hand over. EGO (the outdoor power equipment brand) builds its 24-inch two-stage around two recommended 7.5 amp-hour packs [2]. Together they hold 840 watt-hours in mild air and roughly 420 when the packs are cold. The blower that clears a wide driveway in December is the same machine in January, running on less than half the energy it was sold with.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Makers rate packs at 27°C (80°F) because the numbers look best there, and the label never says so [1]. Warm cells also live longer in service. A pack run at 40°C gives up 40 percent of its cycle life against one kept near room temperature [1].

Cold adds a second effect that a fuel gauge cannot show. The extra resistance drops voltage when the motor pulls current, so the tool shuts off early, before the charge is gone [1]. Electric vehicles show the same thing at larger scale. Cold cuts their fuel economy about 39 percent in mixed driving and their range by 41 percent [4].

What I keep circling is that the engineering answer here is heat, not capacity. Chargers built for cold refuse to charge a pack below freezing [3]. That is the industry saying the cell sets the limit, not the motor.

## By the numbers

- **27°C — the rating temperature:** every run-time figure on the box is measured at 80°F, and the label never says how far the number moves in colder air [1].
- **0.02C — the charge rate at -30°C:** the rate research allows at that temperature is so low that one charge would stretch past 50 hours, a wait the source calls impractical [3].
- **12 to 15 years — pack life in a mild climate:** modeling from the National Laboratory of the Rockies puts the same packs at 8 to 12 years in an extreme climate, which covers heat as well as cold [5].
- **40 percent — the cycle-life cost of heat:** a pack run at 40°C instead of room temperature loses 40 percent of its cycle life, so where it is stored matters too [1].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## Where this bites:

The garage is the failure point, not the driveway. A pack left in an unheated shed or an attached garage sits at air temperature, and a January cold snap holds it below freezing for weeks [3]. Charging it there is the expensive move. Below 0°C a consumer lithium pack cannot take a charge without plating metal onto the anode [3]. The pack looks like it is charging the whole time.

- **The charge window:** the best range is 10°C to 30°C, so a pack that comes in from the cold wants an hour indoors first [3].
- **The pack in the machine:** a warm pack carried out to a cold blower gives back some of what the label promised, then cools toward the air temperature while the machine sits between passes [1].
- **The spare:** rotating two packs through an indoor charge beats running one down and topping it up in a cold garage, and it keeps the spare away from the temperature that shortens its life [1].

I'm watching whether the winter machines start printing a cold-capacity number beside the run time. The rest of the desk's coverage of these machines sits under [outdoor power and snow equipment](/categories/outdoor-power-equipment/).

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The half-capacity figure is a lab discharge at one rate on one chemistry, and the source says the momentary drop differs by chemistry [1]. A reader whose storms run at -5°C loses less than half. The arithmetic here is about the pack, not about one driveway.

The climate band is "extreme climates", which includes heat, and it is modeling rather than a warranty term [5]. A bigger pack does buy more cold run time, and that is not wrong. It just buys it at the pack's cold efficiency instead of its label's, which is a poor trade if an hour indoors solves the same problem.

My read is that one fault here is worth remembering when a January morning goes wrong. A pack charged below freezing does not come back, and nothing on the charger tells you it happened [3].

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** winter run time is a pack-temperature problem before it is a capacity problem.
- **The number:** a pack rated at 100 percent in mild air gives about 50 percent at -18°C (0°F).
- **The risk:** charging a frozen pack plates lithium on the anode, and the charger shows nothing wrong.
