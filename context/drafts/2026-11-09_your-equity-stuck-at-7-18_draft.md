<!-- lead -->
The record $13.2 trillion of accessible home equity is a number that moves when mortgage rates move, not when a homeowner decides anything [1]. Most of it stays locked: $11.6 trillion sits behind a first mortgage cheaper than today's 7.18%, so what you can reach is set by the gap between your own rate and the market, and that gap decides how much of the pile stays locked [2].

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** 88% of the $13.2 trillion in reachable equity sits behind a cheaper mortgage [1].
- **The number:** $11.6 trillion is counted as locked in, below June's 6.49% benchmark [1].
- **The risk:** The market is now 7.18%, so the locked share grows with rates, not choices [2].

<!-- tension -->

## The lock moves with rates

First American Data & Analytics published its first accessible-equity report on October 28, measuring the market as of June 30, when the average 30-year rate was 6.49% [1]. Freddie Mac's weekly survey put the same average at 7.18% on November 5 [2]. Between those readings, the bar a mortgage must clear to count as locked in rose 0.69 points.

The definition is what makes the rise matter. Equity counts as locked in when the owner's own mortgage rate sits below the going rate, because replacing that loan would cost more than keeping it [1]. A homeowner at 6.6% was not locked in on that June date, since 6.6 sits above 6.49. The same owner is locked in now, since 6.6 sits below 7.18. Nothing about the house changed, and nobody borrowed a dollar.

The share is not a measure of wealth sitting idle. It counts the owners for whom the cheapest money in the house is the loan already on it, and that is most of them. The report's own trend shows the mechanism from the other side. Rates eased between mid-2025 and mid-2026, so locked-in equity fell 2% while the total rose 1.5% [1]. The least locked band grew 25% over that year, and every state still reported at least three quarters of its eligible equity as locked. What I keep circling is that the reachable pile grew because the benchmark came down to meet owners, not because owners did anything.

## By the numbers

- **$11.6 trillion locked in:** Equity held by owners whose first mortgage sits under the 6.49% benchmark the report used [1].
- **83% of it extreme:** Locked equity held by owners at least 1.5 points under that benchmark, a band that begins below 4.99% [1].
- **0.69 points wider:** The distance the going rate has risen since, to 7.18% in the latest survey [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## Where the two groups split

1. **Owners far below the market.** This group holds 83% of the locked money, at rates more than 1.5 points under that benchmark and below 4.99% [1].
2. **Owners close to the market.** This group holds the low and moderate bands, and its share rose from about 10% at the end of 2025 to 13% [1].

Most locked money sits with the first group, and that concentration is why lenders read the report as a map of the second-lien market. Replacing a mortgage priced under 5% with one at 7.18% raises the cost on every dollar owed. A second lien, such as a home equity line or a fixed home equity loan, raises the cost only on the amount drawn, and the cheap first mortgage stays where it is [1]. The two routes differ in what they reprice, which is the distinction a [HELOC and ARM stress test](/calculators/heloc-arm-stress/) exists to make plain.

What the label does not describe is the house. A roof at the end of its life, a panel with no spare slots or a water heater past its warranty makes the same claim on a household whether or not the mortgage under it is cheap. The arithmetic is plain, and it settles most cases. On a $250,000 balance, a move from 6.49% to 7.18% adds $1,725 a year before the owner draws a cent for the work [2]. That balance is my assumption rather than a published figure. A second lien on a $30,000 roof charges its rate on $30,000 alone, so when a job is small against the balance, the loan that looks more expensive is often the cheaper one.

The second group matters for a different reason. Those owners sit close enough to market that a cash-out refinance stops being a penalty and starts being a sideways move, and the trade press reads the same shift the same way [3]. I would want the December edition of the report before treating 13% as a trend rather than one quarter.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The two anchors are not one series. First American's 6.49% is its own national average at quarter end, while Freddie Mac's 7.18% covers conventional purchase loans with 20% down and excellent credit [1]. Subtracting one from the other gives direction with more precision than level.

Accessible equity is also an estimate, not a credit line. The publisher assumes a total loan-to-value at or below 80% and warns that real borrowing depends on underwriting and the owner's own finances [1]. My figure of about $1.6 trillion for the reachable remainder comes from four rounded bands and a rounded total, so it stays approximate by construction.

I could be wrong, but the direction matters more than the label. Locked in is an incentive to keep a cheap loan, not a bar on borrowing, and a fall in rates would loosen it as quietly as this rise tightened it. What it does not do is wait: the reachable share was 12% in June, and the market has spent four months pushing it back down.
