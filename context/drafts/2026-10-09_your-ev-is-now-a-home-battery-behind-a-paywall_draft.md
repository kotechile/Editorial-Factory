<!-- lead -->

Tesla made its two best-selling cars — the Model 3 and the Model Y — able to power a house, and it did it without the costly charger that every other home-backup system needs [1][3]. The change covers cars ordered in the United States after Oct. 1, 2026, and it turns the electric vehicle (EV) in millions of driveways into a backup battery [1].

The catch sits a few feet from the car. To send that power into a home, the owner has to have a Tesla Powerwall 3, a home battery that sells for $8,200 before installation [3].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The cheapest new home grid capacity may already be parked in the driveway — and the paywall that decides who gets it is a home battery, not the car.

Power bills are climbing, and the grid is not catching up. The average U.S. home price for power reached 18.44 cents per kilowatt-hour in May 2026, up 6.2% from a year earlier [6].

Utilities asked for more than $18 billion in rate increases this year, and diesel hit an all-time high in late September [5][4]. At the same time, California holds back about 22 gigawatts of clean energy and batteries behind transmission lines it has not built [7]. I have been watching the home-energy story all year, and this is the first time the fix and the problem have landed in the same month.

The fix arrived on Oct. 6. Tesla said all new Model 3 and Model Y cars can now feed power back into a home [1]. Most vehicle-to-home (V2H) systems in the U.S. need a separate box on the wall, a direct-current (DC) charger that costs $6,500 to $10,000 and up [3].

Tesla instead moves the conversion inside the car. The car already turns alternating current (AC) from the wall into DC for its battery, so it can run that process in reverse [3].

## By the numbers

- **11.5 kilowatts — Home backup draw:** The most power a Model 3 or Model Y can push into a house, more than several rivals offer [2].
- **$8,200 — Powerwall 3:** The home battery the setup requires, before installation, which Car and Driver says "can run well into five figures" [2][3].
- **18.44¢/kWh — U.S. residential price:** The national average in May 2026, up 6.2% year over year [6].
- **$18.6 billion — Rate requests:** What utilities asked for in the first half of 2026, a record set against rising household bills [5].

## What I'd watch:

- **The retrofit path:** Tesla's service notes include a draft procedure to upgrade an older Model Y to the new hardware [2]. I'd want to know whether Tesla offers that upgrade to owners, or leaves the work to the factory.
- **Older Powerwalls:** The feature works with the Powerwall 3 today, and Tesla says support for earlier Powerwalls is "coming soon" [2]. Until it lands, the option stays closed to the first wave of home-battery buyers.
- **Utility programs:** Pacific Gas & Electric (PG&E) is piloting home backup with Tesla's system, and its own data shows why. The utility says the $10,000 to $15,000 a DC setup costs is the barrier that keeps ordinary customers out [3].
- **The grid question:** Only California and Maryland let homes send power back through this kind of connection outside a pilot [3]. Tesla has not switched on grid export for the Model 3 or Model Y yet, so for now the car backs up the house, not the neighborhood [3].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

V2H is a perk for people who already bought a home battery, not a cheap path to one.

The car does not replace the Powerwall 3; it acts as a backup to that backup [2]. During an outage, the home battery drains first, then the car refills it while also running the house [2]. That design keeps a car from being tethered to the home for days, which is a real gain.

But the price of entry is still the $8,200 battery plus installation. The extra days of power only matter to a household that already cleared that cost. I keep circling the same gap: the homes most exposed to rising bills are the least likely to own the battery that unlocks the car.

The scale is real, though. One study found California could meet a third of its grid storage goals by enrolling just 10% of its EVs [8]. That is the promise. Today it runs through a paywall.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Tesla made the Model 3 and Model Y able to power a home through its Powershare system, moving the power conversion inside the car instead of a pricey DC charger [1][3].
- **Why It Matters:** Household power prices are up 6.2% year over year and utilities are seeking record rate increases, so home backup is worth more — but the feature only works if the household already owns an $8,200 Powerwall 3 [3][6].
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The retrofit:** Whether Tesla upgrades older Model 3 and Model Y cars to the new hardware [2].
  - **Older Powerwalls:** Whether Tesla extends support beyond the Powerwall 3, opening the feature to earlier battery buyers [2].
  - **Grid rules:** Whether more states let homes send power back through this connection beyond California and Maryland [3].
- **The Catch:** The car backs up the home battery rather than replacing it, so the price of entry stays the $8,200 battery plus installation [2][3].
