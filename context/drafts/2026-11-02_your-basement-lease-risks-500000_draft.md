The basement conversion cost $60,000, and the tax story attached to it runs 27.5 years. Divide the spend by that schedule and the yearly write-off is $2,182 [1]. The rules that follow are the ones a rental calculator leaves out. The Internal Revenue Service (IRS) taxes that depreciation back when the house sells [4], and it cuts the home-sale exclusion by the same amount [3]. On a joint return the exclusion covers the first $500,000 of gain, and a lease that runs past three years puts it at risk.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The code treats a rented dwelling as a business asset, so it spreads the cost across a useful life. For residential rental property that life is 27.5 years, longer than the appliances, the carpet or the tenant stay [1][5]. It is also the one place where a house's wear is written down as a schedule, and it reaches a single owner: the one who rents the space out.

What I find hardest to square is how small that split makes the benefit. A dollar spent on a kitchen you cook in is never deducted. The same kitchen above a rented unit becomes a deduction of a few cents on the dollar each year. The code is not rewarding a landlord. It is saying that a building wears out, and that wear on a business asset is a cost of earning the rent.

The timing matters more than the size. The write-off arrives slowly, it is often unusable in the years it builds up, and it is reversed at the sale. A homeowner who signs a lease to capture a tax break is buying the smallest part of the deal first.

## By the numbers

- **3.64 percent — Annual slice:** One divided by 27.5 is the share of a conversion the code lets an owner deduct each year, which is $2,182 on a $60,000 build and $1,091 on a $30,000 one [1].
- **$150,000 — Where the loss stops counting:** A special $25,000 allowance for rental real estate falls by half of modified adjusted gross income above $100,000, and it reaches zero at $150,000, so a household above that line carries the loss forward instead of deducting it [2].
- **25 percent — The rate on the way back:** Depreciation taken on a dwelling is taxed at up to 25 percent on sale, rather than at the long-term capital gains rate [4].

## What I'd watch:

I keep circling the horizon, because it decides more than the deduction does. The exclusion needs the owner to have lived in the home for 2 of the last 5 years [3], which leaves roughly three years of renting before the test fails. Rent longer and the exclusion goes, and the rented years cut it even when the test still passes [3].

- **Three years — The exclusion's fuse:** A lease that outlasts the residence test turns a gain that would have been tax-free into ordinary long-term gain [3].
- **Five years — The parts inside the walls:** Appliances and carpet in a rental run on five-year schedules while the building runs on 27.5, so a conversion quoted as one number is not one asset [1].
- **Fair market value — The basis you actually get:** At conversion the depreciable basis is the lesser of fair market value or adjusted basis [1], so a soft market lowers the base you write off, not the price you paid.
- **The $25,000 allowance — Who can use it:** Above the income line the paper loss is suspended, so the deduction works against collected rent and never against a salary [2].

The build-versus-buy case for adding the unit already rested on a tenant, and [that tenancy is what starts this clock](/your-next-bedroom-417500/). Nothing about the schedule changes the rent. It changes what the rent costs you later.

## The catch

My read is that the deduction is close to a wash for most of the people who chase it. At a 24 percent marginal rate the write-off saves $14,400 across the full schedule, and the 25 percent recapture takes $15,000 back at the sale. At 35 percent the owner keeps the gap, which comes to $6,000, or about $218 a year.

That is the honest size of the prize. The conversion should be justified by the rent, the space or the resale, and the schedule changes the timing rather than the verdict. Where it does change the verdict is at the exit. An owner who rents for a decade and then sells has turned a tax-free gain into a taxable one, and paid the recapture in the same transaction.

Nothing here replaces an accountant reading the return, and the rules above come from the 2025 editions of the IRS publications, so a reader filing later should check the current ones. The arithmetic is mine; the rules are theirs.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** Renting a converted space turns a house into a business asset with a write-off, and the write-off is smaller than the exclusion it endangers.
**The number:** $2,182 a year on a $60,000 conversion, before it is taxed back at the sale.
**The risk:** A lease past three years ends the residence test, and the exclusion goes with it.
