<!-- lead -->
Your claim dies at 9 months, and the note your dock writes on the delivery receipt does not count as a claim at all [1][2]. A notation of shortage or damage on a freight bill or receipt "shall, standing alone, not be considered by carriers as sufficient to comply with the minimum claim filing requirements" [1]. What counts is a written demand that says the carrier is liable and names a specific or determinable amount of money [1].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The federal cargo-claim rules in the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) — 49 CFR Part 370 for trucks and freight forwarders, Part 1005 for railroads — are not about who crushed the pallet [1][5]. They are about the shape of the paperwork.

A carrier is not obliged to pay a claim that was never filed as one. So the rule defines a claim as three things: facts that identify the shipment, an assertion that the carrier is liable, and a request for a sum of money [1].

A damage note carries the first of those three and says nothing about the other two. That is why the signed receipt in a driver's hand proves the shipment arrived short and does almost nothing to recover the cost.

The dates are the part worth reading, because they run from the shipment rather than from the argument. Congress wrote two floors that a carrier cannot contract around: at least 9 months to file the claim, and at least 2 years to bring a lawsuit, counted from the carrier's written denial [2][3].

My read: the rule is doing something narrower than most ops teams assume. It is not deciding who was at fault. It is deciding whether a demand exists in the first place — and the paperwork a dock produces all day long is not that demand.

## By the numbers

- **9 months — Filing floor:** A motor carrier may not set the claim period below 9 months, by rule or by contract, and gives at least 2 years to sue from its own written denial [2]. Rail carriers are held to the same pair [3]. For a load delivered on 15 October 2026, the floor runs to 15 July 2027.
- **One year — Ocean suit bar:** At sea the ship and carrier are free of liability unless a lawsuit is brought within one year of delivery, or of the date delivery should have happened. Where the damage is not visible, written notice goes to the carrier within three days — and skipping that notice does not cut off the right to sue inside the year [4].
- **30 days — Acknowledgment:** A carrier must write back to acknowledge a proper claim within 30 days of receiving it, unless it has already paid or declined it inside that window. Rail carriers may answer electronically [1][5].
- **120 days — Disposition:** The carrier must pay, decline, or make a firm written compromise offer within 120 days. If it cannot, it writes a status note at that point and every 60 days after while the claim sits open [1][5].

## What I'd watch:

What I am watching next is the handoff inside the building, where a dock note either becomes a demand or becomes a folder.

- **The exception report's output:** Whether a shortage or damage capture ends in a written demand with a dollar figure attached, or in a photo stream nobody asks about again [1]. The difference between those two outcomes is the whole article.
- **The evidence bundle:** A claim needs the bill of lading, proof of the freight charges, and either the invoice or a certified statement of the value involved [1]. Operators who already scan those at receiving are the ones who can assemble a filing in an afternoon.
- **The carrier's clocks as service levels:** The 30-day acknowledgment and the 120-day disposition window are dates a shipper can chase, because the carrier owes a written answer on each [1][5]. I would want to know how many claims sitting in a portal have passed 120 days without a status note.
- **The cadence of the modes:** Three days for a hidden loss on the water against 9 months on the road, inside one company's own shipments [1][4]. That is a calendar problem before it is a legal one, and I am curious whether it is anybody's named job.

## The catch

The 9-month and 2-year figures are floors, not deadlines. Both statutes forbid a shorter period; a contract or tariff may allow a longer one, so the bill of lading still decides what an individual shipper has [2][3].

The three-day ocean notice is softer than it sounds. The sea-carrying statute says that missing it does not affect the right to sue within the year, which is the line that actually closes the claim [4].

And none of this decides fault. The rules say what a carrier must answer and when, not what it owes; packaging, count, temperature and custody all still get argued on their own merits [1].

The part I keep circling: the piece of paper that feels decisive to the people on the dock — the signed receipt — is the piece the rule explicitly declines to treat as a claim. The demand that does count is one somebody has to write, price, and file.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The federal cargo-claim rules treat a written demand for a stated amount of money as the claim, and state plainly that a shortage or damage note on a delivery receipt is not one on its own [1].
- **Why It Matters:** A carrier may not shorten the claim period below 9 months, nor the time to sue below 2 years, so the clock is a floor rather than a negotiation — and a claim that was never filed as a claim has no clock to protect it [2][3].
- **What I'd Watch:** How the first fortnight after delivery is handled, because that is when the record is still retrievable.
  - **The demand with a number:** Turning a dock capture into a written request for a determinable sum, which is what the rule recognises [1].
  - **The bundle:** Keeping the bill of lading, the freight-charge proof and an invoice or certified value statement with the shipment file [1].
  - **The carrier's two dates:** The 30-day acknowledgment and the 120-day disposition clock, which give a shipper something to chase in writing [1][5].
- **The Catch:** These are minimums that a contract may extend and rules about procedure, not fault — the ocean one-year bar and the softer three-day notice both cut differently from the road clock [2][3][4].
