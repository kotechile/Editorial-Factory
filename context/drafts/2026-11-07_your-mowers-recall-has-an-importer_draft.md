<!-- lead -->
Two outdoor machines failed this autumn and got answers that had nothing to do with each other. About 42,860 walk-behind trimmer mowers from DR (a Generac Power Systems brand) were recalled on 8 October, after 19 reports of them catching fire, and every owner was offered a free repair kit and a dealer to fit it [1]. Fourteen days later the Consumer Product Safety Commission told owners of PowRyte pressure washers to cut the cord and throw the machine in the bin, because the seller had not answered a single request for a recall [2]. The gap between those two outcomes is not the defect or the price. It is the importer, and that is the job a license on your mower won't do. In the same quarter, both of the industry's biggest names began moving their factories away from their own badges.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Both big outdoor-power makers are separating brand from factory this quarter.
- **The number:** 42,860 mower owners got a free repair kit; the washer warning offers nothing.
- **The risk:** Who answers for a machine is set by its importer, not its brand.

<!-- tension -->

## The badge left the factory first

What strikes me first is the order of events, because the order is the argument. On 4 September, Stanley Black & Decker agreed to sell Excel Industries to Bad Boy Mowers. Excel builds the gas ride-on and zero-turn mowers sold as Hustler, and it is expected to bring in about $300 million this fiscal year [3]. On 21 October, Husqvarna said it will move some production of its own battery handheld tools from Europe to Asia [4].

On 4 November, Stanley Black & Decker confirmed the other half of the same retreat. Its gas walk-behind mower line already runs on a license, and that change took 2 percentage points out of Tools & Outdoor sales in the quarter [5]. Both recall notices landed in the middle of that stretch.

A license splits two things a buyer has always treated as one: the name that sets the spec sheet, and the company that owns the plant, the parts desk and the warranty. That split is not new here, and it is not a fault in itself. What changed this quarter is that the two biggest names in the category started doing it at the same time, in public, just as a regulator was showing what the far end of the setup looks like.

The rest follows. A recall has to be served on somebody who can be reached, and in practice that means the importer named on the box or a US parent with money to lose. DR Power's mowers were imported by Generac Power Systems of Waukesha, Wisconsin, so the fix arrived as a repair kit with a phone number attached [1]. Powell Works, the company behind the pressure washers, is in China and did not answer the agency at all, so the notice carries no refund and no company contact [2]. Same regulator, same month, same kind of danger, opposite outcomes.

## By the numbers

- **19 fire reports:** The incidents behind the recall of about 42,860 walk-behind mowers [1].
- **2% of Tools & Outdoor sales:** What the gas walk-behind license took out of the quarter [5].
- **$320:** The top of the online price band for the washers owners were told to destroy [2].

## Who answers in year three

What I watch in a quarter like this is the parts counter, not the press release. A licensed machine keeps its badge, its spec sheet and its price, and the thing that quietly changes hands is who has to stock a spindle, a control cable or a battery contact plate five winters from now. Whether an orphaned machine is worth keeping usually comes down to the price of one spare part, and [a spare battery pack is where that math most often goes wrong](/your-550-battery-spare-is-wrong/).

The money explains the timing better than any strategy deck. Stanley Black & Decker's Tools & Outdoor margin rose to 17.0%, up 5.2 points, and about 4.3 of those points came from tariff refunds rather than from anything the business did [5]. Husqvarna reported organic sales down 5% and is halfway through a 3 billion kronor savings plan, with roughly 935 million kronor banked [4]. When a refund is carrying the margin and Europe is buying less, a factory is a cost to hand off, and the badge is the part that survives the handoff.

Two things would settle the open question. The first is a label. Whether the next Cub Cadet walk-behind names Stanley Black & Decker or a license holder as the maker decides who a recall gets served on. The second is the pressure-washer file. The same missing part has now produced a refund, a recall and a warning in eight months. That pattern is a list of sellers, not one bad actor [6].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

What I keep circling is how little any of this shows about build quality. None of it shows that a licensed machine is built worse, and it would be wrong to read it that way. DR Power is the counter-example to my own argument: an importer with a phone number did exactly what the system is supposed to do, and Agiiman, a seller in China, refunded every one of its 80 pressure washers in April [6]. The variable is not the country and not the license. It is whether anyone with money and a phone number is standing behind the label when the machine fails.

That part is knowable before you buy, which is the only piece of this a homeowner controls. The importer is printed on the carton and in the manual's small print, the parts channel is one phone call away, and on a corded machine the safety device sits in plain sight at the plug. None of it is on the product page, and all of it outlives the badge.
