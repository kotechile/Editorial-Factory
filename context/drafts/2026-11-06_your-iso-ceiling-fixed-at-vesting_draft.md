<!-- lead -->
Your incentive stock option (ISO) cap is $100,000 of stock per calendar year, a ceiling fixed by your vesting calendar long before you sign an exercise form. An ISO is a grant that can be taxed as a long-term gain instead of wages. Most holders think that label is settled when they exercise. It is settled when each slice of the grant first becomes buyable, at the price on the day it was granted. That is why a buyout is the moment the label can go dead: the deal usually makes every slice buyable at once, and a slice exercised after it is a plain option in full.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Tax treatment is set by the year a slice becomes buyable, not the year you exercise.
- **The number:** $100,000, the grant-date value that qualifies per calendar year.
- **The risk:** A buyout's acceleration pushes most of the grant out of the qualified bucket.

<!-- tension -->

## Why the calendar decides it

The tax code measures the cap once a year. Options that become buyable for the first time in a calendar year are valued at the share price on grant day, and anything past $100,000 in one year is treated as an option that is not an ISO [1]. The same rule counts grants in the order they were made, so an older one fills the year before a newer one gets a look [1].

The Treasury's own examples say it better than any adviser. In the first, a worker gets an option he can exercise at once, every January for ten years, and he exercises all ten in one later year; every one still counts as an ISO, because no single year's grant crossed the line [3]. Exercise timing played no part. The test ran year by year, as each option became buyable. I keep circling how early that decision lands: years before anyone fills out an exercise form.

Acceleration is where the trap sits. The rules count an option in the year its acceleration clause fires, and a change of control that makes every option buyable at once counts as that trigger [4]. The third example runs three options against one year's allowance. The first stays qualified, the second is split into a qualified part and a plain part, and the third, exercised after the deal, fails in full [4].

One piece of folk advice falls at the same wall. Selling the shares at once does not hand the room back: the rules say an early sale has no effect on whether an option crossed the ceiling [4]. The cap is spent when the slice becomes buyable, not when the cash lands.

## By the numbers

- **$400,000 grant-day value:** A four-year grant that spreads a quarter of its value into each calendar year stays wholly qualified up to this total [1].
- **110,000 of grant-day value:** The regulation's acceleration example pushes two options past the ceiling, so one is split and the third fails [4].
- **$135,980 median pay:** The 2025 median yearly wage for software developers, the pay the ceiling is read against [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## The order that protects the label

The rules draw one usable line. An acceleration does not count until the clause fires, so an option exercised before that point keeps its label [4]. In the fourth example a worker exercises a slice in June, a change of control lands in September, and that slice stays qualified while a later one is split [4]. Anyone watching a deal close has a reason to read the calendar, not the term sheet.

A second rule bites founders harder than most staff expect. Ownership above ten percent changes the terms. The price must then be at least 110 percent of fair market value, and the option runs five years or less instead of ten [2]. Crossing that line quietly raises the price and shortens the clock.

I keep coming back to what a company can fix. The rules let a company issue separate certificates, or mark shares in its own records, to say which ones were bought under the qualified option and which under the plain one [4]. Without that mark, shares count as qualified up to the ceiling and plain after it [4]. Divide the ceiling by the grant-day share price to get the shares that fit, so a lower grant-day price buys more qualified shares for the same money.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

What I find hard to square is how little the label is worth in some exercises and how much in others. The gain sits in the gap between the strike price and the share value on the day you buy: inside an ISO the gap goes into the alternative minimum tax (AMT), a second tax sum, while a plain option books it as wages [6]. Buy when the gap is near zero and the downgrade costs almost nothing. Buy into a big gap and the same downgrade is the difference between a capital gain and wage income on a real number.

The history is the rest of the answer. The ceiling went into the code in 1981 and has never been indexed, so it shrinks against pay every year pay grows [1]. At the 2025 software developer median it is 73.5 percent of one year's salary [5], so a strong package can pass the yearly limit with nothing unusual going on.

The open question is what the plain share does to the offer, and [the total-compensation model](/calculators/total-comp/) answers it by pricing base, cash and vesting stock against a growth guess.
