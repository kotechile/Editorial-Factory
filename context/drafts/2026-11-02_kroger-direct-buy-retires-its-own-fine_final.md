<!-- lead -->
Kroger is moving roughly a tenth of its buying straight to growers by 2029, and the 3% late fee that has kept its suppliers honest since the on-time bar rose to 93% has nothing to attach to on that share. Its own supplier letter says it in one line: "many of these fees will not apply if Kroger manages your inbound lanes" — the freight routes into its warehouses [1]. That lane is what kills the fee: it ends at the loading dock once Kroger takes control. On October 20, its chief financial officer told investors the company had opened sourcing hubs in South America and Asia on the way to "a fully-owned import distribution model" [2].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

What strikes me here: read the fine schedule as a gauge rather than a punishment, and the clash is easy to see. Kroger lifted its Original Requested Arrival Date (ORAD) bar — the day an order is due — from 90% to 93% for 2025, and kept a 95% case fill floor [1]. A late order that the supplier caused costs 3% of the invoiced amount, and a short fill costs 3% of the missing items' value. "Fees will be assessed via invoice deductions" [1], and you cannot deduct from a bill nobody sent.

That is why the program works. Each bar is enforced by taking money off a bill the supplier issued, on a lane the supplier booked, with a 180-day window to argue [1][3]. The scorecard is not a report Kroger writes about itself; it is a settling up between two firms. Remove one of them, and there is nothing left to settle.

Direct sourcing removes the other firm. Its grape deal in Peru has already locked in about 40% of imported grape supply from large growers "with support from service partners" [2]. The company frames the saving the way a buyer would: "Previously, we would have paid margin to a third party to sit between us and the grower" [2]. That margin bought more than a middleman's cut; it bought the party who booked the slot, carried the import, filled the case, and paid the fee when any of it went wrong.

Kroger's own policy sees the shift coming without naming it. On loads Kroger runs, "Kroger is responsible for location data," and any order above 10,000 pounds or 750 cube must send location and temperature readings every 15 minutes [1]. When the store runs the lane, the store carries the duty to report on it as well.

## By the numbers

- **Under 1% today, tenfold by 2029 — Direct buying:** Kroger's share of items bought straight from source, and the lift it has told investors to expect [2].
- **93% — On-time bar:** Kroger's ORAD line since January 2025, up from 90%, met only when an order is checked into a warehouse on its requested day [1].
- **95% — Case fill floor:** The share of an order that must be filled; short of it, the missing items are billed at 3% of their value [1].
- **$200 and $500 — Flat fees:** A load more than 30 minutes past its slot costs $200, and a label, pack, or product-code error costs $500 per order [1].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

My read is that the telling number is not the share target. It is the slice of a supplier's scorecard that quietly stops being worked out.

- **Where the scorecard shrinks first:** The grape deal is the template — one crop, a short grower list, and service partners doing the physical work. Lines with a short, known grower list move long before the tail does.
- **What replaces the fine:** A store running its own lane still needs a miss to show up. The open question is whether Kroger builds a receipt-side gauge — gate-in times, case counts, slot loyalty — or simply stops printing a supplier number for those loads.
- **The service partners:** They pick up the slot, the temperature feed, and the case fill, yet the fee table is written for suppliers, not for them. I'd expect a separate annex for third-party logistics firms before a new scorecard for growers.
- **The data clause:** The 15-minute location and temperature rule is the nearest thing to a new gauge already in the policy, and it watches the load rather than the promise.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The honest limit is scale. Nine tenths of what Kroger carries stays on a supplier lane through 2029, and there the ORAD program runs as written — same bar, same fee, same window. Nothing in the investor material says the scorecard is going away, and Kroger may simply apply it to its own hubs and its partners under another name.

A store need not publish the gauge it uses at home. Kroger could keep a clean gate-in number for direct loads and never put it in a supplier portal. That would leave the most quoted service yardstick in the trade intact for suppliers and invisible on the share that moved. A grower bidding against Kroger's own import desk would carry a burden the buyer no longer carries on its own volume.

The word I keep circling back to is "many." The letter says many fees do not apply on Kroger-run lanes, not all, and the policy is reviewed each year [1]. A soft clause can tighten in the next pass, which is the kind of change a supplier tends to read six months late. What I am watching is whether Kroger builds a scorecard for the direct share before that share grows; the gap between the number a supplier reports and the service a store actually gets is one this desk has traced before in [the fill-rate arithmetic](/your-98-fill-rate-18-dont-ship/).

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Kroger is moving a tenth of its buying onto lanes it runs itself, where its supplier fine schedule largely doesn't apply.
- **The number:** 93% — the on-time bar enforced by taking 3% off a supplier's bill.
- **The risk:** No supplier invoice means no fine, so the direct share goes unscored.
