Hewlett-Packard (HP) bills the overage page, the one you print past your plan's cap, at $0.20 because the subscription stops paying once you top its monthly count [1]. That matches what HP charges on its smallest plan, and it runs about 50 times what a black page costs on a bottle-fed tank [1][2].

Six prices for one printed page sit on those two vendors' pages, and the printer on your desk sets none of them.

Epson's EcoTank machine lists at $239.99, with a $50 promo running until October 31, 2026 [2]. A tank flips the deal: you buy the hardware once, and the page is nearly free after that.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

My read starts with what an ink subscription really sells, because the monthly fee is not a price for ink.

Each tier is a page quota with a fee attached, so the rate per page falls as the quota rises. The 700-page tier charges less than a quarter of what the 10-page tier charges for the same page, and the yearly plan's largest quota works out to $0.0424 [1].

The overage is where the design shows. Past the cap, pages come in sets of ten to fifteen for $2, which puts the extra page at $0.13 to $0.20 [1]. That hits a customer on the largest plan with the smallest plan's rate.

Buying ink with no plan at all costs more per page than any tier HP sells, so the option sold as freedom is the most expensive way to print [1]. A home whose printing spikes carries all the risk, while HP gets a forecast it can bill against and a printer that reports ink levels back [1].

## By the numbers

- **$0.0112 — Tank color page:** A black bottle page plus three color bottle pages, each bottle at the 522's $17.99 list price, comes to about a cent, which is 3.8 times under the cheapest subscription page [1][2][3].
- **$0.0424 — Cheapest HP page:** Buying the 8,400-page yearly tier at $355.99 works out under a nickel, which is still nearly four times the tank's color page [1][3].
- **$0.0425 — Tier step cost:** Upgrading from the 100-page to the 300-page tier buys 200 more pages for $8.50, costing 3.1 to 4.7 times less per page than an overage set [1].
- **$239.99 — Tank hardware cost:** Printing 75 pages a month puts the tank's three-year total at $239.99 against $305.64 in fees, and at 300 pages a month the gap widens to $284.34 [1][2].

## What I'd watch:

I watch three variables here, because each one moves the yearly total more than the plan's base rate does.

- **The forecast quota:** A home printing 120 pages a month on the 100-page tier pays overage on 20 pages every month, and the step up to the 300-page tier costs about a fifth as much per page as those extra sets [1].
- **The rollover ceiling:** Unused pages bank at three times the monthly allowance, hitting 150 pages on the 50-page plan, and the yearly plan banks nothing at all [1]. A quota bought for a busy quarter does not carry over a quiet one.
- **The boxed ink:** Epson's two-year claim rests on 125 pages a month, so the bottles inside the box cover about 3,000 pages before the first purchase [2]. A busy printer hits the bottle sooner than the calendar suggests.

## The catch

What I find hard to square is the tank's cent-a-page claim, because it comes from a standard text pattern run under the maker's own method, not a count of anyone's real documents. Epson notes that yields fall when printing is infrequent or leans on one color, so a photo-heavy month drinks color bottles far faster than a text month [2][3].

The comparison also assumes a hardware purchase. An owner who already has an eligible HP printer holds that capital, and for that reader the fair contest is the subscription against the pay-as-you-print sets, which the plan wins at every tier [1].

Neither figure carries paper, and neither carries the failure that ends most inkjet lives: a printhead that dries out in a machine nobody uses. Epson's advice to print often is not a marketing line but the physical condition under which its own yield figure holds [3].

For the same math run against a habit instead of a quota, the desk's [cost-per-cup calculator](/calculators/coffee-arbitrage/) prices one machine against a weekly routine.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** A page's price is set by the page count you commit to, not the printer you own.
**The number:** $0.20 past the cap, against $0.0424 on the biggest plan.
**The risk:** Both assume text pages printed often; a photo month or an idle machine costs more.
