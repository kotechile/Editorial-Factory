<!-- lead -->
The duty on the robots and machine tools a reshoring project buys was neither set nor dropped this week. It went to talks, after a Commerce Department review found the country imports most of the equipment its own factories run on [1]. The order signed on 28 October asks for a progress report in 180 days and names no rate, which leaves that equipment line still unpriced. So the largest capital item in a reshoring case now sits against a duty nobody has set, with the next decision due in late April 2027.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Section 232, the national-security tariff law, used to mean a duty within weeks. This week it meant a memo. It is the third memo of its kind since July: aircraft went to talks first, wind turbines went in early October, and now robots and machine tools have followed [2]. The legal design is what matters for a buyer's spreadsheet. The president has kept tariffs, quotas and fees in reserve, plus a plan to pay for more output at home. He can use them if no deal lands in 180 days, or if one lands and then fails [1].

What I keep circling is the shape of that reserve. A pause is not a zero. A zero is a number a planner can type into a cell, and this is not a number. The order's own findings say the country makes less robotics and industrial equipment than it uses [1]. That is an argument for acting later, not for acting never. Buyers already know how fast policy moves under a landed-cost model: a tariff gets cut on one line while [a fee on another snaps back](/goods-tariff-relief-vs-vessel-fee/), and neither change shows up in the quote the vendor sent.

The other half of the story is what buyers do with their models. The Reshoring Initiative's annual report, out on 23 October, found that 40% of original equipment manufacturers now use total cost of ownership analysis to compare home and offshore supply, up from 30% a year earlier [5]. That is the biggest one-year jump the survey has logged on the measure. It came in the same month the machine rate went open, and the money behind those models is large. Tracked private investment in new factories reached $2.091 trillion as of 26 October [4].

The volume is what makes an open rate expensive. Manufacturers and foreign investors announced 247,000 factory jobs through reshoring and foreign investment in 2025, and more than 2.3 million since 2010 [5]. High-tech goods made up 90% of those pledges, the highest share the group has recorded [5]. Those are the projects that buy robots and machine tools, which is why a rate on the equipment matters more here than a rate on the finished goods.

## By the numbers

- **180 days — Review date:** the trade office and the Commerce Department must report by late April 2027, and tariffs, quotas and fees stay on the table as remedies [1].
- **$86 billion — Equipment made at home:** that is what the sector produced in 2024, against about $7.1 trillion of factory goods shipped, the gap the review cites [1].
- **40% — OEMs on total cost:** up from 30% in 2025, the share comparing home and offshore supply on total cost rather than on price [5].
- **16% to 33% — Contract makers quoting:** the share of contract manufacturers quoting reshoring work roughly doubled in a year [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

- **A report date is not an action date.** The order sets a day for a status report, not a day for a new entry rule. Trade counsel's read is that importers should not touch codes on the strength of it [3]. A model that treats April as a tariff deadline has misread the paper.
- **Whether the habit holds.** Three pauses in three months looks like a pattern, not a one-off. The aircraft and wind files are the places to look if the machinery talks stall [2].
- **When pledges turn into steel.** The group counted 64 conditional projects in 2025 and found 14 had partly or fully converted by mid-2026 [5]. Pledges lead. Buildings prove.
- **Who pays for home capacity.** The White House says it may fund domestic output as one remedy [1]. In the same week, the Agriculture Department put $573 million of proposed money behind eight fertilizer plants worth more than $1.3 billion [6].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

I could be wrong, but the pause may be the best result a buyer could get, and a hedged plan is not a bearish one. Talks can end in a deal that removes the threat, and then the hedge reads as insurance bought at the top. The report's own figures cut both ways.

Policy uncertainty was the top concern, named by 57% of respondents. The group also projects about 309,000 jobs for 2026 off first-half numbers, and says a summer surge could carry the year to 400,000 [5]. Both are forecasts from a group with a stake in the answer. The 14-of-64 conversion rate is the honest weight on the other side of the scale.

## At a glance

**The shift:** The duty on robots and machine tools went to talks instead of being set, with tariffs and quotas still in reserve.
**The number:** 180 days — the report date, after which the remedies come back.
**The risk:** The equipment line has no rate, so a cost model built now carries an unpriced liability.
