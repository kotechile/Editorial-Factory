<!-- lead -->
Forty percent of billion-dollar companies now say they are scaling AI agents, up from 27 percent a year ago [1]. The share that can point to any earnings impact from AI did not move. It sits at 37 percent, exactly where it was last year [1]. And nearly one in three companies has already declined to buy software it could now build itself [1].

<!-- tension -->
The gap is the story. Adoption is racing ahead of returns, and the numbers now quantify it. McKinsey's 2026 State of AI survey — 1,719 leaders across 97 nations, fielded from May 4 to June 8 — found 88 percent of organizations use AI in at least one function, and 44 percent say it is scaling across the enterprise, up from 38 percent a year ago [1]. Yet only 37 percent attribute any earnings before interest and taxes (EBIT) impact to AI, and just 6 percent qualify as "high performers" who credit AI with at least 5 percent of EBIT. Both figures are flat year over year [1].

The sharpest new signal is on the build-vs-buy line. Thirty-two percent of organizations say they have decided against buying at least one software product or feature because agentic coding tools let them build it in-house [1]. For software vendors, this is the quiet threat inside an otherwise bullish report: a third of the market now defaults to build. That is a structural shift in how technology budgets get allocated, and it happened in one survey cycle.

Cost is starting to bite back, too. About 20 percent of respondents say AI operating costs — including the token costs of every model call — are constraining their AI use [1]. KPMG's pulse data adds the operational picture: 65 percent of organizations now cite difficulty scaling AI use cases, nearly double the prior quarter, and 62 percent name skills gaps as a barrier to showing return [2]. The bottleneck is no longer the model. It is the work around the model.

<!-- tactical-insight -->
The 6 percent who are winning point to a repeatable playbook, and it is not "buy better models." McKinsey's high performers do three things differently [1]. They pursue growth or innovation alongside efficiency, not efficiency alone. They redesign the workflow that AI runs inside, rather than inserting AI into an existing one. And they back the deployment with leadership commitment and operational rigor.

For an engineering leader, that translates to two concrete Monday-morning moves. First, stop measuring AI by adoption. Pick one use case with a clean before/after cost baseline — customer service, claims processing, back-office reconciliation — and instrument it from day one. The organizations stuck at zero are the ones that rolled out a copilot to ten thousand people with no defined metric. Second, treat the build-vs-buy decision as a default question now, not an annual review. Before renewing any vendor line, ask whether an agentic coding tool can build a first version cheaply. But do not confuse a prototype with a product: the 32 percent who declined to buy are still on the hook for integration, security, compliance, and maintenance — the parts a vendor bundles in.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
Read the 37 percent honestly. The word "some" is doing a lot of work in McKinsey's phrasing — "at least some EBIT impact" [1]. Only 6 percent credit AI with 5 percent or more of EBIT. Eighty percent of individuals report AI improved their own productivity, but individual productivity is not the same as enterprise earnings [1]. That gap is not just an execution problem; it is a measurement problem. Productivity that never reaches a cost line on a financial statement is easy to claim and hard to defend.

The report's own title — "On the road to ROI" — is the tell. Roads are where you are going, not where you are. The honest takeaway is that AI has not yet cleared the earnings bar for the vast majority of companies, and the data gives boards a reason to keep asking the same question they asked last year. What changes is who has to answer it: engineering, now, with a number.

<!-- tldr -->
- Adoption hit a record: 40% of billion-dollar companies scale AI agents (up from 27%), yet EBIT impact is flat at 37% and only 6% are "high performers" [1].
- The build-vs-buy line flipped: 32% of companies already declined to buy software they can now build with agentic coding tools [1].
- The fix is workflow redesign plus a clean cost baseline, not better models — and "some EBIT impact" is a weaker claim than it looks [1][2].
