<!-- lead -->
The remodel that pays you back at resale is not the one you want to live in. In the 2025 Cost vs. Value study, a garage-door replacement returned 267.7% of its cost — $4,672 spent, $12,507 added at sale — and eight of the report's ten best projects sat on the outside of the house [1]. A mid-range major kitchen remodel returned 51%: $82,793 spent, $42,130 back [2]. The distance between those two numbers is the most useful single fact in home-improvement finance.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Home capital splits into two markets that the resale data prices very differently. One is the shell — doors, siding, the roof, the envelope that keeps weather out. The other is the show — the kitchen and the bathroom that sell a home to the people who live in it.

The annual studies keep finding the shell pays and the show does not, and the gap is wide enough to change a decision. Zonda, which publishes the Cost vs. Value report, put it plainly: exterior replacements "remain the clear winners," while large interior remodels are "personally rewarding" but "too subjective to deliver the same return when it's time to sell" [1]. The National Association of Realtors agrees from the agent side; its chief economist calls the distance between the joy a remodel brings and the money it returns "intriguing" [3].

My read: this is not a mystery, and it is not going away. A buyer pays for a house that keeps them dry, warm, and insurable. The finish on your cabinets is your taste, not theirs, so it prices at close to nothing.

## By the numbers

- **267.7% — Garage door pays most:** A garage-door replacement cost $4,672 and added $12,507 at resale in the 2025 Cost vs. Value study, the top return of the projects it tracks [1].
- **207.9% — Stone veneer, third:** Manufactured stone veneer cost $11,702 and added $24,328, another exterior job near the top of the list [1].
- **51% — Mid-range major kitchen:** The same study's national kitchen figures show a mid-range major remodel costing $82,793 and returning $42,130; the upscale version returns about a third [2].
- **54% — Financed with home equity:** In the 2025 Remodeling Impact Report, 54% of consumers paid for their remodel with a home-equity loan or line of credit, against 29% from savings and 10% from credit cards [3].

## What I'd watch:

I've been watching how homeowners and their agents sort a fixed budget, and three signals keep coming up.

- **Where the data says the money comes back:** Eight of the ten best-returning projects in the 2025 study are replacements on the outside of the house, and the only interior project in the top five is a minor kitchen refresh at 112.9% [1]. I keep circling how narrow that list is — front door, garage door, siding, decks.
- **Where the spending actually goes:** The remodeling market sits above $600 billion, still 50% higher than before the pandemic, and Harvard's housing researchers say far more of it is needed on efficiency and resilience across 145 million homes [4]. The dollars and the returns do not point at the same parts of the house.
- **Who carries the gap:** With 54% of remodels financed against home equity [3], the shortfall between cost and recovery is borrowed money. A kitchen that returns 51% and is paid for on a credit line is a loss with interest attached.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

I could be wrong to read the two studies as one ranking, and they are not the same instrument. Cost vs. Value prices a hypothetical sale across 115 local markets, so its national numbers hide wide regional swings. The Remodeling Impact Report asks agents for an estimate rather than tracking completed sales.

Both are annual, too. The 2025 editions carry these figures; next year's will move, and a hot market can lift interior returns. A minor kitchen refresh actually returned more than it cost this year [1], which is the counterexample worth keeping — the trap is the size of the job, not the room.

What does not move is the direction. A remodel you keep is partly consumption, and the sale price only repays the part a stranger values.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The annual resale studies keep pricing the shell of a house above the show. Exterior replacements recover more than they cost; major kitchen and bath remodels recover about half.
- **Why It Matters:** With 54% of remodels paid for by borrowing against home equity, a project that recovers less than it costs is a loss with interest. The real choice is where a fixed budget goes.
- **What I'd Watch:** I'm watching whether the gap holds, and where the next owners point their spending.
  - **Envelope and entry work:** Doors, siding, garage doors, and roofing — the outside jobs the resale data keeps ranking highest.
  - **Minor versus major interiors:** A light kitchen refresh recovered more than it cost; a full mid-range kitchen recovered about half.
  - **How it is financed:** The share of remodels funded by home-equity borrowing, which turns a weak return into a compounding one.
- **The Catch:** Both studies are annual and partly survey-based, and regional markets swing widely inside the national averages. The direction is steady; the exact percentages are not.
