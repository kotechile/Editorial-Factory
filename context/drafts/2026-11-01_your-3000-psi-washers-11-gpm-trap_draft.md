Greenworks sells its 3,000-PSI pressure washer with two numbers that never meet: 3,000 pounds per square inch (PSI) at 1.1 gallons per minute (GPM), and 2.0 GPM at 100 PSI [1]. Your 3,000-PSI washer's 1.1 GPM trap is that second clause, because the big flow belongs to a rinse and the machine is only quick where it is not cleaning. The 2,300-PSI Gen 2 at $312.99 moves 1.2 GPM at its own top pressure on the same 14-amp motor, which makes it the better driveway buy for most houses [2]. The 3,000-PSI Gen 2 at $349.99 is the one to take if stained concrete is the job and a second pass is acceptable [1].

## The big picture:

Pressure and flow do different jobs, and only one of them sets how long a surface takes. Pressure is what lifts a stain off concrete or strips a film of algae off siding, while flow is the volume of water carrying that loosened dirt away [1][6]. A nozzle that narrows the spray raises pressure and cuts flow at the same time, which is why the two headline numbers sit at opposite ends of one setting.

Greenworks writes the condition into the feature list on all three pages, in the same sentence that carries the claim. The 3,000-PSI model reads 3,000 PSI at 1.1 GPM, then adds 2.0 GPM at 100 PSI in brackets, and the two smaller machines do the same with their own figures [1][2][3]. DeWalt publishes the identical qualification in the footnotes of a competing unit, where maximum pressure is measured at the 15-degree nozzle and maximum flow at the soap nozzle [5]. I read that bracketed clause twice before it landed: the larger flow is the rinse. Nobody is hiding this, and it is simply not the sentence a buyer repeats.

The larger point is what the ladder actually changes. All three machines carry a 14-amp brushless motor, a 25-foot hose, a 35-foot cord and five nozzles [1][2][3]. What moves up the ladder is pressure, from 2,300 to 3,000, while the flow at that pressure stays at 1.2 gallons a minute and then falls to 1.1 at the top [1][2]. A buyer comparing the three is choosing how hard the water hits, not how fast the work goes.

Popular Mechanics shows how the construction travels. Its review lists the 2,700-PSI Greenworks at 2,700 psi and 2.3 GPM with no condition attached to either, and the same page prices it at $379 [6]. That is not an error in the review, which also says a 1 GPM machine leaves the reviewer rinsing a large patio or long siding a little longer [6]. It is the same pair of numbers the maker prints, minus the clause that explains them.

## By the numbers

- **$260.83 — Cost per gallon a minute at pressure:** the 2,300-PSI machine's price against the 1.2 GPM it delivers at 2,300 PSI, against $316.66 for the 2,700 and $318.17 for the 3,000 at 1.1 GPM [1][2][3].
- **$0.117 — Cost per rated PSI:** the 3,000-PSI unit is the cheapest per pound of pressure at $0.117, against $0.136 for the 2,300 and $0.141 for the 2,700, because it carries the deepest discount of the three [1][2][3].
- **8.3% — Water the top rung gives up:** 1.1 GPM against 1.2 GPM at working pressure, a fall that runs opposite to the 30.4% rise in pressure across the ladder [1][2].
- **$37.00 — The step that buys less flow:** the gap between the 2,300 and the 3,000, which buys 700 more PSI and one tenth of a gallon a minute less water at pressure [1][2].

## What I'd watch:

The three machines differ on three things that change the buy, and none of them is the number on the box. The first is what the water strips: 2,300 PSI is the rating of the brand's own 12-inch rotating surface cleaner, so a driveway head and the cheapest machine are matched to each other [2][4]. The second is the pace across a surface, where the 2,300 and the 2,700 deliver the same 1.2 GPM and the 3,000 gives up a tenth of a gallon [1][2][3]. The third is what the extra pressure does to soft wood, where a narrower, harder stream marks a deck faster than it cleans it.

What I keep circling is the sale order rather than the spec order. The 3,000 sits $30.00 below the 2,700 today, and the 2,300 sits $37.00 below the 3,000, so the lineup reads cheapest to dearest as 2,300, 3,000, 2,700 [1][2][3]. A reader who ranks the boxes by PSI and buys the middle one pays the most for the same flow as the cheapest.

The storefront also sells a second 3,000-PSI listing under an older model number, and that number is what to check at checkout rather than the series name. The prior-generation listing carries the same 3,000 PSI and 2.0 GPM headline at a higher price on the same page, so the pair is a reminder that the model number, not the badge, decides what arrives. ## The catch

The accessory is the part that fails the logic of the ladder. Greenworks sells a universal 12-inch rotating surface cleaner for $39.99, and its specification block gives the maximum pressure as 2,300 PSI [4]. That is the rating of the cheapest machine and 700 PSI below the top rung, so the head that makes a driveway quick is not rated for the machine that would drive it hardest. A buyer who wants both has to run the 3,000 at part pressure or accept an accessory working above its stated ceiling.

The pricing has a second seam. A prior-generation 3,000-PSI listing on the same store sits at $424.99 with the same headline numbers, $75.00 above the Gen 2, and the 2,700 also ships as a bundle with a 15-inch cleaner and a wand extension for $479.98 [1][7]. Bundles and generations move the price more than the specification does, which is the opposite of how the model names read.

I could be wrong about how much the missing tenth of a gallon matters on a sloping driveway, where water pools and a pass takes longer anyway. What the pages do not carry is the one number that would settle it: the flow each machine delivers through the 15-degree nozzle specifically. The maker prints flow at peak pressure and flow at 100 PSI, and nothing in between, so the middle of the curve stays a guess for every buyer of every brand here.

## What we did not test

This review is built from three manufacturer product pages, one competitor's specification page and one publisher's review roundup; no unit was handled here, nothing was run, and no pressure or flow was measured by this desk. Every PSI and GPM figure belongs to the maker that published it under its own stated condition, and the one measured observation in the piece is Popular Mechanics', attributed to that review.
