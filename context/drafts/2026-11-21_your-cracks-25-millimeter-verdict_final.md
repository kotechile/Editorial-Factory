<!-- lead -->
A foundation crack is a measurement problem before it is a repair problem, and the measurement most guides skip is time. The width homeowners are told to fear — one-eighth of an inch — is not a standard's number. A concrete standard allows cracks from 0.004 to 0.016 inches on a loaded wall [3]. The ladder that does decide is BRE Digest 251, the UK Building Research Establishment's class scale for cracks: cracks up to 5 mm (three-sixteenths of an inch) are "aesthetic" and need only paint, mid-range widths let water in, and only the widest class is structural [1][2]. Getting the call wrong runs from $250 to $800 to repair a single crack [4] to the $5,179 average foundation-repair bill [5].

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

This article explains how engineers classify foundation cracks, for homeowners deciding whether a crack needs repair. The one finding that carries it: width alone settles nothing — direction and change over time do.

<!-- tension -->
## Width alone was never the diagnosis

BRE Digest 251 — the UK Building Research Establishment (BRE) — sorts crack damage into six classes, and the width number changes meaning at each one. Classes 0 through 2 — widths up to 5 mm — are "aesthetic" issues that need only paint [1]. Class 3, widths of 5 to 15 mm, lets water in and may need brickwork repointed [2]. Class 4, widths of 15 to 25 mm, is where door and window frames distort and walls lean or bulge; class 5, beyond 25 mm, is the first class the digest calls a stability problem, where beams lose bearing and the structure is generally unsafe [2]. The same digest warns against judging a crack on width alone, because a stair-stepped or offset crack says more than its narrowest point does [2].

Against that ladder, the folklore threshold looks arbitrary. The gap between the two numbers is not small. The one-eighth-inch line that repair-company guides quote as "cause for concern" [4] sits far above the widths a concrete standard calls fine. My read is that this gap explains most of the panic in week one: a homeowner applies a repair vendor's selling number to a wall the standard never judged. The vendor number is not a standard — it is a marketing line that happens to rhyme with one.

## By the numbers

- **$800 per crack:** The top of the $250-to-$800 band to repair a crack in an otherwise sound wall [4].
- **$1,000:** The top of the $300-to-$1,000 band for a structural engineer's report [4].
- **25 mm:** Where Digest 251's category 4 ends — frames distorted, walls leaning — and the stability class begins [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## How the trades price the call

The people who live on these numbers behave like the ladder exists. Cost guides price the decision in bands, not verdicts: crack repair at $250 to $800 per crack, bowed-wall reinforcement at $5,000 to $15,000, and sinking-foundation work at $4,500 to $20,000 [4]. Those bands only make sense if someone can tell a quiet cosmetic crack from an actively bowing wall — which is what the engineer's $300-to-$1,000 report buys [4]. In my read, the report becomes the cheap option the moment the crack's class is unclear: it costs a fraction of one repair band, and the bands it picks between differ by ten times or more.

**What I'd watch:** what the consumer-facing guidance itself settles on — change over time. This Old House's decision framing holds that the most important factor is not size alone but whether a crack is stable or changing, especially alongside water or doors that start to stick [5]. That is why practitioners date-stamp: a pencil mark at each end of the crack, re-read weeks later, turns a width into a trend. The trend is what the ladder was built on — damage that widens or comes back moves up the scale, damage that sits still does not [2].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch

The ladder is an engineering class scale, not a legal one, and it does not travel unchanged to American framing. Digest 251 was written for low-rise brick-and-block buildings; a poured-concrete or block foundation reads its classes sensibly, but a homeowner in a US insurance dispute will find that a US guide's width, not the digest, is what an adjuster answers with. The honest framing is that the first instrument costs a pencil and a calendar, and the second costs $300 to $1,000 [4] — and nothing in the ladder shortens the waiting. The part that still bothers me is that this discipline asks a homeowner to live with an open question, which is why so many pay the report fee instead.
