<!-- lead -->
On September 18, the battery plant at Moss Landing — California's flagship grid battery, already the site of one of the largest battery fires in history — started burning for the fourth time in seven years, as roughly 1,500 still-charged modules reignited on their own during decommissioning [1][2]. Twelve days later, on September 30, Governor Gavin Newsom signed two laws that begin paying homeowners for the batteries, electric vehicles and smart thermostats sitting in their own garages [4].

<!-- tension -->
**Why it matters:** California spent the last half-decade betting on a few giant, centralized lithium plants to bank daytime solar for evening peaks. The flagship one is now a recurring fire hazard, built with a chemistry and an enclosed design the industry has abandoned [1]. San Jose State professor Dustin Mulvaney put it bluntly: "If we had only known the worst-case scenarios of how not to build batteries, we wouldn't have built Moss Landing. Because it keeps coming back to haunt us." [1]

My read: the failure of the big battery quietly became the argument for the small one. California now runs the nation's largest battery fleet, and the state is all-in on storage [1] — yet the utility next door, PG&E, has kept its own Elkhorn battery plant dark since the January 2025 fire and doesn't plan to restart it until July 1, 2027 [3]. Into that gap steps SB 913, which orders the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) — the state agency that sets utility rules — and the grid operator to put a price on the power a customer-owned battery can send back when the grid is most stressed [5].

**By the numbers:**
- **1,500 — modules reignited:** roughly 1,500 still-charged, inaccessible modules spontaneously caught fire during decommissioning [1].
- **4th — incident since 2019:** the September 18 flare-up was the site's fourth safety incident in seven years [1].
- **2 — bills signed Sept 30:** SB 905 and SB 913 tell the CPUC to value and compensate customer batteries [4].
- **July 1, 2027 — Elkhorn restart:** PG&E's adjacent battery plant stays offline until mid-2027 [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
**What I'd watch:** The people closest to this are already reading the fire as a chemistry story. Moss Landing burned with nickel-manganese-cobalt and a tightly packed, enclosed layout; Canary Media notes Vistra "used a less-stable battery chemistry than today's installations" [3]. Today's home batteries use lithium iron phosphate (LFP), which doesn't fail the same way when damaged [1]. What I keep circling is whether homeowners are asking for that distinction by name, or just being sold "a battery."

Three signals I'd want answered next:

- **The CPUC's numbers.** The bill's own author said he was "shocked" Newsom signed it, and the trade group that backed it warns "implementation of the bill at the CPUC under the next governor will determine whether customers see lower bills" [5]. The signature is not yet the payout.
- **Who's left outside.** Newsom vetoed AB 1813, the community solar-battery program, the same day he signed the VPP bills [4]. Renters and anyone who can't front a battery and an EV aren't in the tent.
- **Whether the chemistry lesson travels.** The fire doesn't indict modern installations — the aging design and NMC chemistry do [1][3]. The open question is whether a homeowner sizing a backup system now gets told the difference.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
**The catch:** My read: this is not a clean passing of the torch. Moss Landing is an aging design, and Canary Media is careful to say the latest fire "does not reveal anything new about the safety of modern battery installations" [3]. The VPP payout is still hypothetical until the CPUC writes the valuation methodology — and this is the same governor who vetoed three VPP bills last year before reversing course [5]. The centralized bet failed in a very public way; the distributed bet is still just a signature on a September afternoon.

<!-- tldr -->
- **The Big Shift:** Moss Landing's grid battery burned for the fourth time on September 18; on September 30, Newsom signed SB 905 and SB 913 to pay homeowners for the batteries, EVs and thermostats they already own.
- **Why It Matters:** The state is giving up on a few giant batteries that keep failing and betting on the distributed ones homeowners own — which shifts who earns money when the grid is stressed, and puts the chemistry question in the homeowner's hands.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The CPUC valuation:** the state agency that sets utility rules now has to price customer-battery power, and its numbers decide whether bills actually drop.
  - **The LFP-vs-NMC question:** whether homeowners start asking for lithium iron phosphate, the chemistry that doesn't burn the way Moss Landing's nickel-manganese-cobalt did.
  - **Community solar:** Newsom vetoed AB 1813 the same day, so renters and non-battery-owners stay outside the tent.
- **The Catch:** Moss Landing is an old design the industry abandoned, so the fire doesn't indict modern batteries — but the payout is still just a signature until the CPUC writes the rules.
