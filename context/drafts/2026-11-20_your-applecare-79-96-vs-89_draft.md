<!-- lead -->
Apple's pooled AppleCare One plan covers up to three products for $19.99 a month. Divide that by the devices and your AppleCare costs $79.96 per device per year, whether or not anything breaks [1]. The one repair you can look up in advance is battery wear, which lists at $69 to $99 for Apple's own swap and $29.99 to $59.99 as a do-it-yourself (DIY) kit [3]. On the battery alone, the plan is roughly twice the repair bill and three times the kit. That is why the subscription only makes sense for a household that actually breaks things.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

Anyone running several Apple devices faces one decision: pay the pooled plan, cover each device on its own, or set money aside and pay Apple's repair prices yourself. Apple's own prices put each extra device at about one battery swap a year, so the bet rides on accidents and theft.

<!-- tension -->

## What the pooled plan prices

The plan is insurance, and its own page states what it covers. Apple's one-year warranty does not cover accidental damage or batteries that wear down from normal use, so an uncovered repair is a bill the owner pays [2]. AppleCare One exists to absorb those two gaps, and it prices them as a pool: up to three products starting at $19.99 a month until cancelled, each extra product at $5.99, or the whole family's products at $49.99 [1].

Divide the pool and the per-device price appears. The $19.99 tier spread over three products is $79.96 per device-year, and every $5.99 add-on works out to $71.88 a year for the device it carries [1]. The Family tier at $599.88 a year beats the Individual pool only past about seven and a half devices, which makes it a big-household tier, not a household default [1]. What I find hard to square is how rarely anyone runs that division, because the pool is sold as a bundle and read as a bargain.

Claims are capped too. A savings account has no such cap. AppleCare+ with Theft and Loss allows two claims a year, AppleCare One Individual three, and the Family tier six [1]. The theft-and-loss replacement itself carries a $149 service fee [1]. So the contract is a prepaid repair budget with a ceiling. The premium is due every month whether or not a claim arrives.

## By the numbers

- **$79.96 a device-year:** The pooled $19.99 tier divided by the three products it covers [1].
- **$71.88 a device-year:** What each $5.99 add-on costs annually — about one $69 out-of-warranty battery swap [1].
- **2 to 3 claims a year:** The theft-and-loss cap on AppleCare+ and on AppleCare One Individual and Family [1].

## Where the plan pays

Battery wear is the only failure whose price both sides publish. Apple's out-of-warranty battery swap runs $69 on the oldest models in iFixit's table of Apple's estimates, dated November 2024, and $99 on the newest [3]. The plan replaces a battery with no extra fee once it holds less than 80 percent of its original capacity [1]. A kit does the same job for $29.99 to $59.99, and iFixit puts the saving at $34 to $49 a repair [3]. At one swap every two years, a three-phone household faces about $133 a year at Apple's counter or $75 in kits. Both sit well under the pooled premium, and that is the honest version of the math.

Accidents are the other leg, and here the plan's fees are flat. A damaged iPhone screen or back glass runs a $29 to $99 service fee under the plan, and other accidental damage such as liquid damage a flat $99 [1]. The uninsured prices for the same events are the numbers Apple does not publish. Its own repair page shows no price table outside a browser. My read is that this gap is the real product. The plan sells certainty about a number Apple refuses to state.

Before paying either premium, measure the device against its own repair bill. The desk's [repair-or-replace calculator](/calculators/tech-debt-repair/) takes a repair estimate and a replacement price and returns the ratio the decision turns on. That is the check to run before a cracked screen becomes a shopping trip.

## The catch

The battery ladder is a vendor's table of Apple's November 2024 estimates, not a live price list [3]. Attach that date to every figure from it, because Apple's counter prices move without the table following. The swap rate in the break-even is also a guess, and no source states how often a battery needs replacing. A household that keeps phones four years halves the battery leg again.

The deeper limit sits on the accident leg. Apple publishes the plan's fees but not the uninsured ladder, so the true break-even is your own quote against $239.88 a year. The only reader who can settle it is the one who already dropped the phone. What I keep coming back to is that this is the opposite of most subscription creep. The plan is fairly priced as insurance, and the case for skipping it is that careful owners of fixable devices rarely collect.
