The window to close your flood gap has 44 days in it [3]. A federal flood policy does not start for 30 days, so the last day to buy one that is in force before that window shuts is Nov. 11 [3][6]. The math stayed abstract until October, when two storms in two states split a single damage bill into a half that gets paid and a half that does not. Florida's Office of Insurance Regulation (OIR) now counts about 55,000 claims and $582 million of losses from Hurricane Isaias, which came ashore near Destin on Oct. 9 with 105 mph winds [1][2]. Only the half with a payer ends up in that figure.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

A standard home policy covers wind and leaves out flood, so water is a separate buy [7][8]. What a state counts after a landfall is therefore a property-and-auto ledger: wind damage, car repairs, lost business, the harms with a payer at the end of them [1]. Moody's, the risk analytics firm, put flood-program claims from the same storm at $250 million, in a count kept apart from the state's [1]. 

What strikes me is that both numbers describe the same weather event, and only the wind half reaches an official tally. Storm Rachel's remains crossed Southern California and southern Nevada in the same week, pushing surf into Long Beach homes and setting a rain record in Las Vegas [8]. That harm came from the exact perils those standard home policies leave out.

## By the numbers

- **$582 million — Florida losses:** about 55,000 claims from Isaias, roughly 47,000 of them on homes, in the state's Oct. 25 snapshot [1].
- **8,500 against 3,500 — claims closed:** nearly 8,500 closed with no money, against just over 3,500 that closed with some, and another 1,454 open claims had paid [1].
- **86 in 100 — at-risk properties with no cover:** the share of at-risk properties without federal flood cover as of April 2026, per a Government Accountability Office (GAO) report, with policies down from 5.5 million in 2010 to 4.5 million in 2026 [5].
- **$250,000 and $100,000 — federal caps:** the most the program pays for a house and for what is inside it, on a policy that waits a month to start [5][6].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

- **The Dec. 11 vote:** the flood program has run on short extensions, 36 since 2017, and its last gap lasted 43 days with no new policies sold or renewed [3][4]. The next vote decides if a gap is a few weeks or a season.
- **What a gap stops:** sales and renewals stop, cover in force carries on, and claims keep getting paid from cash on hand, though the Treasury line fell from more than $30 billion to $1 billion during the last lapse [3][4]. The harm lands on a sale or a closing.
- **FEMA's counter-move:** the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) announced online quotes and sales at floodsmart.gov on Oct. 20, across almost 22,700 communities in the program [5]. What I am watching is whether self-serve checkout shortens the run from a decision to cover, or just makes the wait more plain.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

A gap sounds worse than it tends to be, since Congress has extended this program 36 times and claims on live policies keep paying [4]. Private flood firms keep selling while the federal door is shut, taking $784 million in net premiums in 2025 [7]. Where I've landed is that Nov. 11 is my own subtraction rather than a published rule: Dec. 11 minus one month [3][6].

The claim counts have limits of their own. The snapshot is about two weeks old, so a claim closed with no money in late October is not the same as a claim turned down, and those closures cover every line the state counts rather than water alone, so the flood bill never shows up in the early math [1]. Hurricane Michael's settled Florida record, $9.1 billion on nearly 159,000 claims with almost 130,000 paying something, is a final tally next to a storm still being worked [1]. The sum behind a gap this size, with its deductible, its limit and what a policy leaves out, is what a [coverage-gap calculator](/calculators/insurance-coverage-gap/) exists to make plain.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Two October storms split one bill into wind, which a home policy covers, and water, which it does not, just as the federal flood program's authority runs down to Dec. 11.
- **Why It Matters:** A new federal flood policy takes 30 days to start, so the last buy that is in force before the authority ends falls around Nov. 11, a date no forecast contains.
- **What I'd watch:**
  - **The extension vote:** the program runs on short extensions, so Dec. 11 is a habit rather than a cliff.
  - **The gap itself:** sales and renewals stop, cover in force carries on, and the Treasury line shrinks from $30 billion to $1 billion.
  - **Online sales:** the new FEMA checkout shortens the buy but not the 30-day wait after it.
- **The Catch:** extensions have been routine, private flood firms keep selling, and the Nov. 11 date is my own subtraction, not a rule anyone published.
