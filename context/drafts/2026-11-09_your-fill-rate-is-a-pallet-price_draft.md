<!-- lead -->
The price of warehouse space in the United States has split in two. In September the gap between the cheapest quarter of the market and the dearest was $3.79 a pallet, against $1.06 in July, and that gap now sets your fill rate — the share of order lines a warehouse can hand over complete [2].

The gap opened because two kinds of buyer stopped shopping in the same places. Cut-price bids from logistics firms pulled the cheap end down, while scarce space in tight cities pushed the dear end up [2].

For anyone who owns a fill rate, this is a change of problem. A fill rate depends on stock already sitting in the right building. Adding stock at a site that has just repriced upward raises the cost of the promise without improving the odds of keeping it.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Committed warehouse space is shrinking and repricing up while short-term space is plentiful but unevenly priced.
- **The number:** 3.6 times — how much wider the pallet-price gap grew since July.
- **The risk:** A fill target at the same site pays your fastest-rising cost.

<!-- tension -->

## Committed space is the scarce kind

Both readings cover the same three months. Flexe published its market report on October 21 [2]. The Logistics Managers' Index followed on November 3 with its October survey [1].

Six universities and the Council of Supply Chain Management Professionals run the index, which polls supply chain managers every month. Its scores are diffusion figures: above 50 means growth, below 50 means shrinkage, so they show direction and speed rather than size [1]. Stock levels jumped to 66.9, the fastest growth since September 2022. Stock costs rose to 88.8, the highest since March 2022. Warehouse space shrank again at 43.5, the sixth shrinking month in nine this year [1].

Inside that last figure sits the part that matters for service. Firms upstream read 37.5; firms downstream read 50.0, which means no change at all [1]. Smaller firms read 40.0, against 48.3 for larger ones. The space that is disappearing sits on the factory side of the chain, not at the retailer that already owns its sheds.

The report names the cause plainly [1]. Firms are "pulling inventories in as quickly as they can — often at high costs". They expect a strong holiday season and want the goods already on hand.

| Market | Short-term price, September | Change in the quarter |
|---|---|---|
| National index | $14.14 a pallet | +2.7% |
| Dallas | $10.97 a pallet | -9.2% |
| Lehigh Valley | $11.33 a pallet | +6.4% |

The supply behind those prices is still arriving. Gross warehouse completions reached almost 75 million square feet in the quarter [2]. Developers started another 88 million, the most since mid-2023 [3]. Space is not short in the country. It is short where the stock needs to stand.

What I keep circling is that the two documents do not disagree. One prices the commitment, the other prices the week. Storage prices in the survey rose to 80.7, the fastest since May 2022 [1]. The national short-term index rose 2.7% over the quarter, and four overbuilt cities fell outright [2].

<!-- by the numbers -->

## By the numbers

- **$3.79 a pallet:** The gap between the cheapest and dearest quarter of the market in September, against $1.06 in July [2].
- **43.5 on the space index:** Warehouse capacity shrank again, with upstream firms at 37.5 and downstream flat at 50.0 [1].
- **Eight of 21 markets:** The number that changed their buyer-or-seller rating during the quarter [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## Where your own site sits

Nine of the 21 markets Flexe tracks are rated Buyer's, six Neutral and six Seller's [2]. Eight changed rating in the quarter, so the local answer to "can I get space near my customers" now moves faster than the national one [2]. Demand pressure rose from 30 to 38 as imports held up, which means the leverage buyers gained over the summer is already narrowing.

The release says shippers are still bringing goods in but avoiding long-term commitments [3]. CoStar expects vacancy to peak early in 2027, not fall through next year. That is a window with a closing date on it.

What I am watching is whether the buyers who rented flexibility this quarter turn it into a network shape before the leverage goes. [Fewer, better-placed warehouses halve stock](/four-warehouses-halve-stock/), and the saving only lands if the sites are picked before the lease is signed.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The honest limit is that the two numbers describe different things. One is a survey of what happened to a respondent's own costs last month; the other is what short-term space actually cleared for in 21 markets. Both can be right, and neither proves the other wrong.

The publisher has an interest too. Flexe sells access to the market whose prices it indexes, so its report is a commercial house describing its own market, even where the data is plainly measured [2].

There is also a gap the index cannot price. Space you can rent in a fortnight is not automatically a warehouse that can ship an order complete in the same fortnight. What I find hard to square is that one quarter produced both the cheapest space in years in some cities and the fastest-rising storage costs in the survey. Both are true. The difference is the contract you sign.
