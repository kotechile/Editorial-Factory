<!-- lead -->
A heat pump quote priced at exactly 7.5 HSPF2 (heating seasonal performance factor, version 2 — the federal test of how much heat a unit delivers per unit of electricity) buys the weakest machine you can legally install. The number that decides whether the upgrade pays is not on the quote at all. It sits in the ductwork, which on average leaks 15 to 20% of the energy the new unit uses [5].

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
This is a guide for a homeowner pricing a heat pump this winter. Every split-system unit sold today clears the same federal floor, so quotes differ in how far above it they sit. The ducts feeding the unit are rarely priced at all.

<!-- tension -->
## Why the floor changed
Federal standards rewritten in 2015 took effect on January 1, 2023, because the Code of Federal Regulations (CFR) then raised the split-system floor to 14.3 SEER2 for cooling and 7.5 HSPF2 for heating [1]. It stands there as of the 2026 edition, so the floor is now the starting bid, not a selling point. The reason it moves your bill is load. Heating water and heating space together took 52 percent of the average household's energy consumption in 2020, the two biggest uses the home has [4]. So the machine's rating matters, but only inside a sealed system — and ducts installed in attics and crawlspaces leak on average, even in brand-new construction [5]. Beside the machine, Congress put one date. A qualifying unit earns a federal tax credit of up to $2,000, and it runs through installations to the close of 2032 [2].

## By the numbers
- **7.5:** The heating floor, in HSPF2 rating units, every split-system unit sold today must meet [1].
- **20% of space-conditioning energy:** What average duct leakage wastes, even in new construction [5].
- **$2,000:** The federal tax credit for a qualifying unit, through December 31, 2032 [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## Where the money leaks
Run the floor math on a real house. Say a home needs about 40 million Btu (British thermal units — the heat in roughly one cord of dry wood) over a winter. At the 7.5 HSPF2 floor that costs about 1,563 kilowatt-hours (kWh) of electricity. At a 10 HSPF2 tier it costs about 1,172 kWh. The better machine saves roughly 390 kWh a winter. At the measured 2025 residential price of 17.30¢ per kWh, that saving is about $68 [3]. Real money — but small. Now price the ducts. The same floor unit feeding a leaky run loses 234 to 313 kWh to the attic, which at that price is $41 to $54 a year spent on nothing [5]. What I find hard to square is how often the conversation stops at the model number. The gap between the floor and a good machine is smaller than the hole in the duct run that feeds either one.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch
The arithmetic cuts both ways here. Duct sealing is invisible on a test label, hard to inspect from a supply grille, and costs its own four figures. A quote that leaves it out is not always a scam — sometimes the sealed alternative is a machine you never buy. The saving from stepping above the floor also shrinks at each step, because heating electricity falls as one over the rating: 7.5 to 10 cuts it 25 percent, and 10 to 12.5 only 15 percent more [1]. The defensible order is simple. Take the $2,000 credit on the machine while the window is open [2], spend what remains on sealing the ducts the unit depends on, and treat anything above that as a comfort purchase — the same order in which [the winter's cheapest fuel decision already runs](/your-fuel-decided-your-winter-bill/).
