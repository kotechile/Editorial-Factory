<!-- lead -->
One Level 2 charging port puts out 7.2 kilowatts (kW) of power on its own 40-Amp circuit [1]. Plug a van in and it gains about 25 miles of range for every hour it sits there [3]. That one line settles more of a fleet's running cost than the van's spec sheet does.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

I keep reading the federal charging pages next to the power bills people send me, and the same thing shows up: the wall sets the bill, not the badge on the hood.

Chargers are not one product. A Level 2 unit spans 2.9 to 19.2 kW, runs on 240 volts in a house but 208 volts in a commercial building, and can pull 40 to 80 amps [2]. Each port wants its own 40-Amp circuit [1].

Going faster means three-phase power. A 2018 standard for that kind of charging (SAE J3068) covers 6 kW to 130 kW on the 208/120-volt and 480/277-volt feed those sites already have [4].

Then there is the ceiling nobody puts in the quote. Fast chargers can deliver up to 500 kW, yet many electric vehicles on the road today cannot take a charge above 200 kW [5]. A bigger box cannot push past what the van will accept.

The metered kilowatt-hour is the other half of the decision. U.S. retail power averaged 13.63 cents a kilowatt-hour in 2025, and state averages ran from 35.72 cents in Hawaii to 8.20 cents in North Dakota [6].

Less arrives in the battery than the meter charges for. Charging efficiency usually runs 84% to 93%, so the meter reads more than the pack receives [7]. The van then turns over 77% of the power it draws into motion at the wheels, against about 12% to 30% for a gasoline van [7].

And the federal help is gone. The Alternative Fuel Infrastructure Tax Credit is listed as expired on June 30, 2026 [8]. Any plan still counting it as live money is out of date.

<!-- By the numbers -->

## By the numbers

- **7.2 kW — One Level 2 port:** The common unit runs at up to 30 amps and needs its own dedicated 40-Amp circuit under the National Electric Code's Article 625 [1].
- **13.63 cents — 2025 power price:** The U.S. average retail price per kilowatt-hour, with state averages running from 35.72 cents in Hawaii to 8.20 cents in North Dakota [6].
- **84% to 93% — Charge efficiency:** The share of metered power that typically reaches the battery, before the van turns 77% of it into motion at the wheels [7].
- **Expired June 30, 2026 — The 30C credit:** Businesses could claim 6% of qualifying costs, capped at $100,000 per item, or 30% where the work met federal wage rules, with each charging port counted as its own item [8][9].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The people closest to this are not shopping for chargers. They are reading a service panel and a rate schedule, and I think that order is the whole game.

- **The circuit count:** Each port needs its own 40-Amp circuit [1], so the panel caps the port count before any hardware is ordered. One field note we hold has a delivery company ordering 50 electric vans before it asked the utility anything; the utility came back with a 26-month wait and $450,000 in transformer work to fast-charge all of them at once [11].
- **The charge window:** Staggering the overnight charge avoided both that upgrade and the peak demand charge in the same case [11]. The ports stayed the same; the schedule moved.
- **The van's own limit:** Many vehicles on the road cannot take more than 200 kW [5], so paying for a faster charger buys capacity the van turns down.
- **The rate class:** The same kilowatt-hour carries a different average by customer class, and a wider spread by state [6]. Where the depot sits moves the fuel line in the model.
- **The credit's status:** The window for the refueling credit closed on June 30, 2026 [8]. I'd want to know what took its place in anyone's budget, because no page here names a successor.
- **The pack horizon:** Makers often cover batteries for 8 years or 100,000 miles, and the models the Department of Energy cites put today's packs at 12 to 15 years in mild weather [10].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that the federal pages are sharp on the port, the price and the losses, and silent on the two numbers that differ site to site.

None of them prices a depot's demand charge, the wait for a bigger utility feed, or what it costs to hold power ready at 2 a.m. Those are the costs the field note's $450,000 surfaced, and no national table carries them [11].

The efficiency figure is a range for a reason [7]. A fleet that quotes the top of it is quoting a best case, not a measured depot.

And with the credit closed [8], the honest model rests on the power bill and the rate schedule. The electrical facts will hold for years. The incentive did not.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A depot's electric fleet cost is set at the wall — 7.2 kW and a dedicated 40-Amp circuit for a standard Level 2 port, three-phase power for anything faster, and a metered kilowatt-hour priced by class and state.
- **Why It Matters:** The charger and the van are separate buys with separate limits. A depot that sizes its ports from the van's spec sheet can pay for capacity the vehicle will not take, and miss the panel and transformer work that sets the schedule.
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether fleets settle the service panel and the rate schedule before the first van is ordered.
  - **The circuit count:** How many 40-Amp circuits the existing panel can feed, because that caps the port count.
  - **The charge window:** Whether overnight staggering removes the spike that triggers a transformer upgrade.
  - **The van's acceptance rate:** The top charge rate the van itself can take, which is often well under what a fast charger offers.
  - **The rate class:** The commercial price at that address, since the same kilowatt-hour is priced differently by class and by state.
- **The Catch:** National pages cover the port, the price and the losses, but the demand charge and the wait for a bigger utility feed are site-specific and unpublished, so the depot's own bill is the only place the real number exists. The federal refueling credit closed to new work on June 30, 2026.
