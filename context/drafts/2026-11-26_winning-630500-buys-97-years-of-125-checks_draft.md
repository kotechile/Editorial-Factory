<!-- lead -->
A Maine judge ordered a builder to pay a homeowner $630,500 back. The checks come at $125 a week, which pays the debt off in 97 years, before any interest [1]. The family won the case and still saw the payments stop for more than six months; the first check since then arrived this week [1]. If you hire a builder, the lesson is blunt: a court order is not money in your hand. The only cash you can truly hold is the cash you have not paid yet.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

One Maine family's court win shows what a judgment against a broke builder is worth. The deal ran $281,000; the award ran $630,500; the pay-back runs $125 a week and already stopped once. The state's own records say this outcome is common, not rare.

<!-- tension -->

## The order was real. The money was not.

The record is short [1]. In 2022, Adam Goode hired a builder a friend had praised, on a fixed-price deal of about $281,000 to rebuild his family's camp in Raymond, Maine down to the studs. A year went by and the camp was still unfit to live in. A text asked for "$20,000 more to continue your project." The family stopped paying. A second builder then found a roof and ceiling letting water into the house, with no vapor barrier under it. In court, records showed the company the builder claimed as his — Timberline Construction of Windham — was not active in the state's registry. It had been struck from it years before the deal was signed. A judge then found the builder broke the state's home-contract law again and again, and awarded more than $630,500 plus interest. The court order set the pay-back at $125 a week. Miss those checks, and the order warned he could face a contempt charge and jail.

The mechanism is the part that matters, and I can only call it structural. Maine does not license home builders at all, so nothing screens the person behind the name [2]. A company struck from the registry can still take deposits, and an award can only collect from someone who still has assets. The state's own words say how often that fails: even after wins in civil suits and criminal theft cases, Maine has been unable to collect a large share of the money its courts ordered paid, because the builders were bankrupt, broke for good, or gone [2]. The attorney general's office gets more complaints about builders than about any other line of work [2]. The verdict ends the argument. It does not end the problem.

## By the numbers

- **$630,500 plus interest:** The pay-back a Maine judge ordered against the camp's builder [1].
- **$125 a week:** The agreed payment, which retires the award in 97 years without interest [1].
- **1/3 of the deal price:** Maine's cap on a home-build deposit — about $93,666 on this $281,000 job [2].

## Where the leverage actually sits

The state's own offices point at the same answer, and they act on it. The attorney general's office will tell anyone whether a given builder has drawn complaints — a call or an online form away — and urges you to check a builder's record before work starts [2]. State law already builds the escrow shape into every deal over $3,000: the deal must be written, the deposit capped at a third, start and end dates set down, and every change order signed by both sides on paper [2]. A homeowner who keeps the unpaid balance large — last payment held until a second set of eyes checks the work — does on purpose what the Goode family could only do after the fact: hold money while they still hold leverage. The bond and insurance lines on a bid are a smaller backstop than most assume; a bond's ceiling — $25,000 flat in California — and its claim clocks are [fixed no matter what the job costs](/your-contractors-bond-dies-at-25000/) [3]. The complaint check is free. The registry search for the signing company is public record.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

Every one of these controls buys leverage, not certainty, and as I read the record the state's own numbers prove it. A deposit capped at a third still leaves two-thirds of a job exposed while the work runs. A written deal is only as strong as the live company behind it. The state's page lists theft convictions where collection still failed in large part [2]. What survives is modest: know who you are paying before the deposit moves, keep the last payment large, and treat a win in court as the start of a collection fight, not the end of one. The Goode family's first check in six months arrived this week. At this pace, the last one lands around the year 2123.
