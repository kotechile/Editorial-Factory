The window to close your flood gap has 44 days in it [3]. A federal flood policy does not start for 30 days, so the last day to buy one that is in force before that window shuts is Nov. 11 [3][6]. The sum stayed abstract until October, when two storms in two states split one bill into a half that gets paid and a half that does not. Florida's Office of Insurance Regulation (OIR) now counts about 55,000 claims and $582 million of losses from Hurricane Isaias, which came ashore near Destin on Oct. 9 with 105 mph winds [1][2]. None of those claims is a flood claim.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

A home policy covers wind and leaves out flood, so water in a storm is a separate buy rather than a line on the same bill [7][8]. What a state counts after a landfall is therefore a wind list: wind claims, car claims, lost business, the parts of the storm with a payer at the end of them [1]. Isaias's flood claims were put at $250 million by Moody's, the risk analytics firm, and they sit outside the total the state reported [1].

The bit that stuck with me is that both numbers describe one storm, and only one of them reaches a headline. Rachel's remains then crossed Southern California and southern Nevada in the same week, pushing surf into Long Beach homes and setting a rain record in Las Vegas, with harm that came from the perils those policies leave out [8].

## By the numbers

- **$582 million — Florida losses:** about 55,000 claims from Isaias, roughly 47,000 of them on homes, in the state's Oct. 25 snapshot [1].
- **8,500 against 3,500 — claims closed:** nearly 8,500 closed with no money, against just over 3,500 that closed with some, and another 1,454 open claims had paid [1].
- **86 in 100 — at-risk homes with no cover:** the share of at-risk homes without federal flood cover as of April 2026, per a Government Accountability Office (GAO) report, with policies down from 5.5 million in 2010 to 4.5 million in 2026 [5].
- **$250,000 and $100,000 — federal caps:** the most the program pays for a house and for what is inside it, on a policy that waits a month to start [5][6].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

- **The Dec. 11 vote:** the flood program has run on short extensions, 36 since 2017, and its last gap lasted 43 days with no new policies sold or renewed [3][4]. Whether this round brings another extension or something longer decides if a gap is a few weeks or a season.
- **What a gap stops:** sales and renewals stop, cover in force carries on, and claims keep getting paid from cash on hand, though the Treasury line fell from more than $30 billion to $1 billion last time [3][4]. The harm lands on a sale or a closing, not on a policy already paid for.
- **FEMA's counter-move:** the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) opened online quotes and sales at floodsmart.gov on Oct. 20, across almost 22,700 communities in the program [5]. What I'm watching is whether self-serve checkout shortens the run from a decision to cover, or just makes the wait more plain.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

A gap sounds worse than it tends to be, since Congress has extended this program 36 times and claims on live policies keep paying [4]. Private flood firms also keep selling while the federal door is shut, and that market took $784 million in net premiums in 2025 [7]. Where I've landed is that Nov. 11 is my own sum and not a date anyone published: it is Dec. 11 minus a month, and it says only when a policy bought that day would start [3][6].

The claim counts have limits of their own. The snapshot is about two weeks old, so a claim closed with no money in late October is not the same as a claim turned down, and those closures cover every line the state counts rather than water alone [1]. Hurricane Michael's settled Florida record, $9.1 billion on nearly 159,000 claims with almost 130,000 paying something, is a final tally next to a storm still being worked [1]. The sum behind a gap this size, with its deductible, its limit and what a policy leaves out, is what a [coverage-gap calculator](/calculators/insurance-coverage-gap/) exists to make plain.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Two October storms split one bill into wind, which a home policy covers, and water, which it does not, just as the federal flood program's authority runs down to Dec. 11.
- **Why It Matters:** A new federal flood policy takes 30 days to start, so the last buy that is in force before the authority ends falls around Nov. 11, a date no forecast contains.
- **What I'd watch:**
  - **The extension vote:** the program runs on short extensions, so Dec. 11 is a habit rather than a cliff.
  - **The gap itself:** sales and renewals stop, cover in force carries on, and the Treasury line shrinks from $30 billion to $1 billion.
  - **Online sales:** the new FEMA checkout shortens the buy but not the 30-day wait after it.
- **The Catch:** extensions have been routine, private flood firms keep selling, and the Nov. 11 date is the desk's own subtraction, not a rule anyone published.
