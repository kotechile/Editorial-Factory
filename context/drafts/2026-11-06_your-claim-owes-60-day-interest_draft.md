<!-- lead -->
Florida gives a property insurer 60 days from the day it hears about a storm claim to pay or refuse it, and any payment that lands after that date pays interest back to the day the notice arrived [3]. That one line changes what a homeowner should do with a slow file, because it turns waiting into something a carrier has to price. The lever is not a louder argument about the size of the damage. It is a dated record the carrier has to answer on a schedule, and much of that record is paper the insurer has to hand over anyway [1]. Read that way, the claim turns on the dates each side can prove, and the owner who keeps the calendar holds the proof.

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Late claim payments now carry interest the policy cannot waive.
- **The number:** 7 days — the deadline to confirm a claim message.
- **The risk:** Miss the notice bar and no clock protects you.

## The homeowner's clock and the carrier's

The calendar starts with the storm, not with the first phone call. Under Florida law a claim, or a claim the insurer has closed and the owner reopens, is barred unless notice of it reaches the insurer within one year after the loss [6]. A follow-up claim, the second and often larger one a contractor finds once the drywall is open, is barred unless notice reaches the insurer within 18 months of the loss [6]. For a hurricane, windstorm or heavy-rain loss the date of loss is the day the event is officially confirmed, not the day the roof let go [6]. Those are hard stops, and they sit on the homeowner's side of the table.

The carrier's clocks start when the claim arrives, and they run much shorter. The insurer must confirm the message, look into the loss once a proof of loss is filed, send someone out if the house needs looking at, and then pay or refuse the claim [1]. The payment deadline is the one with teeth: money that arrives late pays interest from the day the insurer first had notice, and the policy cannot waive that [3]. No clause in a homeowner's contract can write the clock out of the law.

That design is deliberate. A legislature that wanted to ban slow payment would have simply banned it; Florida instead put a price on delay, and the price moves every quarter with interest rates [5]. The interest is meant to pay back the household carrying a hotel bill and a mortgage at the same time. That household is usually the last one to ask for the line. What I keep circling is how lopsided the two sets of clocks are: the owner gets a year to notice a defect, and the carrier gets a payment deadline it has to beat.

## By the numbers

- **400 basis points — the interest markup:** Florida's rate is the averaged New York Fed discount rate plus 400 basis points, reset quarterly [5].
- **Three business days — the cheque window:** Washington calls it unfair to leave a settlement draft unhonored three business days after the bank's notice [7].
- **18 months — the follow-up window:** costs found after the first estimate must be noticed within 18 months of the loss [6].

## What the carrier owes on paper

The paper is the part a homeowner can actually push. In Florida the confirmation of a claim message is due inside seven calendar days, and if it is not in writing it has to be noted and dated in the claim file, a record the owner can later ask to see [1]. A visit to the house has its own thirty-day limit, and the person who makes it must hand over a document with his or her name and state adjuster license number [2]. With that number an owner can check who is actually pricing the loss.

What I keep coming back to is how much of that paperwork exists to be read later rather than acted on in the moment.

One more document matters, and almost nobody asks for it. The insurer's own adjuster writes a detailed estimate early on, and a copy of it must reach the owner inside seven days of it being written [1]. That figure is what the whole claim will be argued against. The law puts it in the owner's hands long before a settlement is offered. It also pairs with the second cheque in a replacement-cost claim, the money held back until the work is [done](/roof-claim-hidden-second-check-12-months/).

Washington does the same job with conduct rules instead of a payment law. An insurer there must say within thirty calendar days whether a first-party claim is accepted or refused, and must write again every thirty days while the file stays open [8]. The rule was also amended this autumn: since 18 October 2026, an insurer that prices a loss from a database, a survey or estimating software must tell the owner, on request, when the data was gathered, where it came from and which firms supplied it [8]. The same code says a fair investigation may not rest on a database alone [7]. An owner holding an estimate that looks nothing like the contractor's bid has a request worth writing down.

## The catch

None of these clocks forces a carrier to pay a disputed scope, and the interest is small money next to a rebuild. Florida's own law says a missed payment clock is a breach of the insurance code but "does not form the sole basis for a private cause of action," so the honest use of it is an interest line on the payment and a complaint to the state regulator, not a threat to sue [4]. The rules above are also two states' rules, not a national standard: the shape of the ask travels, the numbers do not, and an owner has to look up their own state's version before quoting it at an adjuster. My read is that the paperwork works as a test of the file and not a penalty: a claim that produces an estimate, a license number and a data source on request was always going to be documented, while one that produces none of them has told the owner something more useful than a settlement figure.
