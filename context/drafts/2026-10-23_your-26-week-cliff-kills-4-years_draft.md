On 11 September 2026, Oracle told federal regulators it had added $700 million to a plan for cutting jobs, a plan whose costs its filing calls "primarily" pay for people leaving [1]. Nineteen days later, ElevenLabs said it had spent $300 million buying shares back from its own staff at twice the value it set in February [4][5]. Both sums settle a promise made in stock, and both were decided by the employer alone. What separates them is what reaches your side of the table: Oracle's exit pays a 26-week ceiling in cash, then kills unvested awards that take 4 years to earn. The cliff is the door, not the vest date.

<!-- tension -->

My read starts with the paperwork, because one side is a filed number and the other is not.

## The big picture:

The quarterly report puts the 2026 job cut at "up to $2.1 billion" as of 31 August 2026, then adds a supplement of "approximately $700 million" for steps the company expects to take [1]. In plain terms, it is paying to end a payroll it no longer wants. It pays in weeks of base salary, while the stock it cancels was granted over four years [2][7].

Pay for a layoff and the vesting clock both count years of service, and they pay in units that never meet. The package gives four weeks of base for the first year, one more week for each year after that, and stops at 26 weeks [7]. Staff have spent about eight years at the firm on average, and 31% have been there ten years or more [2]. Head count fell from about 162,000 to about 141,000 in fiscal 2026, a drop of 21,000 people or 13% [3][2]. So a ten-year employee reaches 13 weeks of pay under the reported formula, and the cap only arrives at 23 years [7]. Three months of cash, set against whatever part of a four-year grant has not yet vested.

The filing is open about where the forfeited stock goes. Oracle counts "forfeitures of stock-based awards as they occur", and books the cost of service awards "over the service period of the award, which is generally four years" [2]. Cancelled units go back into the plan pool, ready to grant again. The company keeps the shares it had already promised, and the employee keeps the date.

ElevenLabs runs the mirror image, and says so. The firm took $300 million from outside investors to buy stock from staff at a $22 billion value, double the $11 billion of its February round, with Wellington and T. Rowe Price in the lead [4][5]. "We're committed to giving our team regular opportunities for liquidity," its post says, next to a head count now past 800 [4]. A year earlier the same firm ran a $100 million buyback at a $6.6 billion value [9]. That is a company making a market in its own paper once a year, at a price it and its chosen buyers agree.

## By the numbers

- **$2.8 billion — Severance plan ceiling:** $2.1 billion sat in the plan at 31 August 2026, plus about $700 million added for further steps; $324 million in cash went out that quarter [1].
- **26 weeks — Exit pay cap:** four weeks of base for the first year, one more per year after, capped at 26, on a package an internal document says cancels unvested stock [6][7].
- **$300 million — Staff buyback:** outside investors bought shares from staff at a $22 billion value, twice February's round, and the firm calls liquidity a regular commitment [4][5].
- **19 days — Gap between the two:** Oracle filed on 11 September 2026 and ElevenLabs posted on 30 September 2026, so two prices for one kind of asset landed in a single month [1][4].

## What I'd watch:

The people closest to this story are not arguing about values. They are reading contract lines, and the notices are public.

- **The early-vesting line:** whether a contract treats a cut as a vesting event or as a cancel is settled in the offer, and the reported Oracle terms put a layoff outside vesting [6][7]. I'd want to know how often that line gets read before signature.
- **The notice dates:** three Oracle notices filed on 14 September 2026 named a 13 November 2026 last day, 60 days apart [8]. A public date that far out makes the vest dates inside it visible.
- **The buyback rhythm:** ElevenLabs went from a $6.6 billion staff buyback to a $22 billion one in twelve months [9][5]. If a yearly buyback becomes normal, the question shifts from "will there be cash" to "which year's price do I take".
- **The pool:** forfeited units go back into the plan and get granted to someone else [2]. Who gets them, and at what price, is a quieter signal than any headline.

## The catch

None of this is misconduct, and the honest version is duller than the outrage version. Oracle's package is in the contract and, by the reporting, thinner than its peers — Salesforce's standard runs to 30 weeks, Microsoft's recent round to 39 [6]. The cancelled stock is the bigger line and the least discussed, but it is also common plan design: unvested means unearned until the service test is met.

A buyback is not a market either. It has one buyer, one price and terms the company does not publish, and it is the company's call; a firm that never runs one has broken no promise. I could be wrong, but the useful read is narrower than "private stock is safe": the price of a grant is an event, the event belongs to the employer, and the only thing you can size in advance is how much unvested pay sits in front of the next one.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** In September 2026 one employer added $700 million to a plan for cutting jobs while another spent $300 million buying staff shares at double its last value, 19 days apart. One company priced the same kind of asset up; the other cancelled it [1][4].
- **Why It Matters:** The cash formula in an exit counts years of service in weeks, capped at 26, while the stock it cancels was granted over four years. For a long-serving worker the cancelled units are usually the larger sum, and they never show up in the pay figure [2][7].
- **What I'd Watch:** Which event your employer is likely to schedule next, and what the contract says happens when the job ends.
  - **Early-vesting clauses:** wording that treats a layoff as a vesting event instead of a cancel, usually settled in the offer long before anyone needs it.
  - **Buyback rhythm:** a yearly buyback turns a single hope for cash into a series of priced windows, each with its own value [9][5].
  - **State layoff notices:** public filings that set a last day weeks ahead, which makes the vest dates inside that window visible [8].
- **The Catch:** Forfeiture on a layoff is normal plan design, and a buyback is a one-buyer arrangement the company can skip; the comparison here is structural, not a measure of any one person's loss.
