<!-- lead -->
On Walmart's Aug. 20 earnings call, the CFO said the retailer was eligible for about $2.9 billion in refunds of the IEEPA tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February — roughly 0.5% of its annual U.S. net sales — and that "substantially all" of it had already arrived. At the time Calcbench tallied the numbers on Aug. 28, that single largest figure appeared in no earnings release, no investor deck, and no SEC filing. It exists only as spoken words on a call recording. [3]

Across 101 public companies, the pattern is the same: about $18.7 billion in gross refunds disclosed as of the end of August, with the eleven biggest importers accounting for roughly $12 billion of it. [3]

<!-- tension -->
The Supreme Court ruled on Feb. 20, 2026, in Learning Resources v. Trump, that the International Emergency Economic Powers Act does not give the president the power to impose tariffs — a 6-3 decision that voided the "Liberation Day" and fentanyl-related levies. [1] But a ruling and a cash refund are different things. Roughly $166 billion in IEEPA duties had been collected. [2] By June 29, about $104 billion had been accepted for processing in CBP's new CAPE refund system, and about $71 billion had been sent to Treasury for disbursement. [2] Penn Wharton projects the full clawback could reach $175 billion. [6]

That money is not landing evenly. The refunds are hitting the biggest importers first: Caterpillar's second-quarter operating profit carried $392 million in expected IEEPA recoveries. [5] Deere booked $382 million year-to-date through its fiscal Q3, which ended Aug. 2. [4] Who gets paid is a function of who is big enough to have filed protective actions and whose entries moved fast enough to clear Phases I and II of the refund rollout — unliquidated and reconciliation entries, which together cover roughly $130 billion of the total. [2]

Who doesn't get paid is the more interesting question. Phase III, the category for finally-liquidated entries whose protest window has closed, has not been scheduled, and at a June 9 hearing CBP signaled that only importers of record who filed protective actions before the Court of International Trade would get processed at all. [2] The Justice Department has appealed the universal-refund order, arguing the trade court only has authority to refund parties that actually sued. [2] For a small importer that paid the duty and never filed a protective action, the refund may simply not come.

<!-- tactical-insight -->
Three moves for an ops leader this quarter.

First, reopen the pass-through conversation with your big vendors. Caterpillar got $392M back in a quarter it still paid about $400M in duties [5]; Deere's FY26 net tariff exposure is roughly $750 million after refunds, and it assumes no further refund activity this fiscal year [4]. The money a supplier books as a windfall is money that can come back to you as a price concession — but only if you ask, and only if you frame it as shared rather than pocketed. If a vendor's tariff line suddenly looks slimmer on its next call, that is your opening, not theirs.

Second, grade your own refund exposure before Phase III opens. If you are the importer of record on any IEEPA-paid entry, check whether it was unliquidated (Phase I) or a reconciliation entry (Phase II); those are the ones already moving. If your entries are finally liquidated and you never filed a protective suit, you are currently outside the process — and the window to act is bounded by a two-year statute of limitations whose start date is itself unresolved. [2] That is worth a call to your customs broker, not a quarter-end panic.

Third, expect this to show up in the numbers as "one-time." Because the recoveries are frequently booked as a non-recurring add-back, they are easy to miss. The largest single recovery in the data was disclosed only verbally on a call; Apple disclosed a roughly $2.19B impact through gross margin, and several companies tucked the figure into a non-GAAP reconciliation rather than a headline. [3] When a supplier reports record margin, ask what portion is tariff-refund one-timing before you extrapolate it into next quarter.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
The honest caveat: a refund is not a strategy. The IEEPA money is a windfall that unwinds a tax the Supreme Court said never should have existed — but two other tariff regimes survived. Section 232 duties on steel, aluminum and autos, and Section 301 duties on Chinese goods, were not struck down. [2] Deere still expects roughly $1.1 billion in FY26 direct tariff expense, and it says the run rate steps up toward $1 billion in 2027. [4] So the big OEMs and retailers are pocketing refunds this year while the underlying tariff architecture — and the cost it was built to pass through — remains in place. The clawback improves cash, not the baseline cost of your supply chain. Treat it as a reconciliation window, not permission to loosen sourcing discipline.
