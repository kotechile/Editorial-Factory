<!-- lead -->
STIHL (the Virginia Beach outdoor power maker) put a number on winter on 2026-10-21. Its first snow thrower, the company said, is rated for use in outdoor air as cold as -4°F. Five days later, on 2026-10-26, Health Canada recalled 37,667 Benchmark corded throwers sold through Home Hardware, because the power cord "may break when exposed to extreme cold weather, causing a potential electric shock injury" [1][2]. One notice sells the cold as a spec you can read, and the other shows what the cold actually breaks. It is not the battery.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The pack is where this industry has an answer. A cold-start function, a stated -4°F operating point (about -20°C) and a charger that reaches 80% in under ten minutes are the words of a solved problem [1]. The cord on a 15-amp thrower has no such words, and the recall never says what temperature breaks it [2]. I keep coming back to that gap, because it tells a buyer which half of the machine the market has actually built for winter.

Cold does real damage inside a cell. A Department of Energy merit review from Argonne National Lab reports that discharge capacity falls by as much as 65% between room temperature and -40°C [3]. That work used vehicle cells, not a cordless tool pack in a driveway, but the direction is not in doubt. The review describes lost run time, while the recall describes a cracked cord jacket and the shock risk that follows it.

## By the numbers

- **37,667 — Units recalled:** Benchmark model YF6022, a 22-inch, 15-amp electric thrower sold in Canada from November 2020 to May 2026 through one chain [2].
- **19 — Cord failures reported:** as of 2026-10-05, with no injuries logged, across those 37,667 units [2].
- **-4°F (-20°C) — Rated cold point:** STIHL's first snow thrower, paired with a new pro pack whose cold-start function carries the rating [1].
- **Up to 65% — Capacity lost to cold:** lab cells measured from 25°C to -40°C, in a Department of Energy battery review [3].

## What I'd watch:

The open question is whether -4°F stays one company's talking point or becomes a line every maker has to print. No one in this category competes on an operating temperature today, because the sheets sell volts, amp-hours and a count of car driveways cleared, and none of those numbers moves when the mercury drops [1]. I am watching for the first rival to answer with a figure of its own, since that is the moment cold turns into a spec.

- **The platform split:** the rated machine runs on STIHL's pro battery line, which fits more than 60 existing tools, while its homeowner tools use a separate system [1]. A buyer who wants the rating is buying into the pro line.
- **The pack numbers:** the same pack claims up to 60% more peak power and 20% longer run time than the model it replaces, plus 3,000 charge cycles [1]. Those decide what a spare costs in year three.
- **The remedy:** Health Canada tells owners to stop using the thrower and contact the retailer to destroy it for a refund [2]. The fix runs through one chain, in one country, on a product made by a Chinese supplier and sold under a house brand.
- **The cord:** the notice names a 22-inch, 15-amp machine and a cord that breaks in extreme cold, and gives no degree value [2]. That blank is where an unrated cord of your own lives.

The clearing width on the box deserves the same doubt that [the snow robot piece](/snow-robot-stops-at-6000-ft2-it-docks/) on this site applies to rated area.

The remedy is worth reading past the headline, because it is harsher than a repair kit. Owners are told to stop using the thrower, then to contact the chain for instructions on destroying it in exchange for a refund [2]. Canada's product safety law bars a recalled product from being redistributed, sold or even given away, and the notice is also posted to an international recall portal [2]. A machine sold across five and a half years of winters does not get a new cord; it gets scrapped.

## The catch

Both records are thin in the same spot. STIHL's -4°F is a maker's claim tied to a tool-and-pack pair, and the release names no test method behind it [1]. The recall says "extreme cold weather" and stops, so any degree value quoted for that cord was invented [2]. The 65% drop comes from vehicle cells run to -40°C, colder than most driveways get and a different battery from the one on the shelf [3].

I could be wrong about where this lands, but the rate argues for calm. Nineteen cord reports against 37,667 units is a small share, and a report is not a failure count. What the week does settle is narrower: the part of an electric snow machine built for cold is the part with a spec, and the part without one is the part that reached a safety agency.

## At a glance

**The shift:** Snow gear now prints a cold rating; corded machines are recalled for cords that fail in it.
**The number:** 37,667 throwers pulled in Canada, five days after a rival's -4°F rating.
**The risk:** The rating covers a tool-and-pack pair with no stated test.
