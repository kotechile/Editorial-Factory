<!-- lead -->
On Sept. 27 the U.S.-China Board of Trade recommended reduced tariffs on $30 billion of non-sensitive goods in each direction — household goods, toys, agricultural products and medical devices [1]. Forty-three days later, a separate fee of up to $120 per container on the ships carrying everything else is scheduled to switch back on [2][3]. The relief and the fee are two different instruments pointed in opposite directions, and the landed-cost math is what decides which one wins.

<!-- tension -->
I've been reading both releases this week, and what strikes me is that they don't talk to each other. The tariff relief is a *recommendation* — the White House's terms of reference say the actual cuts "will be determined and implemented in accordance with each side's domestic legal processes," with no amounts or dates attached [4]. The vessel fee, by contrast, is a hard deadline: it was suspended for one year beginning Nov. 10, 2025, and that suspension expires Nov. 9, 2026 [3].

The big picture: the fee doesn't scale with what's in the box. Under the Section 301 structure, a Chinese-built vessel pays $18 per net ton or $120 per container discharged, whichever is higher; a Chinese-operated vessel pays $50 per net ton [2]. A $10 toy and a $2,000 medical device riding the same ship pay the same per-container charge. So the goods-tariff relief on low-value, high-volume imports is measured in cents on the unit, while the vessel fee is a flat charge that ignores the tariff entirely.

**By the numbers:**
- **$30 billion each way:** the value of non-sensitive trade recommended for reduced tariff treatment, covering roughly 30% of U.S. exports to China [1].
- **$120 per container / $18 per net ton:** the fee on Chinese-built vessels, "whichever is higher," collected at the first U.S. port of arrival [2].
- **$50 per net ton:** the fee on vessels owned or operated by a Chinese entity [2].
- **Nov. 9, 2026:** the date the one-year suspension of these fees expires at 11:59 p.m. ET [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
Where this bites: the people closest to this are already organized against it. On Sept. 23, roughly 200 associations — the National Retail Federation, the Retail Industry Leaders Association, the Agriculture Transportation Coalition, the American Association of Port Authorities, the U.S. Chamber of Commerce among them — signed a joint letter urging USTR to extend the suspension [3]. Their argument, in the letter's own words, is that "imposing new or resumed fees on China-built or China-linked vessels would create immediate and far-reaching consequences for U.S. companies that rely on ocean transportation" [3].

What I'd watch: importers who buy almost nothing from China directly still charter capacity on Chinese-built tonnage, because those ships are a large share of global carrier capacity [3]. The letter notes the costs would "ripple across shipping networks, affect vessel deployment decisions, and increase uncertainty" for shippers who have no control over who built the ship [3]. The signal to track is whether USTR extends the pause before the Nov. 9 deadline — the letter explicitly asks for the extension to be announced during the Trump-Xi summit [3].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
The catch: neither side of this is settled. The tariff relief is not yet law, and the vessel fee is a scheduled expiry, not a confirmed reinstatement — USTR has said it will "consider whether it is appropriate to continue the suspension period" [3]. A relief that hasn't been priced and a fee that hasn't been collected are both still hypothetical. My read is that the collision matters because landed cost is the sum of both, and planning around either number alone is the exact mistake the two releases invite.

<!-- tldr -->
- **The Big Shift:** Washington recommended cutting tariffs on $30 billion of Chinese goods in each direction, while a separate fee of up to $120 per container on China-linked ships is set to resume Nov. 9, 2026.
- **Why It Matters:** The vessel fee is flat per container or per net ton and doesn't scale with cargo value, so it can erase the goods-tariff relief on low-value, high-volume imports — the very categories being relieved.
- **What I'd Watch:** whether USTR extends the vessel-fee suspension before the Nov. 9 deadline, and what the Trump-Xi summit yields on both fronts.
  - **Section 301 vessel fees:** government charges on ships owned, operated, or built in China, distinct from the tariffs on the goods they carry.
  - **The 30-for-30 framework:** the U.S.-China Board of Trade's reciprocal list of non-sensitive goods recommended for reduced tariffs.
- **The Catch:** the tariff relief is still a recommendation with no amounts or dates, and the vessel fee is a scheduled expiry USTR could still extend — neither number is locked in yet.
