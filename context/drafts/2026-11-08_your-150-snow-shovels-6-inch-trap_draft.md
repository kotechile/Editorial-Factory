<!-- lead -->
A two-car driveway in the second week of November comes down to what the hardware store has on the shelf, and this year that shelf holds a 12-inch machine and a 22-inch one. The Toro Power Shovel 38361 lists at $149.99 and calls itself ideal for small driveways that hold up to four cars [1]. The Snow Joe SJ627E (the 22-inch unit) lists at $249.99 and is sold for large driveways and walkways [2]. Your $150 snow shovel's 6-inch trap is the intake, printed two lines under that set of claims. It takes 6 inches of snow at a time, where the machine that costs $100 more takes 13.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

- **The shift:** Depth of cut decides the pass count; clearing width does not.
- **The numbers:** 13 inches of intake against 6, so one pass or two.
- **The risk:** 15 amps fills the outlet, and the maker's cord stops at 100 feet.

<!-- tension -->
## Why depth sets the walk

Both pages make the same shape of claim. Toro's bullet says the shovel suits small driveways that hold up to four cars, then adds "and up to 6-in of snow at a time" [1]. Snow Joe writes that the thrower cuts a path 22 inches wide by 13 inches deep in a single pass [2]. Neither sentence is false; they answer different questions, and only one of them is the question a February morning asks.

Depth governs the pass count because a single-stage rotor moves only the snow that fits under its housing. A 10-inch fall needs a second pass from a 6-inch intake, and that second pass doubles the width you walk [1]. A 24-foot driveway is 288 inches across, so the 12-inch cut is 24 passes at one pass deep and 48 at two, while the 22-inch cut with its 13-inch intake takes the same storm in 14 [2]. That is about 3.4 times the walking, and neither page prints it, because both makers sell the job in pounds a minute instead of passes.

The pounds a minute are 300 from Toro and 840 from Snow Joe [1]. Divided by the amps each page prints, those come to 40 pounds a minute per amp on the shovel and 56 on the thrower, so the wider machine is not just a bigger motor [2]. It also costs less per pound, at $0.298 for every pound a minute of rated capacity against the shovel's $0.500.

The other number the pages bury is the draw. Toro's motor row reads 7.5 amps [1]. Snow Joe's own description reads 15 [2]. Set against the 120 volts both pages print, that pair is 900 watts and 1,800 watts, and 1,800 is what a 15-amp household circuit carries in total.

Popular Mechanics states the consequence as a class limit, noting that corded machines are "limited to smaller, 15-amp motors that run on the power an outlet can provide" [3]. That sentence reads like a reason to buy batteries. It is more useful read as a reason to check what else sits on the circuit behind the outlet.

## By the numbers

- **$0.298:** the thrower's listed price divided by its rated 840 pounds a minute, against $0.500 for the shovel at 300 [2].
- **48 passes:** a 24-foot driveway in a 10-inch storm, doubled by the 6-inch intake, against 14 for the 22-inch cut [1].
- **1,800 watts:** 120 volts times the 15 amps Snow Joe prints, against 900 watts for the Toro's 7.5-amp row [2].

## Where each machine wins

The shovel is not a worse machine. It is a machine for a different shape of job, and the shape is narrow. A 12-inch cut fits between deck railings and along a front walk, its 20 feet of throw is longer than most of those spaces run, and its 7.5-amp motor leaves half the outlet free for the lights on the same circuit [1]. What I keep circling is how much of that fits under the same four-car headline the thrower's maker claims territory in.

The thrower wins wherever the snow lands somewhere you have to drive out of. Its 13-inch intake clears a plow pile in one pass, and 840 pounds a minute is the rated figure for the class Popular Mechanics says is capped by the outlet [2]. The price of that is the draw, since it wants an outlet with nothing else running and copper thick enough to carry the full load to wherever the snow is.

The cord is the part buyers budget for last and makers budget for first. Snow Joe publishes the requirement on the 18-inch model in the same family: a maximum length of 100 feet, a minimum of 12 gauge at that length, and 14 gauge at 25 feet [4]. That model is listed as sold out and is not a purchase option here, but the row belongs to a 15-amp machine and reads as the maker's own answer. Popular Mechanics makes the same point from the other side, noting that a power cord "substantially reduces their range" [3].

## The catch

Toro's own specification table disagrees with itself, and the disagreement is instructive. One row reads "Motor Amps 7.5 Amps", and two rows below it the motor type reads "15 Amp Series Wound Electric" [1]. The lower row is the one that governs every figure above, because it is the one the arithmetic uses, and I could not find a page on either host that resolves the two.

What I find hard to square is how little measured evidence sits behind either machine. Both makers publish pounds a minute and throw distance, and neither publishes the test behind them, so those capacities are vendor claims of the same class as the driveway badges. The pass arithmetic also assumes straight, full-width passes on a flat apron, which is the best case for both machines and the case no driveway quite is.

## What we did not test

The figures here come from two manufacturer product pages, one sister-model specification table and one publisher's roundup. Neither machine was handled here, nothing was run, and no capacity, throw distance or amp draw was measured. Every pounds-a-minute figure belongs to the maker that printed it, the watts and pass counts are arithmetic on those printed numbers, and the only outside voice in the piece is Popular Mechanics, quoted as a publisher rather than as a test of either machine. The Toro page lists its figure and routes the buyer to a dealer, so a dealer may price it differently on the day.
