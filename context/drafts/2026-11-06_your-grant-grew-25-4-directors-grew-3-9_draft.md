<!-- lead -->
For two and a half years the market has been bidding for you one level below the director line. Carta's mid-year pay report, published on 28 October, shows the median starting grant at that level rose 25.4% since the start of 2024, while your director's rose 3.9%, a sixth of the pace, and the grant above director level fell [1]. Two days earlier Microsoft offered a buyout to about 60,000 people, close to a quarter of its 223,000 staff, with no age or tenure test and a decision due in early December [3]. The buyout is priced on the part of pay that grew slowest, and the part that grew fastest is left to each worker's own award letter [2].

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Grants grow fastest below senior and have stopped growing above it [1].
- **The number:** 25.4%, the rise in the median grant at that level since 2024 [1].
- **The risk:** Buyouts still price packages in weeks of base pay, leaving the biggest line unpublished [2].

<!-- tension -->

## Two pay mixes, one offer email

Microsoft ran a narrower version of this in April. A retirement buyout went to about 8,750 United States staff at senior director level and below whose age plus years of service reached 70, and roughly 30% took it [3]. The company booked a $900 million charge for that plan in April, then cut 4,800 jobs in July [3]. On 26 October it widened the net to level 70 and below in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and India, with no age or tenure test, performance no longer a factor, and a decision due in the first week of December [3]. Carta's data, out two days later, shows what such an offer is now up against [1].

Carta reads its own figures as a story about management layers. Its report says firms "seem to believe that AI is simultaneously reducing the need for multiple layers of management and increasing the capabilities of top-level" staff [1]. My read is that the thinning does the work, not the generosity. Take away the layer above someone and the market has to bid for the work itself, and it bids in paper: a bigger starting grant, handed out more often. At the top that starting grant has stopped growing, so a senior person's raise has to come from a new title rather than from the grant that arrives with it.

The buyout was built for the older mix. Its cash is set by tenure. Staff at level 64 and below get one week of base pay for every six months served, levels 65 to 67 get two, and the cap is 39 weeks, as reported from offers seen by Business Insider [4]. The rule pays for time served rather than for the work someone does.

Its stock term shows up in the company's own note as four words, "continued stock vesting", with no length given [3]. So a senior engineer whose grant just rose a quarter gets a sum worked out from the line that did not. Grant size against base pay is the sum that decides the package, and [the total compensation visualizer](/calculators/total-comp/) sets the two side by side.

## By the numbers

- **25.4% — grant growth below senior:** The rise in the median starting grant at that level since January 2024 [1].
- **3.9% — grant growth at director level:** The same measure over the same span, about a sixth of the pace [1].
- **39 weeks of base pay — the cash cap:** What Microsoft's standard severance formula pays at most, three quarters of a year [4].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What the offer letter decides

The public reports do not agree on the stock term, and the gap is the story. GeekWire, briefed by the company, says the package includes "continued stock vesting" and gives no length [3]. An advisory firm reading offer papers for Microsoft staff says some who qualify may keep twelve months of scheduled vesting after a 31 December exit [5]. The note itself names neither figure and says only that staff will get details by email [2]. What I keep circling is how much of a senior package now sits inside that unnamed clause, because a grant worth several years of base pay turns on a sentence the note does not contain.

Two more details matter. Sales is not taking part, and some engineering groups and head-office teams are out, so who qualifies turns on the team someone sits in as much as on their level [3]. Microsoft also reported earnings two days after the note, the place where a plan like this first shows a cost: the April buyout carried a $900 million charge in that line [3].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

Carta's figures come from private firms on its platform, where a grant is a share of a company with no daily price. Microsoft pays in stock that a public market values every trading day, so the two sets describe different things even when the percentages look alike. Seniority still pays more in dollars, too: Carta reports the median starting salary for a senior director was more than twice that of the level above entry, and a faster rate of climb is not a bigger number [1]. What I keep returning to is the mismatch in what the two numbers are measuring. The finding is narrower than "juniors out-earn directors" and firmer than "grants went up". The market has re-priced how fast it adds stock at each level, and the exit offers beside it are still written for a package where salary was most of the money.
