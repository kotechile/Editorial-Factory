<!-- lead -->
26 teams built their own software with AI tools in one two-week stretch, and 23 of them report they saved nothing at all [1]. That cohort is the first hard test of a bet many teams now make at renewal time, because AI coding made building look cheap. The switch to building is real. A build that demonstrably paid has not been shown once.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
A first-edition research panel logged what software buyers pay and what teams that built their own tools claimed it saved them. For engineering and finance leaders, the finding is blunt: building is mainstream, but verified savings are not.

<!-- tension -->
## Where the build bet lands
The record is short. HissingKitty Research, a shop run by people who spent 20 years inside enterprise software firms, published its first monthly report on October 19 and closed the October edition on October 31, covering October 17 through 31 [1]. Buyers shared 177 yearly bills across 68 tools, worth $6.86 million in total [1]. One NetSuite bill of about $2 million is 29% of that figure, so the total says more about who shared than about typical spend [1]. The panel also listed 261 AI-native alternatives against 213 incumbent tools, and HubSpot and Salesforce drew 17 new alternatives each [1].

The verdicts show where the choice lands. Across 64 software categories, the editors rated 15 "build it", 37 "build part of it" and 12 "keep paying" [1]. Most teams keep the core system and build the costly edge around it. And the switch is not fringe behaviour: the report cites McKinsey's Global Survey on the State of AI, 1,719 respondents, in which 32% at organizations using AI skipped buying at least one product to build it with AI coding tools [1]. I read the builds table twice before the shape of it landed. Twenty-six documented builds in the window, and 23 of them state no saving at all; no verified replacement report exists, so realized payback cannot be measured [1].

The buy side runs on the same discipline. On AI spending, the biggest cut most teams get is a committed-spend deal with their cloud or model vendor: promise volume, pay less per token, use just as many tokens [2]. That is procurement, not architecture. The one builder's counter-case sits at the small end of the bill. A solo founder rebuilt his landing page, testimonials and docs chatbot with AI, and dropped about €300 a month for a few days of work [3]. That is a real saving. It is also the smallest kind of bill, and it does not travel upmarket.

## By the numbers
- **37 categories:** Rated "build part of it" out of the 64 the editors rated — keep the core, build the edge [1].
- **15 categories:** Rated "build it" outright; 12 more were rated "keep paying" [1].
- **$120,000 a year:** ServiceTitan's median shared bill, the highest in the panel, from three bills [1].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## What the closest teams do
The report's own limits section reads like a checklist for the decision itself [1]. The sample is self-selected: people who share bills and reviews may be less happy with their vendors than the average buyer. The savings are self-reported, usually posted soon after launch. And teams whose builds failed rarely post about it. My reading, held loosely, is that the teams closest to this have already priced the lesson in: they cost a build the way they cost a vendor — engineer-years, upkeep, on-call — before the commit. That is the long-horizon standard for enterprise builds — [the sticker price is the smallest part of the bill](/enterprisebuildvsbuy-the-250k-ai-upkeep-tax/) — and this cohort has not met it [1]. The answer that settles the open question is the first verified replacement report. Zero exist so far [1]. The first one that documents a measured payback becomes the first real counterexample anyone can cite.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch
This cohort measures savings reporting, not savings. Twenty-six builds is a small, self-chosen sample from a first edition, and "no saving stated" is not the same as "no saving made" [1]. What the window proves is an absence of evidence: with zero verified payback reports, nobody can point you to a build that demonstrably paid. So the rule is not "never build with AI". Price the build against the renewal before you commit. If you cannot state what the replaced bill was, you are not making a build-vs-buy decision. I would call that a hobby, and the December numbers will tell us whether the first counterexample has arrived.
