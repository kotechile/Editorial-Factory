The engine maker prints the bill most buyers never add up. Briggs & Stratton says to change the oil after the first 5 hours of use, then every 50 hours or yearly, whichever comes first [1]. A Honda dealer's storage guide adds a fuel rule: gas left in the tank has to be treated inside 30 days and drained within 90 [2]. The battery mower next to it has no oil and no tank. Consumer Reports measured its class average at 45 to 50 minutes of cut per charge, enough for a quarter acre [3]. So the trade is a 45-minute ceiling against a service list that never hits zero.

## At a glance

This article is for the homeowner choosing between a gas mower and a battery one this spring. The finding: the gas side carries a written upkeep schedule — oil at 5 hours, then every 50 hours or yearly — and fuel that decays in storage, while a cordless that can finish the lawn on one charge carries none of it.

<!-- tension -->

## The schedule you inherited

What I keep circling is that the 50-hour rule is not advice, but a condition the maker attaches to the engine. Briggs & Stratton wants the first oil change after the first 5 hours, then a change every 50 hours or once a year, whichever comes first [1]. A small suburban lot runs a mower 20 to 30 hours a season. That means the calendar triggers the work, not the hour meter. Every spring brings oil, a spark plug, an air filter, and an engine that must start or be diagnosed.

The fuel rule is the part owners skip until spring exposes it. Nichols Equipment, a Honda dealer, splits storage into windows. Gas in the tank for 30 to 90 days needs a stabilizer mixed into fresh fuel, then a 10-minute run to move the treated gas through the system [2]. Past 90 days, the whole fuel system must be emptied, tank included [2]. The same page is blunt about timing: a stabilizer cannot fix fuel that has already gone stale [2]. That is why the spring no-start is so common. The fuel aged on a clock nobody saw, and the carburetor kept score.

## By the numbers

- **50 hours or yearly:** the oil-change interval Briggs & Stratton prints for its small engines, after the first 5-hour change [1].
- **90 days:** how long gas may sit in a stored mower before the entire fuel system must be drained [2].
- **45 to 50 minutes:** the average run time per charge Consumer Reports measured across battery walk-behind mowers [3].

## What the cordless actually removes

My read on the comparison is that buyers price the wrong side of it. The usual worry is what the cordless gives up: run time, peak torque, the freedom to refill and keep going. But the gas mower charges its price after the sale. It asks for oil on a 50-hour or yearly clock, a new filter, a plug, and fuel rotated inside a 90-day window [1][2]. The battery mower asks for planning instead. Consumer Reports found two-thirds of the electric walk-behinds it rated could finish a quarter acre on one charge [3]. A 7.5 amp-hour pack holds 420 watt-hours of that work [5]. If your lawn fits inside one charge, the upkeep line simply goes away. There is no oil to change and no tank to drain. Nothing on the gas side's list has an equivalent on the cordless side, which is the whole argument for paying the higher sticker price.

The running cost is easy to state. A charge or two a season draws from the wall, at a national grid intensity of 0.81 pounds of carbon dioxide per kilowatt-hour [4]. No jerry can, no mixed fuel, no oil bottle in the garage. The trade runs across the rest of the desk's [outdoor power and snow equipment](/categories/outdoor-power-equipment/) coverage, because every machine in this class either carries a service list or a charge one.

## The catch

What I'd watch next is the size of the tax, because honesty about it matters. A season's oil, plug and filter cost a few tens of dollars and one working hour. A battery mower often costs hundreds more at the till. So the schedule alone does not close the price gap in year one. What it changes is the character of owning the machine. The gas mower degrades when it is ignored, and it starts with the fuel first, per the 90-day rule [2]. The cordless degrades mainly through use and years of charge cycles, which is a slower and kinder clock [5]. And power still belongs to gas: Consumer Reports' testers call it unbeatable there [3]. A cordless that bogs down in tall, wet grass has not lost a service race. It has hit the limit it was bought around.

The honest rule follows. If your lawn is a quarter acre or less and you can finish it inside one charge, the cordless deletes a recurring chore, not just a price. If your lot takes several charges or your grass is heavy, the gas machine's upkeep is the salary you pay for its torque, and the service list is the least of what you owe it.
