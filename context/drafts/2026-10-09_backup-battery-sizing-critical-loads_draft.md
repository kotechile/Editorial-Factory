The average American home buys 899 kilowatt-hours (kWh) of electricity a month, or 10,791 kWh a year [1]. That is the number most buyers use to size a backup battery. It is also the wrong one.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The home battery market grew up around a monthly utility bill, not around an outage.

A bill measures a whole year of ordinary life: the dryer, the pool pump, the air conditioning, the car chargers. An outage asks a much narrower question. For a few days, what has to keep running?

The gap between those two numbers is where buyers get surprised. A bank that looks generous next to a bill can be spent in one cold night once a furnace or a well pump sits on the backed-up circuit.

I keep coming back to how pricing changed the math underneath all of this. The U.S. average residential electricity price was 18.31 cents per kWh in July 2026, up 4.9% from a year earlier [2]. The more a utility charges, the more a battery earns back through daily load shifting, and the easier it is to buy one without ever listing the circuits it must carry in a storm.

## By the numbers

- **899 kWh — The wrong sizing unit:** The average U.S. household buys about 899 kWh a month, or 10,791 kWh a year, according to the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) [1]. That full-year total is what many battery quotes are measured against.
- **18.31 cents — The price being avoided:** The average U.S. residential price reached 18.31 cents per kWh in July 2026, 4.9% above a year earlier [2]. Higher rates strengthen the daily case for storage.
- **48 hours — The freezer clock:** A full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours, and a refrigerator for only about 4 hours; food is unsafe after 2 hours above 40 degrees [3]. These hold-times, not the annual bill, set the load a bank has to cover.
- **7.15 kW — The generation baseline:** For its analyses, the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL) assumes an average home solar system of 7.15 kilowatts, inside a range of 3 to 11 [4]. That figure sizes generation, yet it is often borrowed as if it sized storage.

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

Installers who actually work outages do not start with the utility bill. They start with a list.

- **A critical-loads list first:** The people who get this right write down what must run — a well pump, a furnace or boiler circulator, a refrigerator, a network closet — and add up the watts. The first meeting is a worksheet, not a sales pitch.
- **A dedicated subpanel:** Instead of backing up the whole panel, they move only those circuits onto a small subpanel fed by the battery. That keeps the bank small and the runtime long. I would want the subpanel schedule in hand before I believed any "days of backup" claim.
- **A chemistry choice with a reason:** Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) banks are the common pick for daily cycling and long life, while cheaper packs often trade cycle life for up-front price. What I keep circling is how rarely the chemistry is explained in plain terms — "safe and durable" is not a specification.
- **An honest autonomy number:** A useful quote states hours of storage against a named load list, never a vague "partial backup." When a proposal says "up to several days," the load list behind that number is the thing to ask for.

## The catch

My read is that the sizing fix is real, and that it is also a project rather than a checkbox.

A critical-loads subpanel means an electrician, a new enclosure, and a permit in most places — money that buys no battery capacity at all. Sizing conservatively also means accepting a smaller, cheaper bank that will not carry air conditioning through a heat wave. That trade is the point.

The hold-times are a floor, not a promise. A freezer left closed for 48 hours survives; one opened twice does not [3]. Storage helps only if the loads behind it are managed.

The whole-house figure is not useless, either — it is simply a different job. A large bank earns its keep on daily price arbitrage against an 18.31-cent rate [2], and on whole-panel backup for owners who will pay for it. My worry is narrower: that the two jobs get blended into one number at the point of sale.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Home backup batteries are usually sized against an annual utility bill, but an outage only needs a short list of critical circuits to keep running. That mismatch — not the battery's chemistry — is why a "fully backed up" home can run out of power in a single night.
- **Why It Matters:** A bank bought to last days can be spent in hours if the wrong circuits sit behind it. Sizing against critical loads keeps the purchase small and the runtime long, and it gets more valuable as rates rise (18.31 cents per kWh in July 2026 [2]).
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The critical-loads list:** Whether a quote starts from named circuits and their watts, not from a monthly bill [1][3].
  - **The subpanel:** Whether only those circuits move behind the battery, which is what stretches runtime [3].
  - **The autonomy claim:** Whether "days of backup" arrives with a load list and hours attached, or stays a slogan.
- **The Catch:** A critical-loads subpanel costs real money and buys no capacity; the freezer hold-times are a floor, not a promise [3]; and the whole-house number is still the right one for daily arbitrage and whole-panel backup [2].
