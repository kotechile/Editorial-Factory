<!-- lead -->
Apple closed its fiscal year holding $14.5 billion of stock, two and a half times what it held a year earlier, and added $32.7 billion of purchase commitments in thirteen weeks [1]. Its product margin rose anyway, with management crediting mix and tariff refunds and naming memory as a cost that partly offset the gain [1]. Both facts hold together because of the arithmetic in the middle. Memory contract prices are forecast to rise about 20% this quarter [3]. The range most teams use for [the cost of holding stock](/inventory-carrying-cost-rate/) is 20% to 30% a year, which is 5% to 7.5% a quarter [7]. Your stock is the cheaper memory.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Memory now reprices faster each quarter than stock costs to hold.
- **The number:** 20% a quarter against 5% to 7.5%.
- **The risk:** The hedge lasts about one quarter; the commitment outlives it.

<!-- tension -->

## The ladder ran out of room

Memory did not jump once. Dynamic random-access memory (DRAM) is the chip that holds data while a machine runs, and its contract price — the quarterly rate large buyers negotiate, not a retail sticker — rose about 80% in the second quarter [3]. It rose about 40% in the third, and is forecast to rise about 20% in the fourth [3]. The slowdown is not the relief it looks like. Those three rises compound to roughly three times the starting price, and I keep coming back to that compounding, because it decides whether the hedge is worth carrying [3].

| Quarter | DRAM contract price | One quarter of holding cost |
|---|---|---|
| Second 2026 | +80% | 5% to 7.5% |
| Third 2026 | +40% | 5% to 7.5% |
| Fourth 2026 (forecast) | +20% | 5% to 7.5% |

The supply is being steered away from the buyers who need it most. Suppliers are assigning advanced capacity to server memory and enterprise drives, where AI inference demand pays better [3]. Long-term agreements are also starting to cap how high prices can climb [3]. A buyer of ordinary components therefore faces a market that is short and repricing upward.

Apple answered it three ways in the same quarter. It leaned on inventory bought in the June quarter, raised prices by $100 on the iPhone 17 and on some MacBooks, and lifted its purchase commitments sharply [5]. Product gross margin, the share of sales left after the direct cost of the goods, finished the year at 39.3% against 36.8% a year earlier [1]. The first of those three moves is the one an operations team can copy without renegotiating anything. Apple bought the memory before the price moved.

The rest of the manufacturing base is buying forward at the same time. The Institute for Supply Management (ISM) reports customers' inventories at 41.6, in "too low" territory, for a 25th consecutive month [4]. No industry reported customers' inventories as too high in October [4]. Its prices index has risen for 25 months, and memory has been listed as a material in short supply for ten [4]. Buyers are not holding stock because they are careless. They are holding it because the shelf in front of them is empty and the quote behind them keeps moving.

## By the numbers

- **$14.5 billion — Apple's stock at year end:** Two and a half times the $5.7 billion it held a year earlier [1].
- **$89.7 billion — manufacturing purchase obligations:** Committed component and factory purchases, mostly due within twelve months, equal to 123 days of direct costs [1].
- **5% to 7.5% — one quarter of the holding cost:** The 20% to 30% annual range divided by four, against a forecast 20% rise [7].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## When the hedge stops paying

The hedge is already losing value, and Apple said so on the call. Apple guided margin to 45% to 46% for the December quarter, against 47.9% in the one just reported [5]. The chief financial officer expects memory costs to keep rising into next year [5]. The benefit of carry-in inventory declines over time, which is the same statement in accounting language: the stock bought at the old price runs out.

What I keep circling is that the balance sheet now carries two measures of the same bet, and they move apart. The stock line shows what has already been paid for; the obligations line shows what has been promised. Apple's obligations grew almost ten times as fast as its stock in the same thirteen weeks [1]. A buyer reading only the stock line sees a modest build. Reading both, the company has committed four months of production to a market it expects to keep repricing.

Three lines answer the question next quarter. The first is whether the obligations keep outrunning the stock, which would say the company is buying the shortage rather than the demand. The second is the customers' inventories index, which has not moved off 41.6 for a year; when it turns up, the forward buying has gone past what the shelves can absorb [4]. The third is the long-term agreements that TrendForce says are capping prices [3]. A ceiling on the price rise is a ceiling on what the hedge earns.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The obligations are not money spent. They are commitments, payable mostly within twelve months, and Apple accrues cancellation fee reserves for orders it cannot use [1]. So that figure is a claim on next year's cash, not a payment already made. A demand shock turns it into either a penalty or inventory bought at the top of the market.

The arithmetic also leans on a convention. Clear Spider reckons stock costs 20% to 30% of its value to hold for a year [7]. SourceDay gives the same range for what it calls holding costs [8]. Neither is an audited number, and each bundles money, space, insurance and obsolescence into one figure. I find the direction of the comparison robust and the precise multiple less so: a firm with cheap capital and free racking sits nearer the bottom of that range, and a firm holding short-life components nearer the top.

Two limits are worth carrying into the decision. The 20% applies to memory, while the holding cost applies to everything in the warehouse, so this is a spread on one input and not a reason to raise stock across the catalogue. And the hedge pays only if the units sell; stock bought against a price that rose is still stock, and a margin protected by carrying inventory is protected once. What I would not do is read Apple's year as permission to build.
