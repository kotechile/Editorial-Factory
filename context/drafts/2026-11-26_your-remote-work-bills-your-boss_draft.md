<!-- lead -->
A senior engineer kept working from Spain on a tourist-style visa, and the contract authority his employer handed him built the company a permanent establishment — treaty law's word for a branch-like presence that makes a foreign company taxable where it exists. The back-dated corporate tax, fines and forced social-security bills ran to about $110,000 on this desk's own field note. The tax problem your remote work creates is not always yours. It can land on your employer, and the tests that decide it are not the residency clock every guide repeats.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
This is about what your employer owes because you work abroad, not what you owe. Three tests decide it: a 90-day US carve-out, a 183-day services test, and a contract-authority test with no day count at all — plus a 25% activity share that decides your social-security law.

<!-- tension -->
## The clocks your employer answers to
The person every guide talks about is you — whether you become a resident somewhere after 183 days and file your own return there. The [state residency exit carries its own clock set](/move-abroad-still-a-resident-546-days/), and even that piece stops at the personal layer. What I find hard to square is how often a move is cleared on the personal clock alone. Your employer faces its own tests, and those tests ask a different question: does the company itself carry on business in the host country? That is why the answer turns on who signs contracts and who sells to local buyers.

Start with the narrowest test, which protects a short stay. US law does not count a travelling employee's services against the company that sent them, so the stay does not make the company taxable in the US. The carve-out applies when the employee stays in the US no more than 90 days in the tax year and the pay for those services stays under $3,000 [1]. That is a statutory de minimis — a smallness threshold below which the law does not count the activity. It is as generous as the US side gets.

The host country's test is wider. HM Revenue and Customs (HMRC), the UK tax authority, states in its treaty guidance that the services carve-out normally needs a person present more than 183 days in any 12-month period. The services must also be sold to third parties [2]. Read the second half closely: an engineer who works only on your own product may never meet it. A founder who sells to local customers meets it the day the first invoice clears. Most treaties use the same 183-day figure, so the test reads almost the same in Portugal, Spain or Japan.

The test with no day count catches senior people. Treaty law builds a dependent-agent presence when a person in the host country habitually exercises an authority to sign contracts for the enterprise [3]. HMRC's manual paraphrases the clause as someone who "habitually plays the principal role leading to the conclusion of contracts". An engineer on a tourist visa holds no such authority and stays invisible. A vice-president who signs vendor deals carries it from day one. That is how a 90-day visit can grow into a corporate presence over seven months.

A third regime sits under the income tax layer. European social-security coordination asks which country covers a worker active in two or more member states. The test is a share, not a day count. When less than 25% of your activity stays in your home country, that low share signals you work mostly elsewhere [4]. Fall below the line and you may not be insured where you live. The coverage follows the work, not the address.

## By the numbers
- **90 days:** the US ceiling below which a travelling employee's services do not make the company taxable there [1].
- **183 days:** the presence test, over any 12-month window, before service profits count as a corporate presence [2].
- **25% activity share:** the EU line below which your social-security cover can leave your home state [4].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## Where this bites
The advisers who handle this daily check two facts about a remote hire before anything else. One: what can the employee sign? Two: does the host country see customers of the company? Both are design questions, and reorganising signing authority is cheaper than a back-dated corporate bill. The 25% share is measured on turnover, work time, services and income together, so a part-time worker can drift across the line without any wrong turn. What I watch for is the quiet upgrade of a remote contractor into someone who "can approve deals" — that phrase, in human-resources language, is treaty language too.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch
None of these thresholds protects a company from its own employee's seniority. The 183-day services test and the 90-day US carve-out both count days. The contract-authority test counts none — it recognises power instead. That asymmetry is the honest limit of every digital-nomad visa pitch. The visa solves your right to be there. It says nothing about the presence your role can build for the company paying you. What I find sobering is that the failure case behind this desk's field note involved no fraud and no clever structure — only an executive title, a sales season, and a test that was never checked.
