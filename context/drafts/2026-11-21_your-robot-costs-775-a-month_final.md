<!-- lead -->
Yarbo (a maker of modular yard robots) will finance its full four-module bundle at $774.92 a month for a year [1]. The landscaping crew it is sold to replace bills a one-acre lawn just $300 to $520 a month [2]. Put those two printed numbers side by side and the vendor's own financing terms put year one of owning above year one of hiring. This is not a critique of the machine. It is arithmetic from two price tags. Call the monthly payment $775 a month, and the gap is the whole story. A buyer weighing the swap deserves to see that number next to the crew's invoice before signing anything.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

This article compares two printed prices for the same one-acre yard: Yarbo's monthly robot payment and a landscaping crew's monthly invoice. It finds that owning costs more than hiring in year one, and payback waits for year two.

<!-- tension -->
## Why year one loses on paper

The two documents were never meant to meet. Yarbo's storefront, fetched November 21, lists the top bundle at a regular price of $9,299 [1]. That bundle pairs the Y40 Core (the robot's wheeled base unit) with its snow blower, mower and blower modules, and the page finances it at 0% APR (annual percentage rate) — $774.92 a month for a year [1]. MowBook's lawn-care pricing guide, refreshed on November 3, prices a one-acre lawn at $75 to $130 per weekly visit [2]. Four of those visits sum to $300 to $520 on the guide's own monthly billing line [2]. I read both pages twice before accepting the size of the gap: the robot's monthly payment alone exceeds the crew's entire monthly bill.

One fairness note before the count hardens. MowBook's monthly figure is mowing-season billing — four weekly visits [2]. The robot's payment runs flat across all twelve months instead. Even granting that mismatch, the payment still tops the crew's whole bill. Yarbo's site is also mid-Black-Friday sale right now, and some product pages print lower sale prices against the same regular prices [1]. This article uses the regular ladder throughout. The sale prices narrow the gap; they do not close it.

## By the numbers

- **$774.92 a month:** the printed 12-month payment on Yarbo's full bundle at 0% APR [1].
- **$520 monthly:** the top of MowBook's range for a one-acre lawn on monthly billing [2].
- **$7,299 entry bundle:** Yarbo's cheapest rung — core, snow blower and blower, no mower [1].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## What I'd watch

The payback story depends on what the machine replaces, and the owner record is mixed on that point. An owner review posted in November 2026, drawn from Yarbo users, reports owners clearing 80 to 90 percent of snow storms [3]. The same review records that the robot does not clear the street-plow berm left at the end of the driveway [3]. My reading, held loosely: the snow module partly replaces a plow service, not fully, and a buyer still pays for the heavy pushes.

The bundle ladder matters just as much. Yarbo prices the entry bundle at $7,299 and the mower-added tier at $8,199, before the $9,299 top rung [1]. Each step up the ladder adds a module and raises the payment a buyer locks into. The mower tier sits between the rungs, which makes it look like the modest upgrade; the installment math says otherwise.

Timing is the open question. The Core itself ships "Late Nov – Early Dec 2026," while other items leave in days [1]. A robot financed now may mow nothing until the core lands, and whether the sale prices hold through that shipping window is what I am watching next.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch

I keep the test blunt: would I still write the check in month fourteen, when the financing ends and the crew's contract resumes? The purchase only pays if two conditions hold at once. The machine has to survive into year two and beyond, and it has to replace both the mowing contract and the plow visits. Miss either one and the monthly math never closes, because a partial replacement stretches the payback that the 0% APR framing hides. The honest question is not whether the robot works. It is whether one machine can absorb two separate bills.
