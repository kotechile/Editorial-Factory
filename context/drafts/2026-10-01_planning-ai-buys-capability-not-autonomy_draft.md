<!-- lead -->
By 2030, only 5% of companies that have automated their supply chain planning will let software make even 10% of planning decisions on its own — even though 83% of them have already spent at least $3 million on it [1].

<!-- tension -->
**Why it matters:** The money is already on the table, but the payoff is stuck. Gartner's forecast, published September 24, says the industry is pouring cash into planning automation while autonomy barely moves. I've been watching planning vendors promise "hands-free" Sales and Operations Planning (S&OP) for years, and this is the first hard number that says the promise is mostly marketing.

**The big picture:** The gap isn't the technology. Gartner's analyst, Buse Aras, is blunt about it: "Spending millions on supply chain planning automation can expand technical capabilities, but investment alone does not create AI readiness." What holds autonomy back is the unglamorous stuff — data quality, decision ownership, workforce skills, and technology architecture. A model can't own a decision it isn't trusted to make.

**By the numbers:**
- **5%:** the share of organizations that will automate at least 10% of planning decisions by 2030 [1]
- **83%:** have spent at least $3 million on planning automation, including AI; 51% spent between $3 million and $10 million [1]
- **243:** senior leaders surveyed, from organizations with at least $500 million in annual revenue [1]

<!-- tactical-insight -->
**What I'd watch:** The people closest to this are already behaving exactly as Gartner predicts. At the Barclays consumer-staples conference in late September, General Mills said it is using AI for demand forecasting, logistics planning, and manufacturing optimization — while chasing $1 billion in savings by 2030 through human-led process changes [2]. Nestlé is manually cutting underperforming SKUs and consolidating distributors in China [2]. The AI assists; a human still makes the call.

Gartner's own guidance to planning leaders is to stop measuring the technology and start measuring the decision:
- **Classify each decision** as strategic, tactical, or operational — and decide whether AI should support, augment, or automate it [1]
- **Build the foundations first** — data quality, governance, and the talent to run it — before scaling AI-enabled planning [1]
- **Measure decision improvement, not deployment** — whether planners actually use the new tools and stop reverting to spreadsheets [1]

The split that matters: routine decisions like replenishment and order prioritization are the ones with room to automate. Strategic calls — where to place a facility, how much inventory to hold — stay human, because the consequences are bigger than any model's confidence interval.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
**The catch:** "Autonomous" isn't the same as "valuable," and I could be reading this too literally. Gartner's bar is narrow — 10% of decisions made without human involvement. A company that automates 9% of decisions and uses AI to make the other 91% of human decisions sharper is getting enormous value and still lands outside the 5%. The 95% isn't a failure cohort; it includes everyone whose planners are simply better because AI drafts the forecast and a person owns the number.

Two other grains of salt. The survey underneath this was fielded from November 11 to December 18, 2025 — the 83% spend figure is nearly a year old, even though the 2030 forecast is fresh. And "by 2030" is a prediction, not a filing: agentic tooling could compress that timeline faster than Gartner's read suggests. The durable claim is the one I'd bet on regardless of the year — spending buys capability, but it does not buy the decision.

<!-- tldr -->
- **The Big Shift:** Gartner says that by 2030, only 5% of companies running supply chain planning automation will let AI make even 10% of planning decisions on its own — despite heavy spending.
- **Why It Matters:** 83% of firms have already spent at least $3 million on planning automation, yet autonomy stays stuck because data, talent, and trust lag the technology. The money is flowing into capability, not into handing decisions to software.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **Routine decisions:** replenishment and order prioritization, which Gartner sees as the first real candidates for automation.
  - **Strategic decisions:** facility placement and inventory policy, which keep human judgment because the downside is too large.
  - **Planner role shift:** from "data administrator" to "plan orchestrator" — the job doesn't vanish, it moves up the stack.
- **The Catch:** "Autonomous" is not "valuable." Automating 9% of decisions while sharpening the other 91% with AI is still a win — the 95% isn't a failure cohort. And the survey under the forecast is from late 2025, so the 2030 number is a projection, not a guarantee.
