<!-- lead -->
A new refrigerator comes with a federal label promising about $150 in savings over twelve years, which is $12.50 a year [1][2]. At the 2025 residential average of 17.30 cents per kWh, that is 72 kilowatt-hours [3]. So your fridge upgrade pays $12.50 a year, and that sum settles nothing about whether the old machine is worth keeping.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

My read starts with the label's own sums, because three different comparisons sit on one government page and only one of them answers a repair-or-replace question.

The first compares the badge with the law. Certified fridges run about 9 percent leaner than models that meet the federal minimum, and the page prices twelve years of that gap at roughly $150 [1][2].

The second compares an old machine with a certified one, where the page says an older unit draws about 20 percent more energy [1]. The third is a running cost and not a saving at all, since fridges past fifteen years can cost about $95 a year to run [1].

Only the last two describe the box in your kitchen. Most buyers end up reading the first.

The floor sits in the code, which is why the gap stays narrow. Federal rules cap a top-mount unit with automatic defrost at 8.07 times its adjusted volume plus 233.7 kWh a year [5].

For a 24.5 cubic-foot box that cap works out to 431 kWh, or $74.63 at the residential average [3][5]. Every certified fridge has to beat a legal limit that is already tight, so the badge cannot be worth much more than a tenth of the running cost.

That is where the labour enters. The median wage for a home appliance repairer was $24.51 an hour in 2025, on an annual median of $50,990 [4].

A year of an old unit's power at $95 is 3.9 hours of that work, and the whole twelve-year saving is 6.1 hours [2][4]. The repair quote and the efficiency case sit on one scale, while the purchase price sits on another.

## By the numbers

- **$12.50 a year — Badge value:** Twelve years of the certified gain is about $150, so the yearly benefit is $12.50 [1][2].
- **72 kWh — What it buys:** At 17.30 cents per kWh, the 2025 residential average, $12.50 covers 72 kilowatt-hours [3].
- **$24.51 an hour — Repairer's median wage:** The 2025 median for home appliance repairers, against a $50,990 annual median [4].
- **3.9 hours — One old fridge's power year:** A $95 running year works out to 3.9 hours at that median wage [1][4].

## What I'd watch:

I watch three things, because each one moves the answer more than the badge does.

- **The 2029 and 2030 deadlines:** The standards in force today apply to units made on or after September 15, 2014, and the next step arrives at the 2029 and 2030 deadlines depending on product class [5]. A fridge bought this year sits at the older cap, and its replacement will face a tighter one.
- **The haul-away:** Many appliance retailers pick up and recycle the old unit when they deliver a new one [1]. Disposal often rides inside the deal, which makes a replacement look cheaper at the till than on the tag.
- **The rebate channel:** The program's own page points owners at partner-sponsored rebates on certified products [1]. A rebate beats the badge by design, since one $150 payment equals the whole twelve-year saving [1][2].

## The catch

The part I keep circling is the $95, because I cannot fix it from outside. Read as a total running cost, it fits everything else: $95 of power is 549 kWh, a unit using 20 percent less would need 457, and the gap of about 92 kWh is $15.83 a year [1][3].

Read as a premium over a new machine, it would need a fridge drawing 2,745 kWh, which no household unit does. I use the total-cost reading, and the piece rests on the 20 percent gap rather than on the $95.

The rate is the second soft spot, since the same 72 kWh are worth four times more in one state than another. The 2025 averages ran from 35.72 cents per kWh in Hawaii to 8.20 cents in North Dakota [3], so the saving is $25.81 a year in the first and $5.93 in the second. A cheap rate turns even twelve years into small change against any new machine.

Two smaller caveats. The $24.51 is a wage and not a bill, because a service visit carries a truck, insurance and overhead on top, so $95 buys fewer than 3.9 hours of a repairer's day [4]. And the page's own setting advice asks owners to hold a fridge at an ambient temperature of 35 to 38 degrees Fahrenheit, which is the inside setting it means [1]; treat the label as a short summary of a long test rather than a manual.

What none of this supplies is a repair price, because no primary source publishes one. That number is yours, and it decides the question.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The case for replacing a working fridge now runs on a published saving of about $12.50 a year, while the labour that keeps the old one alive costs $24.51 an hour at the median.
- **Why It Matters:** A buyer who replaces a working machine for the badge gets a discount of roughly $150 over twelve years, and pays a purchase price for it that the label never mentions.
- **What I'd Watch:** Three levers move this sum more than the badge does.
  - **The deadlines:** The next federal step lands at the 2029 and 2030 deadlines by product class, so this year's cap is not next decade's.
  - **The haul-away:** Retailers that collect the old unit fold disposal into the deal, which changes what replacing really costs.
  - **The rebate channel:** Partner-sponsored rebates can match the whole twelve-year saving in one payment.
- **The Catch:** The $95 line never says what it measures, the state rate can move the saving by a factor of four, and the repair quote that decides the question appears in no published source.
