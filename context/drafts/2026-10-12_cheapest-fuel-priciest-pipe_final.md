<!-- lead -->

Natural gas is the cheapest way to heat a U.S. home this winter, about $640 for the season. Heating oil will cost the average family $2,115. Right as those forecasts dropped, California passed a law that pays owners to walk away from their gas lines. The fuel is cheap. The hookup is not: replacing one home gas pipe costs $20,000 to $30,000. [1] [2]

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The winter bill now turns on fuel type rather than usage, and the pipe under the lawn decides what an owner pays.

The Energy Information Administration (EIA) expects homes that heat with gas or propane, about half of all households, to spend less than last winter. Electricity sits in the middle at $1,196, up 4%. Heating oil hits hardest at $2,115, a 21% jump, on a price of $5.26 a gallon that runs 34% higher. [3] [4]

Heating oil is a small club: about 3% of homes, mostly in the Northeast. It is also the group with the least room to move. East Coast stockpiles sat 32% below their five-year average in September and are expected to stay 20% to 30% below all winter. [1] [3]

What strikes me here: a family cannot change its fuel mid-season. The bill is set by gear someone put in years ago.

California just made that buried pipe a clear choice. The Home Energy Choice Act tells utilities to offer a deal when an old gas line needs swapping out. The owner can take the new pipe, or take cash to switch to electric heat instead. The payout is set below the cost of the line, so the money left over goes back to the other gas customers. [10] [2]

The Natural Resources Defense Council (NRDC) ran the math on this swap. Giving 1,000 homes $17,500 each to go electric would save the state's gas users $20 million. A large California utility replaces about 15,000 lines a year. One line typically costs $20,000 to $30,000, billed to every gas customer over the life of the pipe. Nationally, utilities replace about 700,000 lines a year and spend over $7 billion doing it. [2] [5]

## By the numbers

- **$640 vs. $2,115 — Winter fuel spread:** the seasonal heat cost for the average gas-heated home next to an oil-heated home [3].
- **$5.26 a gallon — Oil squeeze:** heating oil runs 34% above last winter while East Coast reserves sit 32% below average [1] [3].
- **$20,000 to $30,000 — Pipe swap:** the normal cost to replace one gas line, billed to all gas customers, with about 15,000 replaced each year [2].
- **$17,500 — Buyout math:** the payout per home in NRDC's example, which still saves ratepayers money by skipping the new pipe [2].

## What I'd watch:

I've been watching three things that decide whether this becomes a real choice for homes or stays buried in utility paperwork.

- **The missing rules:** The California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC) still must write the payout levels and the notice rules. Utility programs need approval by January 1, 2028. The law caps programs at 1% of a utility's customers and ends in 2035. One research group has suggested telling owners one to five years ahead. [2] [5]
- **The scaling test:** Con Edison has run a similar swap in New York since 2024. Its filing put the avoided cost near $28,000 for each home that goes electric instead of taking a new pipe. That avoided cost is what makes the model work, more than the size of the payout. [5]
- **The home cost stack:** The federal tax credit for efficient home appliances, up to $2,000, closed to 2026 installs. Heat-pump shipments still held up through the first half of the year, and 2026 is tracking near the sector's best. Equipment prices are moving the other way, with October increases of 3% to 6% and one supplier at 15%. Borrowing $20,000 is not cheap either: home equity loans averaged 8.46% on October 1, the prime rate is 7.00%, and the next rate decision lands October 27–28. [6] [7] [8] [9]

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The California law is narrow, and the $17,500 figure is an example, not a promised check.

Most homes will never see this offer. The program only runs where a pipe is already due for a swap, covers at most 1% of a utility's customers, and has no written rules yet. San Diego Gas & Electric and Southern California Gas argued that as customers leave, the ones left behind carry more of the system. The law answers that by holding the payout below the avoided replacement cost, which shares the saving rather than removing it. That works while pipes are being replaced. It says less about the bills still standing in 2040. [2]

The EIA also warns that comparing fuels is a rough guide. House size, insulation and old gear differ enough that a cheaper fuel does not promise a lower bill. This is a one-winter forecast, not a lifetime price. [3]

The part I keep circling is the default choice. Until this law, the utility swapped the pipe and the customer paid over time. Now a family can be paid to walk away, but only if it finds out in time. [2]

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The winter heat bill now turns on which fuel a home already uses, and California became the first state to pay owners to walk away from the gas lines that deliver the cheapest heat.
- **Why It Matters:** The physical hookup is the pricey part. A gas line costs $20,000 to $30,000 to swap out and gets billed to every gas user, which turns a fuel choice into a capital decision.
- **What I'd Watch:** how the state rules get written, and whether the buyout math moves to other utility markets.
  - **Ratepayer alternative:** a program that pays a home to go electric instead of rebuilding its gas line, since the cash is cheaper than the pipe.
  - **Avoided cost:** what a utility saves when a user leaves, which makes the payout cheaper than laying new pipe.
  - **Notice window:** how far ahead a family learns its line is up for a swap, the line between a real choice and a sudden deadline.
- **The Catch:** the program only triggers where a pipe is already due for a swap, covers at most 1% of a utility's users, lacks written rules, and ends in 2035; the $17,500 is an example, not an offer.
