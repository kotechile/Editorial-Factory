<!-- lead -->
Tractor Supply spent more than $200 million of its own money on one building in Nampa, Idaho, and the site covers 865,000 square feet [1][2]. Its 125 shuttle units form the first storage grid of its kind across its eleven warehouses [1][2]. Sixteen days later, a Brooklyn startup raised $62 million to keep owning the packing robots, so its buyers never buy one — and its CEO said strong demand let him raise prices [3].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Both stories answer the same question: how do you move more orders through the same four walls? They price the answer on opposite sides of the books.

Tractor Supply bought the whole thing. The land, the building, the eighty truck docks and the shuttle grid all sit on its own books [1][2]. It also pays a resident team to put artificial intelligence to work on daily operations [1].

That total is a fixed number. What it has to earn back is a forecast the retailer wrote itself: 123 stores in the first year, with room to reach more than 200 [1]. About 81% of what its stores receive already moves through that network, so these buildings are the supply chain, not support [8].

Ultra's buyers pay none of it. They pay a setup fee up front, then a monthly charge for the hardware and the software [3]. The packing still gets done.

The robot's cost lands on the operating budget instead of the capital budget.

What strikes me is that renting moves the machine, not the building. Ultra's robots sit at packing stations inside sites the renter already leases [3]. The floor space stays tied up.

So the renter swaps one risk for another rather than shedding both.

The part I keep circling is the price. That monthly charge is not a number a buyer can look up.

Ultra's CEO, Jon Miller Schwartz, told Fortune that demand has let the company raise its prices [3]. The software that makes the robot worth renting comes from Physical Intelligence, a firm worth $5.6 billion that the renter never signs with [3][4].

The owned path carries its own risk. Nampa is one of eleven warehouses in the network and the only one with this kind of grid [2]. The retailer has to prove that payback ten more times before it becomes a network number.

Walmart made the same owned bet in the same week. It opened its fifth next-gen center, in Stockton, California: more than 900,000 square feet, with a system that cuts a twelve-step order flow to five [5]. Nobody in that release is renting anything.

The demand data points elsewhere again. Harris Williams, a bank, put out a report on 9 October saying the market should double from about $23.5 billion in 2025 to $47 billion by 2030 [6]. It also said operators now want more out of the buildings they already have.

A packaging study reported the day before said the money is moving to the last manual steps of the line, and that cobots are winning there because they cost less up front [7].

## By the numbers

- **$200 million-plus — The Nampa build:** The retailer's own figure for the 865,000-square-foot Idaho site, which opens serving 123 stores across nine states and is built to reach more than 200 [1].
- **125 — Shuttle units it now owns:** The battery units inside the company's first storage grid, at one of eleven warehouses and the only one with it [1][2].
- **$62 million — Ultra's two funding rounds:** A $50 million Series A led by Framework Ventures and an earlier $12 million seed round, backing a model that rents packing robots by the month [3][4].
- **Twelve steps to five — Walmart's version of the bet:** The order-flow squeeze Walmart reports at its fifth next-gen center, more than 900,000 square feet in Stockton [5].

## What I'd watch:

Four details decide who ends up holding the risk, and none of them is the headline price.

- **The renewal clause:** A monthly term with a price-change right is a different contract from a monthly term at a fixed rate. Ultra has said it raised prices, so the renter's cover sits in whatever the deal says about changes [3].
- **The support boundary:** When a robot drops an order, the renter needs to know whether to call the hardware maker or the firm that trained the model. Ultra builds the machines and Physical Intelligence supplies the brains, which leaves two vendors sharing one promise of uptime [3][4].
- **The site commitment:** The robot takes up the renter's own floor. That is the part of the cost that renting does not move, and it is why the retrofit story matters more than the new-build one here [3][6].
- **Whether the exception repeats:** Nampa is the only Tractor Supply warehouse with this grid. I'd want to see the next one get the same kit before treating the payback model as proven [2].

I'm watching two things: whether a second Tractor Supply site follows Nampa, and whether Ultra's next price is higher again.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

No payback figure exists for either case, and I could not find one hiding in the filings.

The more than $200 million covers land, an 865,000-square-foot building, eighty truck docks and the system inside it [1]. The company has not broken out how much of that total is automation, so any per-shuttle or per-square-foot figure would be invented.

Ultra has not put out a rate at all. Its CEO said the business has booked significant revenue without giving an amount. So the monthly charge that anchors the call is unknown to anyone outside the contracts [3].

The market figures are forecasts, and they disagree. Harris Williams puts the 2025 market at about $23.5 billion; other research houses use a larger base. The doubling claim is useful for direction, not for size [6].

My read is that this choice does not have a settled answer yet. It has a settled question, which is who can change the price after the contract is signed.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** In two weeks, Tractor Supply put more than $200 million into its first storage grid, across eleven warehouses, while Ultra raised $62 million to keep renting packing robots by the month instead of selling them [1][3].
- **Why It Matters:** Buying fixes the number and leaves the buyer holding a forecast it wrote. Renting moves the machine off the books while leaving the floor space, the monthly price and the software roadmap in other hands [1][3][4].
- **What I'd Watch:** Which contract terms carry the risk once the newness wears off.
  - **The renewal clause:** Whether the monthly rate is fixed for the term or open to change [3].
  - **The support boundary:** Which vendor owns a promise of uptime when the hardware and the brains come from different firms [3][4].
  - **The second site:** Whether Tractor Supply repeats the grid at another warehouse before treating the model as proven [2].
- **The Catch:** Neither side has put out a payback figure, and Tractor Supply has not split the automation spend from the building cost [1][3].
