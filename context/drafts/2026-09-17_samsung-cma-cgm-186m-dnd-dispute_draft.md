<!-- lead -->
Samsung Electronics America is suing ocean carrier CMA CGM for at least $186 million, claiming it was forced to pay unlawful detention and demurrage charges on delays it never controlled [1][2]. The complaint — FMC Docket No. 26-12, served September 1 — alleges Samsung paid more than 121,000 of these charges across "store-door" shipments where CMA CGM was responsible for inland delivery [3].

<!-- tension -->
**Why it matters:** This is the largest test yet of whether a shipper can claw back years of detention and demurrage fees under the Ocean Shipping Reform Act of 2022 (OSRA-22). That law, plus the Federal Maritime Commission's 2024 billing rule, gave shippers a hard standard: a carrier invoice must arrive within 30 days, name the right party, and trace to a delay the shipper actually caused [5].

**The big picture:** Most importers treat detention and demurrage as a cost of doing business. Samsung is proving the fees are recoverable when the delay was the carrier's problem — a chassis shortage, port congestion, or a failed inland move — rather than the shipper's.

**By the numbers:**
- **$186M sought:** Samsung's total claim against CMA CGM at the FMC [1][2].
- **$148M:** The share in allegedly improper demurrage, detention, and rail-storage charges alone [2].
- **121,000+:** How many demurrage, detention, and related charges Samsung says it paid [3].
- **$162,799:** The rail-storage charge on a single container from Busan to Texas after its store-door move was cut off at a rail ramp [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
**The playbook:** Turn your own detention and demurrage invoices into a dispute pipeline, not a write-off.

- **Audit every invoice against the FMC standard.** Check the 30-day issuance window and that the bill names the party who actually contracted for the move. An invoice that misses either is defective [5].
- **Track which delays were yours.** Demurrage is only lawful when the delay traces to you. If a chassis shortage or port congestion held your cargo, document it — that's the evidence that wins a dispute [4].
- **Automate the exception handling.** Detention invoices are a bounded, transactional workflow. A dispute agent that flags invalid charges and files claims can cut penalty fees without a headcount [6].
- **Don't wait for a nine-figure case.** The money is in the long tail of small, recurring charges — most shippers never dispute them because they're individually small.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
**The catch:** This is a slow, evidence-heavy fight. The FMC's initial decision isn't due until September 1, 2027, and a final decision until March 15, 2028 [6]. Winning requires clean records and in-transit visibility, not just a valid complaint. And a 2025 court ruling set aside one part of the FMC's billing rule, so some of the standard still has gray areas. The edge goes to shippers who can prove — in writing — that the delay wasn't theirs.

<!-- tldr -->
- **The Reality Check:** Detention and demurrage fees are usually paid without question, but under OSRA-22 a carrier can only bill you for delays you actually caused — and one major shipper is now demanding $186 million back on that basis.
- **The Winning Moves:** Build a dispute pipeline around the FMC's billing standard.
  - **Audit invoices:** Check that each bill arrived within 30 days and names the correct party.
  - **Log whose delay it was:** Record chassis shortages, port congestion, and failed inland moves as the carrier's, not yours.
  - **Automate disputes:** Use an exception-handling agent to flag and file invalid detention invoices at scale.
- **The Fine Print:** The fight is years long and evidence-heavy — the first ruling isn't due until 2027, and one part of the FMC rule was set aside in court, so clean documentation and real-time visibility are the prerequisite, not a nice-to-have.
