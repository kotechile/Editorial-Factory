Your equity payday at an initial public offering (IPO) is mostly a tax bill. One filing this month put a 47.7% assumption on it.

Oura, the smart-ring maker, told US regulators that of the $532.6 million its listing would raise for the company, about $526.4 million was set aside for the tax owed on its workers' restricted stock units (RSUs) [1]. That is 98.8% of the primary proceeds. Four days later, Oura called the listing off [2].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Oura's RSUs carry two conditions. The time condition is already met for most of them. The second is the listing itself, so the shares settle only if the company goes public [1].

I keep coming back to one number. The company said 25,488,554 shares would settle with the listing, that it would hold back 12,150,103 of them for tax, and that it would hand 13,338,451 to the people who earned them [1]. It also booked about $160.0 million of stock pay on the settlement [1].

So a worker's first taste of liquidity and their largest tax bill arrive together. There is no version where they get the shares without the withholding. There is no version where they sell into it either, because the shares land behind a lock-up [1].

Most of the deal was other people's stock. Of the 50,000,000 shares on offer, 36,500,000 belonged to existing holders, who keep that cash, and 13,500,000 were the company's own [1]. That smaller slice raised $532.6 million net, and $526.4 million of it was the workers' tax [1].

One clause in the plan is worth reading twice. On a firing without cause, 25% of the RSUs that had already met the time condition were erased for no money.

The other 75% waited on a performance condition. That rule applied right up to the listing, and the filing says it stops applying to outstanding awards once the shares are sold [1].

Then the event itself became optional. Oura had finished its roadshow.

A source told Fortune the order book was about five times oversubscribed, and the company still pulled the deal hours before pricing on September 29 [3][4]. Holtec Nuclear had shelved its own listing the day before it was to price [8]. Bamboo Insurance and Amaero followed, and Firmus scrapped what would have been Australia's second-largest listing on October 9 [3].

The queue behind them is not obviously readier. Morgan Stanley at Work asked more than 150 private-company executives and decision makers about liquidity.

In that survey, 88% feel pressure to run an event, 47% expect their next one to be a tender offer, and only 26% feel sure of their own readiness [6]. The same report has companies guessing that 37% of their staff do not grasp the tax side of a liquidity decision, and that 41% cannot tie the event to a financial plan [6].

Anthropic is next in line. The prospectus seen by Reuters shows revenue that grew 12-fold in 2025 to nearly $4.6 billion, against a net loss of $42 billion. The sale could value the company at more than $2 trillion, and it now looks pushed past the November US midterm elections [7].

## By the numbers

- **$526.4 million — the workers' share of the raise:** the slice Oura set aside from an estimated $532.6 million of net primary proceeds for tax withholding and remittance on its RSU settlement, with the rest left for general company use [1].
- **12,150,103 shares — held back, not handed over:** against 13,338,451 delivered, from 25,488,554 RSUs settling with the listing, at an assumed blended rate of 47.7% [1].
- **36,500,000 of 50,000,000 — the split:** shares sold by existing holders, whose cash the company never sees, against a company raise of 13,500,000 shares [1].
- **26% — sure of their own readiness:** private companies that feel confident about running a liquidity event, against 88% who feel the pressure to hold one [6].

## What I'd watch:

The part I keep circling is that the tax rate is a guess, and the filing says so. Oura calls 47.7% an estimated blended rate for every RSU settling at the listing, then warns the figure and the shares held back may differ from real results as the price and the forfeitures move [1]. In the United States, employers hold back a flat 22% of supplemental wages, the bucket an RSU settlement falls in, up to $1 million in a year, and 37% above that [10].

- **The settlement mechanic, not the headline valuation.** A company can net shares, sell to cover, or let a worker keep the whole grant. Oura chose to withhold shares, and it sized that withholding in the filing rather than at the vest [1].
- **The split between primary and secondary shares.** Who is selling says more than the price range. About three of every four shares Oura offered were other people's stock [1].
- **Forfeiture terms that move at the event.** The clause that erased 25% of time-vested RSUs on a without-cause exit stopped applying to outstanding awards only once the shares sold. The same plan can treat a leaver better after a listing than before it [1].
- **Whether the event happens on the schedule a worker planned against.** Oura's book was heavily covered and the deal still died in an evening [3][4]. Four other listings went the same way in the same month [3][8]. Anthropic's far larger sale has already slipped past the midterms [7].
- **The tranche calendar at the top of the market.** SpaceX's next unlock covers up to 328.4 million shares on October 24, with up to 1.3 billion eligible after third-quarter results and about 40% of the float tradeable by early December [9].

## The catch

My read is that the tax rate is not the scandal here. Withholding is a prepayment. The shares held back are shares a worker never had to fund from their own cash. A 47.7% haircut on paper is still a large gain on a strike price of nothing.

The part that bites is timing. The bill lands on a vest that cannot be sold, sized by a guess nobody can check until the price is set. The whole thing then hangs on an event the company can cancel at the last hour [1][3].

Two things could change this read. A second Oura filing at a different price would re-cut the withholding math. A public Anthropic prospectus would show whether its RSUs settle another way.

I could be wrong that withholding is the right comparison at all. A worker weighing an offer is really trading a liquid dollar for a locked one, and a lock-up that runs past a tax due date is a cash-flow problem, not a rate problem.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Oura's filing showed that 98.8% of the $532.6 million its listing would raise for the company, about $526.4 million, was set aside for tax owed on workers' RSUs at an assumed blended rate of 47.7%. Four days later the listing was postponed [1][2][4].
- **Why It Matters:** At a liquidity event a worker's first payday and their largest tax bill are one transaction, and the amount kept is set by a settlement mechanic the company picks, not by the valuation in the headline. The event is also optional: Oura, Holtec, Bamboo, Amaero and Firmus all pulled listings inside one month [1][3][8].
- **What I'd Watch:** What the next filings say about the settlement itself, and whether workers get a mechanic they can plan around.
  - **The withholding assumption:** the rate a company picks for its RSU settlement, which Oura put at 47.7% and called an estimate that may not match real results [1].
  - **The primary-versus-secondary split:** how much of a listing is the company raising and how much is existing holders selling, since the company never sees the second kind of cash [1].
  - **The readiness gap:** only 26% of surveyed private companies feel confident about their liquidity readiness, while 88% feel pressure to run an event and companies estimate 37% of staff do not grasp the tax [6].
- **The Catch:** The 47.7% is the company's own estimate, the $526.4 million was planned and never paid because the listing did not happen, and withholding is a prepayment rather than a loss. The real bite is that the bill falls due on shares a worker cannot yet sell [1][3].
