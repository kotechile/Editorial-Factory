<!-- lead -->
Kroger is about to buy roughly a tenth of what it sells straight from the grower, and the 3% late fee that has kept its suppliers honest since the on-time bar rose to 93% has nothing to attach to on that share. Kroger's own supplier letter says it in one line: "many of these fees will not apply if Kroger manages your inbound lanes" [1]. The fee ends at the dock the moment Kroger takes the wheel. On 20 October its chief financial officer told investors the company had already opened sourcing hubs in South America and Asia on the way to "a fully-owned import distribution model" [2].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

My read is that the fine schedule works as a gauge rather than a punishment, and the clash is easy to see once you read it that way. Kroger lifted its Original Requested Arrival Date (ORAD) bar from 90% to 93% for 2025 and kept a 95% case fill floor [1]. A late order that the supplier caused costs 3% of the invoiced amount, a short fill costs 3% of the value of the missing items, and the way it is collected gives the game away: "Fees will be assessed via invoice deductions" [1]. You cannot deduct from a bill nobody sent.

That is why the program works. Each bar is enforced by taking money off a bill the supplier issued, on a lane the supplier booked, with a 180-day window to argue [1][3]. The scorecard is not a report Kroger writes about itself. It is a settling up between two firms, and when you remove one of them there is nothing left to settle. The desk has made a close cousin of this point before about [why a 98% line fill still ships 18% short](/your-98-fill-rate-18-dont-ship/), where the maths of a measure and the experience of a customer part ways.

Direct sourcing removes the other firm. Kroger buys under 1% of the items it carries straight from source today and aims to grow that share tenfold by 2029 [2]. Its grape deal in Peru has already locked in about 40% of imported grape supply from large growers "with support from service partners" [2]. The company frames the saving the way a buyer would: "Previously, we would have paid margin to a third party to sit between us and the grower" [2]. That margin bought more than a middleman's cut. It bought the party who booked the slot, carried the import, filled the case and paid the 3% when any of it went wrong.

Kroger's own policy sees the shift coming without naming it. On loads Kroger runs, the firm states that "Kroger is responsible for location data", and any order above 10,000 pounds or 750 cube must send location and temperature readings every 15 minutes [1]. When the store runs the lane, the store carries the duty to report on it as well.

## By the numbers

- **Under 1% to about 10% — Direct buying:** Kroger's share of items bought straight from source today, and the tenfold lift it targets by 2029 [2].
- **93% — On-time bar:** Kroger's ORAD line since January 2025, up from 90%, and it is met only when an order is gated in at a warehouse on its requested day [1].
- **95% — Case fill floor:** The share of an order that must be filled; a short fill is billed at 3% of the missing items' value [1].
- **$200 and $500 — Flat fees:** A load more than 30 minutes past its slot costs $200, and a label, pack or product-code error costs $500 per order [1].

## What I'd watch:

My read is that the telling number is not the 10%. It is the slice of a supplier's scorecard that quietly stops being worked out.

- **Where the scorecard shrinks first:** the grape deal is the template, with one crop, a short grower list and service partners doing the physical work. Lines with a short, known grower list move long before the tail does.
- **What replaces the fine:** a store that runs its own lane still needs a miss to show up. Watch whether Kroger builds a receipt-side gauge, gate-in times, case counts, slot loyalty, or simply stops printing a supplier number for those loads.
- **The service partners:** they pick up the slot, the temperature feed and the case fill, yet the fee table is written for suppliers, not for them. I would expect a separate annex for third-party logistics firms before I would expect a new scorecard for growers.
- **The data clause:** the 15-minute location and temperature rule is the nearest thing to a new gauge already in the policy, and it watches the load rather than the promise.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

The honest limit is scale. Nine tenths of what Kroger carries stays on a supplier lane through 2029, and there the ORAD program runs as written, same bar, same fee, same window. Nothing in the investor material says the scorecard is going away, and Kroger may simply apply it to its own hubs and its partners under another name.

The second caveat is that a store need not publish the gauge it uses at home. Kroger could keep a clean gate-in number for direct loads and never put it in a supplier portal. That would leave the most quoted service yardstick in the trade intact for suppliers and invisible on the share that moved, and a grower bidding against Kroger's own import desk would carry a burden the buyer no longer carries on its own volume.

The word I keep circling back to is "many". The letter says many fees do not apply on Kroger-run lanes, not all, and it is a policy reviewed each year [1]. A soft clause can tighten in the next pass, which is the kind of change a supplier tends to read six months late.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** Kroger is moving a tenth of its buying onto lanes it runs itself, where its own supplier fine schedule largely does not apply.
**The number:** 93% — the on-time bar Kroger enforces by taking 3% off a supplier's bill.
**The risk:** a fine needs a supplier bill to come off, so the direct share is the part of Kroger's service level nobody is scored on.
