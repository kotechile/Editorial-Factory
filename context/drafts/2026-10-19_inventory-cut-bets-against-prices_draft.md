<!-- lead -->
The next inventory cut your finance team asks for is a bet against prices that the memory industry expects to rise 15% in a single quarter [2]. North America's 1,000 largest public companies closed 2025 with a record $1.94 trillion working-capital gap, and the only line that improved was how slowly they paid their suppliers [1].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The survey behind that number reads like a year of quiet progress. The cash conversion cycle (CCC) fell 0.2 days to 38.4 days, which sounds like a win until you see where the gain came from [1].

Payables did all of the work. Companies paid suppliers 2.9 days slower, while receivables stretched 2.1 days and inventory rose 0.6 days [1].

The cycle did not get healthier. It borrowed from the people who ship to it.

The released detail is blunter still. Receivables are now the largest and fastest-growing bucket, at $773 billion, up 29% and 40% of the whole [1]. Total receivables grew 11%, nearly twice the pace of revenue [1].

Then the same month brought the other half of the story. TrendForce expects conventional DRAM (dynamic random-access memory, the working memory in computers and servers) contract prices to rise 10% to 15% in the fourth quarter, and NAND (the flash memory inside solid-state drives) to rise 15% to 20% [2].

Suppliers are steering advanced capacity to server parts. That keeps the wider market short of chips, and buyers know it: personal-computer makers "continue to procure aggressively" because they expect supply to stay tight through 2027 [2]. Supply Chain Dive, citing Omdia, puts the two-year move at up to 400% [5].

What I keep coming back to is the timing. The lever finance has left is the stock itself, and stock is the one input whose replacement price is climbing every quarter.

Holding a component that costs 10% more next quarter is not a cost centre. It is a position.

Remove a day of that stock and you have written a small short position, which is a bet that the price will be lower when you buy again. You collect a few basis points of financing relief, and you pay the price move to get it.

## By the numbers

- **$1.94 trillion — Record cash gap:** the working-capital opportunity across 1,000 large North American public companies, up 12% on the prior year's results [1].
- **2.9 days — The only improvement:** suppliers were paid 2.9 days slower, offsetting receivables 2.1 days worse and inventory 0.6 days worse [1].
- **$773 billion — Receivables:** up 29% year on year and 40% of the total opportunity, growing nearly twice as fast as revenue [1].
- **10% to 15% — Memory next quarter:** projected rise in DRAM contract prices for the fourth quarter, with NAND at 15% to 20% [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The first thing I would want answered is why the receivables number is not the one on the operating review. It is the biggest bucket, it grew fastest, and it sits in a process most supply-chain teams touch at the invoice stage [1].

- **The buying ahead of 2027:** TrendForce reports that computer makers are already buying past current needs because they expect shortages to last into next year [2]. If that holds, the firms holding stock are the ones with cover, not the ones with the cleanest days.
- **Where the buffer sits:** merchant wholesalers ended August with an inventory-to-sales ratio of 1.18, against 1.28 a year earlier, with sales up 15.6% and stock up 6.4% [4]. The upstream tier is already the lean one, so the next cut lands on stock duplicated downstream, at the spoke rather than the hub.
- **The space the stock needs:** industrial vacancy was 6.8% in the third quarter, the lowest since the end of 2024, with 76.6 million square feet absorbed and new completions 13% behind last year's pace [3]. Extra buffer is not free to store any more.

My read is that the multi-echelon question has quietly changed shape. It used to be about service levels and freight. For a component re-priced every quarter, it is about which node carries the longest price exposure.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that the argument flips the moment prices stop rising. TrendForce is forecasting, not reporting, and its 2027 tightness call rests on capacity staying where it is [2]. A quarter that brings new supply turns the same stock into a write-down.

There is a second limit. For short-life products, seasonal goods and anything with a real obsolescence clock, cutting inventory days is simply correct. The bet only pays on parts that hold their value and get dearer.

The measurement is softer than it looks. The survey's days come from financial statements, so they are dollar days, and part of that 0.6-day rise may be price rather than volume [1]. The Census ratio carries its own warning: it is not adjusted for price changes [4].

The record is also unkind. Of 1,000 companies, only 98 improved the cash conversion cycle for three straight years, nine for five, and two for seven [1]. Gains that rare should be read with care.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** North American companies hit a record $1.94 trillion working-capital gap, and the year's only gain came from paying suppliers slower while receivables and inventory both worsened [1]. In the same month, memory contract prices were projected to rise 10% to 15% in one quarter [2].
- **Why It Matters:** The lever finance has left is inventory, and inventory is now the rising asset. A days target forces a bet against a price curve that moves faster than any carrying charge.
- **What I'd Watch:** Where the cash actually sits, and which node holds the stock.
  - **Receivables:** Money owed by customers, now 40% of the opportunity and growing 29% a year, which is a process fix rather than a stock decision [1].
  - **Buying ahead:** Computer makers buying past current needs for a tight 2027, which tells you which firms expect to be short [2].
  - **Node choice:** Wholesalers at 1.18 against 1.28 a year ago, meaning the upstream tier is already lean and duplicated stock sits downstream [4].
- **The Catch:** A forecast is not a price. If supply loosens, the same buffer becomes a write-down, and for short-life products the inventory cut is still the right call.
