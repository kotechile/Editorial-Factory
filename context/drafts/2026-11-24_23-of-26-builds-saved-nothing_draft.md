<!-- lead -->
26 teams built their own software with AI tools in one two-week stretch, and 23 of them report they saved nothing at all [1]. That cohort is the first hard test of a bet many teams now make at renewal time, because AI coding made building look cheap. The switch to building is real. A build that demonstrably paid has not been shown once.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
A first-edition research panel tracked what teams paid for software, and what the teams that built their own said it cost them. For engineering and finance leaders, the AI build-vs-buy reset has arrived: building is mainstream, but savings reporting is not.

<!-- tension -->
## Where the build bet lands
The record is short. HissingKitty Research, run by people who spent 20 years inside enterprise software firms, published its first monthly report on October 19 and closed the October edition on October 31, covering October 17 through 31 [1]. Buyers shared 177 yearly bills across 68 tools, worth $6.86 million in total [1]. The panel listed 261 AI-native alternatives against 213 incumbent tools, and HubSpot and Salesforce drew 17 new alternatives each [1].

The verdicts show where the choice really lands. Across 64 software categories, the editors rated 15 "build it", 37 "build part of it" and 12 "keep paying" [1]. Most teams keep the core system and build the costly edge around it. The switch is mainstream: the report cites a survey of 1,719 people where 32% at AI-using firms skipped a purchase to build instead [1]. What I find hard to square is the ledger on the other side. 23 of 26 builds state no saving at all, and no verified payback report exists [1].

The buy side runs on the same rule. On AI spending, the biggest cut most teams get is a deal: promise volume, pay a lower price per token [2]. That is procurement, not architecture. The one builder's counter-case sits at the small end of the bill: a solo founder rebuilt his landing page, testimonials and docs chatbot with AI, and dropped about €300 a month for a few days of work [3]. That is a real saving. It is also the smallest kind of bill.

## By the numbers
- **23 builds:** The count that stated no saving at all, of the 26 AI-era builds logged in two weeks [1].
- **$6.86 million in bills:** What 177 buyers reported paying each year across 68 tools [1].
- **261 new alternatives:** The AI-built tools listed against 213 incumbent products in the same fortnight [1].

## What the closest teams do
What I keep coming back to is the report's own limits section, because it reads like a checklist for the decision itself [1]. The sample is self-chosen, the savings are the builders' own claims, and teams whose builds failed rarely post about them. So the teams closest to this price a build the way they price a vendor: engineer-years, upkeep and on-call, before the commit. That is the long-horizon standard for enterprise builds — [the sticker price is the smallest part of the bill](/enterprisebuildvsbuy-the-250k-ai-upkeep-tax/) — and this cohort has not met it [1]. The number to watch next month is the first verified replacement report. Zero exist so far. The first one that documents a measured payback becomes the first real counterexample anyone can cite.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch
The catch is that this cohort measures savings reporting, not savings. Twenty-six builds is a small, self-chosen sample from a first edition, and "no saving stated" is not the same as "no saving made" [1]. What the cohort proves is an absence of evidence. With zero verified payback reports in the first measured window, no one can point you to a build that demonstrably paid. So the rule is not "never build with AI". Price the build against the renewal before you commit. If you cannot state what the replaced bill was, you are not making a build-vs-buy decision. You are starting a hobby.
