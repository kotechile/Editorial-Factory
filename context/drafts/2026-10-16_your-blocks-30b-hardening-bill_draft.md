<!-- lead -->
California's own fire review landed Oct. 2, and it said the quiet part out loud. The state keeps writing better building codes and hazard maps, the authors found, "but these efforts are not consistently translating into reduced risk at the parcel, neighborhood, or landscape level." The part that lags is the house. Four days later, Berkeley researchers put a number on closing that hardening gap: $30 billion or more. The grant meant to pay for it is "likely modest in size relative to the total investment needed." So your block's hardening bill arrives one house at a time.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The 723-page report came from the Fire Safety Research Institute (FSRI). Gov. Gavin Newsom hired it after the January 2025 fires. This is not a report about brush clearing. Embers, flames and heat jumping from building to building played a "substantial role" in spreading the Palisades and Eaton fires [1][3].

Retrofits to homes lagged the brush work around them. The report's harder point is that one owner's retrofit could not save a house alone. Survival also hinged on "surrounding conditions, neighborhood-scale exposure pathways, and defensive actions" [3].

So the fix is real, and it is shared. The part I keep circling: the money is neither.

California's Safe Homes grant (AB 888) is law. It was signed in October 2025 and started Jan. 1. It covers exactly what the report flags: a fire-rated roof and the five feet nearest the walls.

It also has no open sign-up page. The $3 million set aside so far pays to set the program up, not to pay grants [4].

The federal tap is shut too. The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) closed its latest Building Resilient Infrastructure and Communities (BRIC) grant round on July 23. One billion dollars was on the table, and no winners have been named yet [6].

Sonoma County's fire office read the same signals. It stopped taking applications for federally funded wildfire risk reduction after Sept. 1, once the state told it that BRIC rollout money would not be awarded [5]. That award never covered work on private homes anyway [5].

The clock is running. On Oct. 14 the state began moving engines, aircraft and crews into four Southern California counties for the season's first Santa Ana wind event. Utilities warned that Public Safety Power Shutoffs were possible. Los Angeles County told residents to learn their evacuation zone [9][10].

## By the numbers

- **$30 billion or more — Hardening bill:** The California Earthquake Authority's guess at what it would take to harden every at-risk home in the state, cited by the Terner Center on Oct. 6 [2].
- **$3 million — Safe Homes setup:** What has been set aside for AB 888 so far. It pays to build the program, not to pay grants, and the sign-up page is not open [4].
- **40% — Where AB 888's money comes from:** a slice of the tax insurers pay on premiums, above a 2023 baseline. It is not a fixed budget line [2].
- **85% — Expected loss growth:** Climate change lifts the state's yearly wildfire losses on owner-occupied homes 85% by 2047 against recent years, about $5 billion a year [2].

## What I'd watch:

I keep circling where the money that does exist is going, because it is not going to the house.

- **The excluded line item:** Sonoma County's federal award pays for roadside brush work, shaded fuel breaks and prescribed grazing. Its own page lists work on private homes as ineligible [5]. The half of the risk the state's report calls lagging is the half the federal grant cannot buy.
- **The closed sign-up page:** The Safe Homes grant is the one program built for that gap. No page, no waitlist, no published grant size, and a $3 million budget a grant tracker calls setup money [4].
- **The open round:** FEMA is scoring BRIC bids now. Even if a county project wins, a county office has already judged that rollout money is unlikely to land this year [5][6].
- **The five-foot rule:** The Board of Forestry adopted the ember-zone rules on Aug. 19. The state's Office of Administrative Law (OAL) logs the package as pulled back on Sept. 8, and no new version sits in its queue [7][8]. With no filing, there is no state deadline and no clock [11].
- **The local tax route:** Where a district already taxes itself for fire work, money exists today. Marin's Measure C pays for countywide grants and inspections through a joint-powers agency [2].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that two things get blended together here, and the blend is costly.

One is a safety standard. The other is a price cut. Insurance Business reported in August that the ember rule and the carrier discount are separate tools. Insurers check against their own filings, so meeting a five-foot standard does not by itself cut a premium [13].

Research the Terner Center cites found California's discounts were "inconsistently applied and are insufficient in magnitude" [2].

Two caveats sit on top. The FSRI report is advice from an outside research group with no power to enforce it [3]. And $30 billion is a state total across every at-risk home, not a quote for your driveway.

Even the per-house figure is argued over. The state's own analysis says about $960. A field group says the average runs past $2,500 [12].

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A review ordered by Gov. Newsom after the January 2025 fires named home hardening as the part of wildfire risk that keeps lagging. Four days later, Berkeley researchers priced the fix at $30 billion or more.
- **Why It Matters:** The program built to pay for that work, the AB 888 Safe Homes grant, has a $3 million setup budget and no open sign-up page. The federal grant round that funded county risk reduction has closed with no winners named, and one county has already stopped taking applications. The cost lands on owners.
- **What I'd watch:** The signals that will show whether this stays a private purchase, and why each one matters.
  - **The Safe Homes page:** When sign-ups open, plus the per-home cap and the list of eligible work — this is the only state channel aimed at the house itself.
  - **FEMA's BRIC picks:** Whether the states and counties that applied get paid after the July 23 close, since that is the money for neighborhood-scale work rather than one roof.
  - **A Board of Forestry refiling:** A new OAL file number would restart the clock on the five-foot ember zone. Until then, no California homeowner has a state deadline.
- **The Catch:** The report is advice, the $30 billion is a state total rather than a per-house price, and a finished retrofit buys resilience, not a discount — insurers check against their own filings, and the state's discounts have been uneven.
