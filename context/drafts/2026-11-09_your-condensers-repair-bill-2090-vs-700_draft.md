<!-- lead -->
A failed compressor puts three prices on your condenser's repair bill: the part, the labour and the gas. The gas is the one that decides what the next decade costs. Most American systems built since the mid-2000s run on R-410A, which the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) lists at a global warming potential of 2,090 [2]. New residential air conditioners and heat pumps have been held to a limit of 700 since 2025 [1].

That gap is what changes the decision. A repair that refills an old coil puts money into a fluid whose national supply is being cut by statute. A replacement has to use a fluid that stays inside the limit. The number worth reading on a bid is the refrigerant, not the efficiency rating next to it.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** the refrigerant class now sets a system's service life, not the rating.
- **The number:** 2,090 — the warming potential of the gas in most older systems.
- **The risk:** the limit governs new equipment, not the gas sold to service the old.

<!-- tension -->

## The rule came before the price

Congress set the national phasedown in the American Innovation and Manufacturing (AIM) Act of 2020, and EPA's rule for it was published on October 24, 2023 [6]. The part that reaches a homeowner began on January 1, 2025, when the agency barred installing a residential or light-commercial air conditioner or heat pump with a fluid at a warming potential of 700 or above [7]. Variable refrigerant flow equipment sits on a later schedule, because that subsector needed its own dates [7]. EPA then let units built or imported before the 2025 date be installed through the start of 2026, an allowance written so that stock would not be stranded [6].

The cap works on supply. It limits how much of these gases may be made and used in a year, measured against the average volume from 2011 through 2013 [5]. The permitted share is 60 percent through 2028 [5]. It falls to 30 percent from 2029 and reaches 15 percent from 2036 [5]. A coil that leaks in the next decade will draw on a smaller allowance. The one it was built under is already gone. Nothing in that schedule asks how old the equipment is.

What I keep circling in this rule is the gap between the year a system was built and the year its gas got scarce. The effect lands unevenly, too. An owner who replaces a working system pays once and leaves the question behind, and no rule anywhere asks that owner to act sooner. An owner who repairs a failed compressor is buying gas for a box that still has years of service in it, and the value of that repair now rests on a supply schedule written in Washington.

## By the numbers

- **2,090 — the gas in the old box:** R-410A's warming potential, in the A1 safety group [2].
- **470 — the gas in the replacement:** R-454B's warming potential, in the mildly flammable (A2L) class [3].
- **30 percent — the later allowance:** the statute permits this share from 2029, against 60 percent in 2024 through 2028 [5].

## Where this bites in the quote

Three details in a bid tell you which side of the line a system sits on, and none of them is the efficiency rating. The first is the fluid named on the data plate. The two gases cannot be swapped for one another. The second is the build date, because a "new" R-410A system sold today can only be assembled from parts made or imported before 2025 [7]. EPA's allowance for that older stock ran out in January 2026, and that is the date I would ask a contractor to speak to [6]. The third is handling: the substitutes the agency lists for this subsector belong to the mildly flammable (A2L) class, and that class is why the tools and the training around them changed [3].

I watch the 2029 step, because it carries a date rather than a trend. The allowance halves at that point, and the cut lands well inside the service life of anything bought this year [5]. Variable refrigerant flow equipment has its own ladder, with the limit arriving in 2026 and pre-2026 parts installable through 2027 [7]. The agency did reopen parts of the rule last May, so the dates are worth checking rather than assuming [6].

A temperature split across the coil is the reading that separates a metering fault from a dead compressor, and it comes before any decision about what gas goes back in. ## The catch

What I find hard to square is how little of this restricts what a household may own. EPA lists R-410A as an acceptable substitute, so recharging an existing system remains legal, and the limit applies to making, importing and installing new equipment [1]. A reader who hears a ban on their own condenser has heard it wrong. The same table that carries the 2,090 figure also carries the word acceptable [2].

The other limit is that no public source prices the gas at the counter. The scarcity case here rests on the statute's own percentages, and a forecast of what the fluid will cost next winter would be the first number to go stale [5]. So the useful output is a question rather than a number, and it is the question of which fluid the equipment takes. EPA also revisited parts of the rule in May 2026 [6]. The codified text in the electronic Code of Federal Regulations, current as of November 5, 2026, is the version to read before signing anything [7].
