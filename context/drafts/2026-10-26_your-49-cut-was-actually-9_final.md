<!-- lead -->
Helen of Troy's reported inventory drop was actually 9% following a tariff refund that skewed the public math [1][4]. The company behind Hydro Flask and Osprey was celebrated this week for a 49% cut, but its own filed results show a $48.6 million drop in stock. The firm also booked a $26.9 million refund that was almost entirely spent before the quarter closed [1].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

What strikes me here is where the refund lands. Helen of Troy put the cash against its cost of goods sold, which pushed gross margin up to 52.2% from 44.2% on sales of $440.9 million [1].

Take the $26.9 million back out and gross margin falls to about 46.1% of sales. That is my sum on the company's figures, leaving a real gain of 1.9 points rather than eight [1].

The company is plain about where the money goes. Its outlook holds about $80.5 million of refunds across three claims phases, and it plans to put 83% to 88% of that back into brand spending, internal projects and inventory composition, for an estimated $10 million to $14 million of pre-tax income [1].

Of the $26.9 million booked in the quarter, just $0.8 million cut the value of stock on hand, and $4.3 million was still sitting as a receivable at the close [2]. The stock did not fall because the refund came in. The refund bought a budget to re-mix the stock, and the stock fell $48.6 million on its own account.

The counter-example sits in the same window. Fastenal grew sales 15.5% in the quarter and ended it with stock 3.1% above the year before, which is the ratio gain every planner says they want, and it had no refund credit to help [3].

Receivables rose 19.9%, payables rose 29.3%, and net trade working capital rose 8.1% [3]. Cash from operations came in at 105.0% of net income for the quarter, against 115.3% a year earlier [3]. Fastenal's own note is that growth with larger customers pulled cash into receivables [3].

One firm improved its stock figure with a credit it had already promised to spend, and the other improved its stock ratio but handed the cash to customers through longer terms. Neither got free cash off the shelf.

## By the numbers

- **6.1 points — Refund's margin share:** The $26.9 million credit against $440.9 million of sales, inside an 8.0-point rise in gross margin [1].
- **$0.8 million — Refund touching stock:** The part of the $26.9 million booked that cut the value of goods on hand [2].
- **3.1% — Fastenal's stock growth:** Twelve-month change in stock against 15.5% growth in sales [3].
- **94.8% — Cash from operations:** The nine-month share of net income turned into cash, down from 96.2% in the same stretch [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

I've been watching the spending log, because a plan to put 83% to 88% of a refund back into the business is not the same as having spent it. Helen of Troy spent about $23 million of the recovery inside the quarter, which is quick, and the rest of the claims arrive by the end of the fiscal year [1].

- **Where the last phase lands:** The company learned in August 2026 that it could file its third set of claims, and it has not been paid on them yet [2]. The $80.5 million is an estimate, so the share headed for the stock mix is not fixed.
- **The bin count, not the dollar count:** Fastenal ended the quarter with 143,878 weighted vending and scanner sites, up 7.4% [3]. A rhythm that refills 143,878 sites is a skill, while a year-end mix reset is an event.
- **The receivable line next filing:** Receivables at Fastenal grew three times faster than stock, driven by big accounts [3]. That is a price cut wearing a sales win's clothes.
- **Whether the refund goes back to margin:** A credit against cost of goods sold lifts the gross margin line, and the next phase of claims will do the same.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that the case rests on how long it lasts, and it cuts both ways. If the mix reset drops dead lines for good, the gain holds, because the write-off changes what the next plan can order [1].

The refund is also real money. Helen of Troy cut total debt to $672.6 million from $893.2 million and lifted six-month cash from operations to $56.5 million from $47.9 million [1]. Fastenal's numbers need their own caveat, as its stock balance is a twelve-month change while the 15.5% is a three-month sales change [3].

There is a harder limit under both. Tariff refunds come once, and the firms treating them as a stock budget will find the budget gone in a year. What the remaining stock costs to hold is the rate this decision really turns on, and it is better measured fully loaded than at the borrowing rate ([what a dollar of inventory costs to carry](/inventory-carrying-cost-rate/)).

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Helen of Troy's inventory drop was reported as 49% but is 9.2% in its own filing, in the same quarter that a $26.9 million tariff refund lifted gross margin to 52.2% [1][4].
- **Why It Matters:** Working capital is set across three lines, and lifting one of them with a one-time credit or soft payment terms does not put cash in the bank.
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether the refund changes the stock or just the margin, and whether anything changes how often a site refills.
  - **The last claims phase:** The final and largest slice of the refund, still unfiled, which sets how much budget the mix reset really has [2].
  - **Bin sites:** Weighted vending and scanner sites that refill on their own, at 143,878 and up 7.4%, which shows a true rhythm [3].
  - **Receivables:** The line where a sales win and a soft payment term look exactly the same until the cash arrives [3].
- **The Catch:** A refund is real cash, and a true mix reset holds if dead lines stay dropped. The risk hits in year two, when the cheque is gone and the refill rhythm is unchanged.
