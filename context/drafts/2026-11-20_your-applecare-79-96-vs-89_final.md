Apple's pooled AppleCare One plan covers up to three products for $19.99 a month [1]. Divide that by the devices and your AppleCare costs $79.96 per device per year, whether or not anything breaks [1]. The one repair you can look up in advance is battery wear, which lists at $69 to $99 for Apple's own swap and $29.99 to $59.99 as a do-it-yourself (DIY) kit [3]. Against the battery alone, the premium runs higher than the repair and well above the kit. The subscription only pays for a household that breaks things.

## At a glance

This article runs the arithmetic on AppleCare One, Apple's pooled repair subscription, for households with several Apple devices. Dividing the monthly fee by the devices covered, it weighs that premium against the one repair price published in advance: battery wear.

<!-- tension -->

## What the pooled plan prices

The plan is insurance, and its own page says what it insures against. Apple's one-year warranty excludes accidental damage and batteries worn down by normal use, so an uncovered repair is a bill the owner pays [2]. AppleCare One exists to absorb those two gaps, and it prices them as a pool: up to three products starting at $19.99 a month until cancelled, each extra product at $5.99, or the whole family's products at $49.99 [1].

Divide the pool and the per-device price appears. The $19.99 tier spread over three products is $79.96 per device-year, and every $5.99 add-on works out to $71.88 a year for the device it carries [1]. The Family tier, at $599.88 a year, beats the Individual pool only past about seven and a half devices, which makes it a big-household tier, not a household default [1]. I keep noticing that almost nobody runs that division, because the pool is sold as a bundle and read as a bargain.

Claims are capped too, and a savings account has no cap. AppleCare+ with Theft and Loss allows two claims a year, AppleCare One Individual three, and the Family tier six [1]. The theft-and-loss replacement itself carries a $149 service fee [1]. The contract is a prepaid repair budget with a ceiling, and the premium is due every month whether or not a claim arrives.

## By the numbers

- The pooled $19.99 tier divided by three products works out to $79.96 per device-year [1].
- Each $5.99 add-on product costs $71.88 a year — roughly one out-of-warranty battery swap [1].
- Theft-and-loss claims are capped at two a year on AppleCare+, three total on Individual, six on Family [1].

## Where the plan pays

Battery wear is the only failure whose price both sides publish. Apple's out-of-warranty battery swap runs $69 on the oldest models in iFixit's table and $99 on the newest [3]. The plan replaces a battery with no extra fee once it holds less than 80 percent of its original capacity [1]. A kit does the same job for $29.99 to $59.99, and iFixit puts the saving at $34 to $49 a repair [3]. At one swap every two years, a three-phone household faces about $133 a year at Apple's counter or $75 in kits. Both sit under the pooled premium, and that is the honest version of the math.

Accidents are the other leg, and there the plan's fees are flat. A damaged iPhone screen or back glass runs a $29 to $99 service fee under the plan, and other accidental damage such as liquid damage a flat $99 [1]. The uninsured prices for the same events are the numbers Apple does not publish; its own repair page shows no price table outside a browser. My reading, held loosely: that gap is the real product. The plan sells certainty about a number Apple refuses to state.

Before paying either premium, measure the device against its own repair bill. The desk's [repair-or-replace calculator](/calculators/tech-debt-repair/) takes a repair estimate and a replacement price and returns the ratio the decision turns on. That is the check to run before a cracked screen becomes a shopping trip.

## The catch

The battery ladder is a vendor's table of Apple's November 2024 estimates, not a live price list [3]. Attach that date to every figure drawn from it, because Apple's counter prices move without the table following. The swap rate in the break-even is also a guess — no source states how often a battery needs replacing. A household that keeps phones four years halves the battery leg again.

The deeper limit sits on the accident leg. Apple publishes the plan's fees but not the uninsured ladder, so the true break-even is your own quote against $239.88 a year. The only reader who can settle it is the one who already dropped the phone. What I am left with is that this is the opposite of most subscription creep: the plan is fairly priced as insurance, and the case for skipping it is that careful owners of fixable devices rarely collect.
