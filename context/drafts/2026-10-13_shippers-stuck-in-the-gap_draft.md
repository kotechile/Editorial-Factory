<!-- lead -->
Half of the world's container ships missed schedule in August. The global on-time rate fell 5.9 points in one month to 49.9%, the lowest since September 2022, and late arrivals stretched to 6.81 days on average [1]. The dock those ships were sailing toward slowed at the same time. At Los Angeles and Long Beach, a box waiting for a train sat 6.75 days, the longest in a year [2]. Shippers are now stuck between two clocks, and neither one belongs to the control tower they bought.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

I keep coming back to what those two numbers actually measure. An on-time rate counts vessels. Dwell counts boxes sitting on the ground. One says the ship is late, the other says the cargo is late again after it lands. Both cover August, both moved the wrong way, and neither publisher compares them.

The gap is arithmetic, not mystery. A port complex that just handled more than 2.9 million twenty-foot equivalent units (TEU) in June, July and August takes time to clear [2]. Trains must be built, and a terminal can only stack so much before it runs out of ground.

The Pacific Merchant Shipping Association (PMSA) publishes the dwell data. It said volumes through San Pedro Bay stayed strong while rail dwell took "some additional upward movement" [3].

What stands out is where the slowdown landed. Truck-bound boxes moved faster, easing to 2.95 days from 3.03 in July [3]. Rail went the other way, up to 6.75 days from 6.34 [2]. That is a 0.41-day jump in one month, on a measure that had sat near six days for a year.

So the gateway held. Drayage, gates and yard crews kept boxes moving. What stretched was the seam: the step where an import leaves the terminal and becomes a rail load bound for the Midwest or the South.

The load port explains the pressure. Inside Asia the on-time rate fell to 32.3%, with late arrivals at 7.28 days [4]. China, the world's biggest export machine, fell to 23.9%. Shanghai hit 12.2% and Ningbo 16.9% [4]. Delayed ships do not vanish from a timetable. They bunch up behind the ships that ran to plan, which is why Sea-Intelligence counted 12 double-sailings in two weeks [5].

## By the numbers

- **49.9% — Global on-time rate:** The share of vessels that hit schedule in August, down 5.9 points on the month and the lowest since September 2022 [1].
- **6.81 days — Late-arrival delay:** How far behind the stragglers ran in August, up 0.60 days on the month and 1.92 days worse than a year earlier [1].
- **32.3% — Asia's on-time rate:** The regional August reading. Shanghai sat at 12.2% and Ningbo at 16.9% as the port jams spread across countries [4].
- **6.75 vs 2.95 days — The dwell split:** Rail-bound boxes waited 6.75 days at the twin ports, up from 6.34 in July and the longest in 12 months. Truck-bound boxes eased to 2.95 days from 3.03 [2][3].

## What I'd watch:

Carriers are answering the wrong question, which is what I find most telling. Their response to a backed-up network is to pull space out of it, not to add the buffer the seam needs.

- **Extra sailings that are really a backlog:** Shipping lines put 1.50 million TEU into Asia to North Europe sailings for the four-week Golden Week period, 27% more than a year earlier and 60% above the pre-pandemic norm [5]. Those are delayed ships leaving alongside on-time ones. Sea-Intelligence told shippers to treat the mid-to-late October timetables as "highly volatile placeholders" [5].
- **Cancellations land where the cargo is:** Drewry counted 39 blank sailings across the main East-West trades between 5 October and 8 November. That is a 5% cut of 710 planned voyages, half of them on the transpacific [6].
- **One carrier stopped selling into a jammed port:** Hapag-Lloyd suspended bookings to Manila, Batangas and Subic Bay through 31 December. Customs brokers and truckers there held a work stoppage, and only about 10% of cargo was still moving out [7].
- **What I'd watch next:** Whether rail dwell climbs again in the October reading. The National Retail Federation expected a final peak-season bump that could make September the busiest month of the year for import volume [3]. My read: the number worth tracking is no longer the vessel on-time rate. It is the days a box spends between the quay and the train.

One smaller event shows how narrow the manageable slice is. Hurricane Isaias shut the Port of Mobile for about two days in early October, and the port reopened once the Coast Guard lifted its restrictions [8]. That is a true exception: a bounded event with a start and an end. A network running near fifty percent on time is not that.

## The catch

The honest limit is that these are two datasets from two publishers, and neither one proves the ocean delay caused the dwell rise. Volumes alone could explain the rail figure. The busiest three-month stretch in the history of the Port of Los Angeles is a demand story as much as a jam story [2].

I want to be fair to what worked, too. Truck dwell fell. Terminals, drayage firms and rail partners kept local cargo moving at a pace that has held near three days for a year [3]. A gateway is not failing when one of its two exits slows down. It is being asked to hold more than its inland link can take.

The counter-argument deserves a hearing. The on-time rate has run below par for most of 2026, so another monthly drop is a trend line, not an alarm. Carriers can add sailings, ports can add rail slots, and the reading can swing back inside a quarter.

What I take from it is narrower than a verdict. Per-leg visibility is still worth having, and the alert queue it produces is real work. The question I would put to any architect now is where the next dollar goes: another feed into another carrier system, or room at the seam where two delays add up instead of cancelling out.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Global container schedule reliability fell to 49.9% in August, the lowest since September 2022. Rail-bound dwell at Los Angeles and Long Beach climbed to 6.75 days, the longest in a year.
- **Why It Matters:** Both are August readings, and they sit at either end of the same box's trip. Truck dwell fell at the same gateway, so the slowdown sits in the handoff between the ship and the train, where a control tower has the least leverage and the alert queue grows fastest.
- **What I'd watch:** Whether the October dwell figure climbs again as peak season runs late, and whether carriers keep swapping capacity cuts for buffer room.
  - **The seam:** The step where a box leaves a marine terminal and starts moving inland by rail, which is where the two measured delays meet.
  - **Buffer room:** Space to hold cargo at that handoff instead of moving it on the first plan, such as on-dock rail slots or near-port transloading space.
  - **Volatile placeholders:** Timetables published during a jam that carriers do not expect to honor, which Sea-Intelligence flagged for mid-to-late October.
- **The Catch:** Two datasets from two publishers show a link, not a proven cause. Truck dwell fell and the gateway kept working, so the case for spending at the seam rests on where the marginal delay lands, not on a claim that visibility failed.
