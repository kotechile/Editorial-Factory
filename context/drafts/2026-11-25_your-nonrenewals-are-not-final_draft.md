<!-- lead -->
Insurers refused to renew more than 6,200 New Mexico homeowners' policies in 2025, after the state's worst year on record. But a non-renewal is not always final, as the state's top insurance official says: some letters were reversed once owners papered their wildfire work. That path is free to attempt, and it appears on none of the notices. Most owners spend their 30 days hunting a new policy instead. Skip the file, and the backup plan pays what the house is worth used, not new, under a hard dollar cap.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
This piece explains what a non-renewal is in New Mexico right now: a record wave of refusals, the state's own insurance chief on record that proof of mitigation reverses some of them, and a last-resort plan that pays used value under a cap.

<!-- tension -->
## Why the refusals, and what answers

The record went public in late October. State Rep. Anita Gonzales put a blunt question to a panel of carrier executives: if insurers pull out of whole towns, how would those towns rebuild? [1]. Her district sits inside the Hermits Peak-Calf Canyon burn scar. That fire was the largest in state history.

The figures behind the question came from the state's insurance office. Its count says national insurers refused to renew more than 6,200 homeowners policies in 2025, a state record [1]. That is nearly triple the 2021 level [1]. Most of the refusals cluster in wildfire-prone counties: Rio Arriba, Taos, Sandoval and Santa Fe [2].

The industry witness explained the carrier's side. Julie Rochman once ran a national research group for the industry. She told the panel that carriers "back away" when the risk on their books already maxes out what they can pay [1]. Rising reinsurance and building costs squeeze the same balance sheet [1]. Nothing in that account responds to a better cover letter.

What does respond is proof of real work, and I find it telling that the state's own superintendent said so. Alice Kane, the state's insurance chief, told lawmakers this summer that some non-renewals were reversed after homeowners cut their fire risk [2]. She also said insurers will soon give discounts for verified work [2]. Her office points owners to Wildfire Prepared Home, the certification program run by the Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety (IBHS) [2]. The program turns weekend labor into something an underwriter can read. It lists cleared roofs and gutters, open space around the house, and hardened vents and decks, all checked by a third party [2]. The vent item carries the most risk for the least money, because a screen with wide gaps works as an ember door (the cost is the subject of [Your 1/8-inch screen is an ember door](/your-1-8-inch-screen-leaks-embers/)).

## By the numbers
- **More than 6,200 policies:** The 2025 non-renewal count in New Mexico, a state record and near-triple 2021 [1].
- **30 days:** The minimum written notice before a New Mexico non-renewal takes effect [2].
- **$750,000:** The FAIR Plan's maximum residential payout in protection classes 1–7, and $500,000 in classes 8–10 [3].

## What the dropped owners are doing

The notice window is the fulcrum, and here is the pattern I see in the agent accounts. New Mexico requires at least 30 days' written notice before a policy expires [2]. The law does not force the insurer to print a reason; the common ones here are fire exposure, roof condition, and brush near the house [2]. Agents report owners doing three things at once. They ask for the reason. They order a roof check on paper. They quote several carriers through a local agent [2].

The reversal lever lives inside that same month: Kane tied the reversals to fire-risk work. In practice that means a paper record an inspector can check, not a promise to reach the gutters in spring [2]. The IBHS label exists to make that record portable. It lists finished items, checked by a third party, and it counts in New Mexico [2].

The fallback at the end of the month is the FAIR Plan (Fair Access to Insurance Requirements — the state's insurer of last resort), and its terms are the state's own. The New Mexico Property Insurance Program accepts an applicant only after a denial in the regular market [3]. Since early November it also requires the owner and producer to sign an affidavit confirming that denial, when applying and at each renewal [3]. Its coverage list is short: fire, wind and hail and similar perils, and vandalism [3]. It also states that no replacement-cost coverage exists: property there "is not insured for more than its actual cash value" [3]. There is no liability option at all [3]. Even entry has a closure rule, and the plan will not take a new application within 50 miles of an active fire until crews contain it [3].

## The catch

The reversal is testimony, not a rule, and that gap is what I keep coming back to. Kane said some letters were reversed; she did not say a good file entitles anyone to reinstatement [2]. No New Mexico regulation guarantees a discount amount or a renewal for a certified home [2]. A carrier whose regional book is full can still decline a certified house, which is the position Rochman described [1]. So the two tracks run side by side. California answered the same pressure this month with a one-year freeze that blocks non-renewals for roughly 50,000 owners around one fire's edge [4]. New Mexico has no freeze, so the file is the only lever its homeowners hold.
