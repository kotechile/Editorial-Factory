<!-- lead -->
If your gas water heater is rated above 75,000 Btu/hr, the cheap like-for-like tank swap ended on October 6. That is the trap, and it is not really about the tank. It is about the venting.

On that date the efficiency floor for the class rose from 0.5922 to 0.8622 at 75 gallons [1]. A plain tank does not reach that number.

So the replacement is a condensing unit. And a condensing unit needs new venting and a drain for the water it makes [5].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The rule itself is old. The Department of Energy (DOE) set the standard in October 2023 and gave makers three years to comply [2].

What arrived this month is the date. Any unit made on or after October 6, 2026 has to meet it [2].

The class it hits is small and ordinary. Gas storage heaters from 75,000 to 105,000 Btu/hr, with tanks up to 120 gallons, sat in a class called residential-duty commercial [1]. Makers say many high-input units sold into homes fell inside it [5]. Bradford White now lists its plain 55, 75 and 100-gallon models as no longer made [5]. Its plain 50-gallon models stay on sale.

What strikes me here is the gap between how the rule was sold and where the cost lands. DOE sold it on lifetime cost. Its own text concedes the savings are "an overall average, reflecting the fractions of consumers that are better off and that are worse off" [2]. Averages work for a national number. They tell you nothing about the owner whose tank dies on a Sunday.

Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies put out the other half of that picture on October 1. Its new paper measures what the authors call the retrofit gap [6]. The owners most exposed to high bills do the least energy work on their homes.

The split is stark. Owners earning $150,000 or more did 39.5 percent of the $139.1 billion spent on energy-related work in 2023. They were 9.9 percent of the energy-insecure.

Owners under $30,000 did 8.9 percent of the spending. They were 25.6 percent of the energy-insecure [6].

Water heaters sit in the middle of that data. The survey shows 4.5 percent of owners doing water-heater work in 2023. The average job ran $1,490, for $5.9 billion in total [6].

Raise the floor of that job, and you move a number that already sits near the edge of many budgets.

## By the numbers

- **0.5922 to 0.8622 — Efficiency floor at 75 gallons:** the standard for equipment made after October 6, 2026, against the level before it [1].
- **75,000 Btu/hr — The class line:** above it, a gas storage heater falls into the class where condensing gear is now required [5].
- **39.5% vs 9.9% — Spending against exposure:** the share of energy work dollars from owners earning $150,000 or more, against their share of the energy-insecure [6].
- **$1,490 — Average water-heater job:** the 2023 average spend per owner who did the work, across $5.9 billion of it [6].

## What I'd watch:

The first number I would want on a quote is the input rating. A plain 50-gallon tank is still legal to buy and install. If the load fits that size, the venting work goes away [5].

- **The vent route:** a condensing unit cannot share the old metal flue. The quote either runs plastic pipe to a side wall or relines the chimney [2][5]. Where the flue crosses finished space, that is the big line.
- **The drain:** these units give off acidic water. It has to drain away or be pumped out, and DOE counts pumps and neutralisers as real install items [2].
- **The fallback shapes:** tankless with a storage tank, a volume heater, or a boiler with an indirect tank all count as compliant routes [5]. Each one moves the work. None of them removes it.
- **The price letters:** October brought another round of maker increases, mostly 3 to 6 percent [7]. Linesets rose 12.9 percent on average and one controls maker went as high as 15 percent. The parts around the tank reprice on their own schedule.
- **The owners who wait:** in the Harvard data, owners under $30,000 put 6.3 percent of income into energy work. Owners at $150,000 or more put in 0.8 percent [6]. That gap is not taste. It is the lack of a reserve.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

The savings are real, and they are slow. Moving the class from 80 to 95 percent thermal efficiency cuts fuel per unit of heat by about a sixth. That is simple math: one over 0.80 against one over 0.95 [5].

The inspection trade expects a tank to last 7 to 12 years, so the payback runs in years [9]. This winter trims it further: homes heated with natural gas are forecast to spend 9 percent less than last winter [8].

The rule may also move again. DOE has said it will not seek civil penalties for the thermal-efficiency standards through October 2027 [3]. The gas groups have asked for a date of 2030 or later while the Supreme Court reviews the rule [4].

My read: the pause is written around the thermal-efficiency standards, a different paragraph from the efficiency-factor standard this class sits under. That is a reading of two documents, not a legal opinion [1][3].

There is a practical sting either way. The manufacturing date has passed, and stock made before it can still be sold and installed. So the cheap units will thin out on a schedule nobody publishes.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** On October 6, the efficiency floor for one class of gas water heater rose far enough that only condensing units can meet it. A swap in that class now carries new venting and a drain.
- **Why It Matters:** The change lands on a scheduled asset with a 7-to-12-year life. The households doing the least replacement work are the ones carrying the highest bills.
- **What I'd Watch:** Where the new cost shows up, and who ends up putting the job off.
  - **Input rating:** the Btu/hr figure on the old unit's label decides whether the venting work applies at all.
  - **The 50-gallon route:** the smaller plain class stays on sale, which keeps one like-for-like path open.
  - **The court case:** a pending petition and a Supreme Court review could move the date again.
- **The Catch:** The gain is about a sixth less fuel, which takes years to repay a venting job. The DOE math is an average across owners who gain and owners who lose.
