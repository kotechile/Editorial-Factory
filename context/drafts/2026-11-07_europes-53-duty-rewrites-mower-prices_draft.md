<!-- lead -->
Europe's duty on Chinese-built robot mowers would run from 45.6 to 53.3 percent, and it splits next season's prices by factory rather than by brand [1]. On the day that paper moved, the market leader said it would likely have to cut its own starting price below €1,000 [2]. For the 2027 season the sticker follows the plant that built the unit, not the badge on the deck.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Brussels wants to tax Chinese-built robot mowers at 45.6 to 53.3 percent [1].
- **The numbers:** 53.3 percent is the rate proposed for exporters that did not cooperate [1].
- **The architectural rule:** A model built outside China escapes the duty; one built inside pays it [1].

<!-- tension -->

## Why the factory beats the model

Husqvarna's Czech arm asked Brussels to look at Chinese-built mowers, and the case opened in November 2025 [1]. The Commission held back emergency duties in June. Then on 21 October it sent out a 90-page paper that found dumping and harm, and it backs final duties [1]. Those rates are not law yet. They sit in a paper that is not public, and the January deadline is when they get settled [3].

A duty is charged on the value declared at the border, not on the shelf price. One design can therefore pay very different money depending on where its frame was bolted together. Kress has moved most of its mower range out of China and expects most models to sit outside the charge [1]. The Voyager and MasterSeries stay in Chinese plants, and their prices move when the year turns [1]. Two lines, one showroom, two price paths. The gap between them is the duty itself, not a spread anyone has measured on a shelf.

The base matters too. An entry mower from one Chinese brand is listed at $899 against a $999 list on its own US store this week [5]. A charge at the border works through to that sticker rather than replacing it. The factory question therefore reaches a buyer who never opens a customs form.

The incumbent's problem is that it asks for cover while giving up the price it wants covered. Husqvarna's chief executive told Reuters the firm lost more of its European share in units than in value [2]. It held its ground with traditional dealers but faced much tougher competition in shops and online [2]. Mower assembly has already shifted from Newton Aycliffe in Britain to Czechia, which cost around 50 roles [2]. The quarter behind that call was poor, with organic sales down 5 percent and an operating loss of 334 million krona [4]. The group's own note said robot mowing faced swollen dealer stock in Europe [4].

Country of origin is not printed on the carton, and a dealer quoting a price in November is quoting a machine already in the channel. The same badge can cover both cases. A model name tells a buyer nothing about which side of the line the unit came from, and neither does the spec sheet. The only readable clue is the price itself, which is why two dealers can quote figures that look like a mistake. What I find hardest to square is a company asking for protection on the models it still builds in the country its own complaint names.

## By the numbers

- **€1,000 — the starting price Husqvarna says it must cut:** the chief executive ruled out competing with machines costing under €500 [2].
- **53.3 percent — the proposed rate on non-cooperating Chinese-built mowers:** Kress has told dealers its China-built models get new prices on 1 January 2027 [1].
- **50 roles — the cost of moving one line:** robot mower production shifted from Newton Aycliffe to Czechia, completed this autumn [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## Where the calendar does the negotiating

Two dates are easy to confuse, and they do different work. Kress's current prices hold only for machines ordered and delivered by 31 December 2026, with payment terms on those units stretched to 31 March [1]. Anything ordered after the year closes gets priced against whatever Brussels settles in the new year.

What I watch next is the November price list Kress says will cover the rest of its range [1]. It answers the question the paper leaves open: whether a brand can hold a price by moving a factory line faster than Brussels can publish a rate. The brands that moved early inherit the room the duty creates. The rest are left explaining a jump to dealers who were briefed in October.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The duty is European, and the rates sit in a paper nobody can read. None of this changes the price of a mower already parked in an American garage. The American version of the same question is a different instrument — [a federal approval list that decides which models may be sold at all](/your-mower-needs-4-approvals/) — and the two clocks do not move together.

What I find hard to square is the sequence. The protection the incumbent asked for arrives after the entry price it wanted protected was already conceded. The duty therefore reads as a margin repair for the brands that shifted their factories, and a straight cost increase for the rest. A buyer comparing two machines that look identical this November cannot see which path is inside the carton.
