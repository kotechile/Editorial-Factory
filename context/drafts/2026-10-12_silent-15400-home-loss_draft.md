<!-- lead -->

The average water-damage claim on a US home runs $15,400, and about 1.50% of insured homes file one in a typical year [1]. That is roughly one in 67. The parts that feed the loss have set service lives: six to 12 years for a standard water heater, seven to 15 for a central air conditioner [3].

Both the failure and the bill sit on a schedule. Most homes hold no line item for either.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

An insurer prices a house by what breaks, not by how it looks. The Insurance Information Institute (III) is the industry's research arm. It tracks each cause of loss twice: how often a claim is filed, and what it pays [1].

Water damage and freezing is the awkward middle of that table. It took 22.6% of homeowners property-damage claim dollars in 2023, behind only wind and hail [1].

It needs no storm. It needs a supply line, a drain pan, or a water heater at the end of its listed life [3].

Some losses upkeep cannot touch, and those are priced on a different curve. Fire and lightning reaches 0.23% of homes at $88,170 a claim. Wind and hail reaches 2.80% at $14,747 [1]. Neither responds to a service visit.

My read: this is why deferred maintenance reads as a saving and behaves as a liability. A household is not choosing between spending and not spending. It is choosing between a visit priced off a $23.84-an-hour trade [4] and the loss table's own average for the failure [1].

## By the numbers

- **1.50% — Water-damage claim rate:** the share of insured homes that filed a water damage and freezing claim in a typical year from 2019 to 2023. About one in 67 [1].
- **$15,400 — Average water-damage claim:** the average paid for that cause of loss over the same five years. All causes averaged $20,062 in 2023 [1].
- **$231 — Expected annual loss:** 1.50% times $15,400. That is the water-damage payout the average insured home creates for the pool each year — our arithmetic, not a figure the table prints [1].
- **6 to 12 years — Water heater life:** the listed life of a standard water heater. A central air conditioner is 7 to 15 years, and a ceiling vent fan 5 to 10 [3].

## What I'd watch:

I have been reading these loss tables the way an owner reads a service history.

- **The pool math:** 5.33 claims per 100 homes at $20,062 each in 2023 is $1,069 of insured loss per home for the year, before a dollar of premium [1]. Every owner pays into that pool, whether or not they file.
- **The uninsured half:** the EPA (the Environmental Protection Agency) WaterSense program counts household leaks at nearly 1 trillion gallons a year [2]. One faucet dripping once a second wastes more than 3,000 gallons. Fixing the easy leaks saves about 10% of a water bill. None of it shows up in a claim.
- **The labour price:** the US Department of Labor's job database puts maintenance and repair workers at a $23.84 median hourly wage in 2025, or $49,590 a year [4]. That wage, not the part, is most of what a preventive visit buys.
- **The ageing stock:** Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies counts 145 million US homes [5]. The market for remodels sits above $600 billion, still 50% above pre-pandemic levels. Its own read is that far more of that money needs to go into energy and weather resilience.

The part I keep circling: every component on that list is one a household already owns.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

That $231 is not a household's bill, and reading it that way is the easy error. It is what the pool pays out per insured home, across everyone, most of whom never file.

One owner's cash exposure is the deductible and the premium effect, not the $15,400 average [1]. It is also whatever the policy leaves out, plus the disruption.

III's table is a five-year window, 2019 to 2023. The water damage and freezing line mixes in weather that no upkeep controls. Nothing in it measures whether a service visit prevented a loss.

I could be wrong to read a claims table as a maintenance argument at all. The field notes this desk keeps for home capital tell a simpler story: a skipped $150 attic condensate line clear that later closed as a $16,800 claim. That is one case, not a rate.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Deferred home maintenance has a published price. Insurers' own loss tables put water damage and freezing at 1.50% of insured homes a year, at $15,400 a claim.
- **Why It Matters:** The failure date is knowable and the bill is on the record. That makes upkeep a capital decision with numbers, not a chore.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The expected-loss line:** 1.50% times $15,400 gives $231 a year per insured home, the figure a reserve would be built on.
  - **The service-life clock:** water heaters, air conditioners and vent fans have published lives, so replacements can be forecast, not absorbed.
  - **The uninsured leaks:** small leaks waste far more water than owners expect, and they never appear in a claim.
- **The Catch:** those averages are what the pool pays, not what one owner pays. The loss line also includes weather no service visit can prevent.
