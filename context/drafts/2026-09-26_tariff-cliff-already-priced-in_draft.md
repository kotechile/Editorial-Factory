<!-- lead -->
On Sept. 23, the U.S. and China bought two months of tariff calm [1]. A day later, the Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta showed American companies don't believe it: 70% of firms are holding their tariff refunds as cash rather than reinvesting them [2]. Washington extended the truce to Jan. 10, 2027; corporate America responded by building a cash buffer. That gap between the official calendar and the actual behavior is the story.

<!-- tension -->
**The big picture:** Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said the U.S. and China will extend what he calls the "Busan Agreement" — the economic détente that suspends some tariffs — from its Nov. 10 expiry to Jan. 10 [1]. He was blunt about what happens next: "I don't know whether a bigger deal can be done. I don't know whether we will just roll the current deal" [1]. Two months is a pause, not a resolution.

**Why it matters:** The Atlanta Fed's Survey of Business Uncertainty — fielded in August, before the extension — found firms already sitting on their tariff refunds. The refund pool is nearly $170 billion, with roughly $100 billion refunded by late July [2]. Rather than spend it, firms are keeping it as cash. That is the market's revealed bet: when a company chooses a cash buffer over reinvestment, it is pricing in the risk that the money's underlying conditions — the suspended tariffs — snap back. What strikes me is the timing: the survey was fielded in August, before the extension, so firms were already hedging. I read Jan. 10 as a September problem, not a December one.

**By the numbers:**
- **2 months:** The truce now runs to Jan. 10, 2027, instead of expiring Nov. 10 [1].
- **70%:** Firms that plan to retain at least some of their refund as cash or cash equivalents [2].
- **~$100B:** Refunds already paid out by late July, out of a nearly $170B pool [2].
- **17.2% / 14.8%:** The minority planning customer rebates or lower prices — the small slice passing the windfall through [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
**Where this bites:** I would not call the cash hoarding a mistake. It is a hedge, and the cost of that hedge lands in four places.

- **Landed costs get re-modelled first.** The import bill under a tariff snap-back is the number operators are re-running now, and the SKUs that swing cost of goods sold most are the ones that move first.
- **Cash posture is the exposure.** The firms holding cash are hedged; the under-hedged combination is lean cash plus heavy imported inputs.
- **Supplier capacity is cheap today and contested in December.** If the cliff comes, every competitor chases the same alternate sourcing at once, and that queue is the one I would worry about.
- **Pass-through is the signal to watch.** The 17.2% of firms cutting prices and giving rebates are telegraphing their tariff strategy, and their moves reveal who thinks the calm holds.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
**The catch:** Cash hoarding is not proof of a tariff snap-back. It is also standard, prudent liquidity management — the Atlanta Fed itself notes the retention matches "the standard textbook analysis" of how firms handle one-off windfalls [2]. And the truce could still roll into a larger deal before January; Bessent left that door open [1]. My read: the refund data shows firms are not betting the calm holds; they are refusing to bet against it. One-third of refunds go to the most financially constrained firms — for them the cash is survival, not strategy [2].
