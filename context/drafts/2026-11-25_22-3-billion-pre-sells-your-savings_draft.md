# $22.3 Billion Pre-Sells Your Savings

<!-- lead -->

Symbotic closed its fiscal year with $22.3 billion of contracted automation work already on its books [1]. Four days earlier, Procter & Gamble told investors it will remove 20–30% of its product-supply workforce by 2030 as automation expands [4]. The robots for the next decade are largely booked. The headcount they were priced against is already a budget line. If your payback case still counts on wage inflation, it was built for a market both announcements say is ending.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

Symbotic ended the year with $22.3 billion of booked work. Procter & Gamble will cut up to 30% of its supply staff as automation scales. Capex is sold forward against savings buyers are turning into planned cuts. Your payback now rides on a labor line that falls by design.

<!-- tension -->

## Signed before the cuts

Symbotic filed its annual report on November 23, 2026, covering a fiscal year that ended September 26 [1]. Its backlog, by the company's own definition, is unperformed work under existing contracts — mostly systems not yet delivered [1]. Roughly a sixth of it turns into revenue next fiscal year [1]. The same day, the earnings call added nearly $7 billion more, to enter the backlog next quarter through the new Walmart deal [3].

The commitments behind that number go back years. Walmart signed a master agreement with Symbotic in 2017, expanded it in 2021 to 25 distribution centers, then widened it in 2022 to cover all 42 [1]. One buyer now holds about 47% of the backlog and 87% of the year's revenue [1]. The backlog is also priced to guard the seller's margin if costs climb [1].

On November 19, Procter & Gamble put the buyer side on the record. Its supply chief told investors that 20% to 30% of the product-supply workforce will be gone by 2030, contractors included, as manual work gives way to machines [4]. The company already runs autonomous production at more than 15 sites [4]. The same presentation promised to free employees "for higher-value work" [4]. That promise now shares a page with a 20–30% headcount cut. Two descriptions of one workforce — and only one of them is a plan.

I read the two documents as one trade, seen from each side. The labor savings behind a decade of automation were priced in the shortage years. Back then, wages kept rising and workers kept leaving. What is being delivered now is different: headcount falling by plan, at a named buyer, on a dated schedule. The old payback case assumed a labor line that keeps costing more. The new one assumes a line that disappears on purpose. That is a different risk. You amortize committed capital against savings that stop compounding the moment the cut is done.

## By the numbers

- **$22.3 billion:** Symbotic's contracted backlog at fiscal year-end, mostly undelivered systems [1].
- **$3.57 billion:** the desk's division of the slice due to convert to revenue next fiscal year [1].
- **20–30% of product-supply staff:** the Procter & Gamble reduction planned by 2030, contractors included [4].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch

The people closest to this are re-reading contracts rather than celebrating. One buyer carries nearly half the backlog [1], so watch first whether next year's revenue slips as install crews run out of slots. The second thing is renewal pricing. The backlog is priced to protect the seller's margin through inflation [1], so a buyer whose labor savings fade cannot expect the contract to share the pain. This desk has argued before that [the wage floor you assume sets the robot's payback](/wage-floor-sets-robot-payback/) — that floor is now falling on the buyer's side of the ledger. I would want a payback case re-run on a labor line that is being cut by plan, before signing anything that long. Exits stay hard after signing: an industry survey in late October put 94% of buyers calling a switch prohibitively expensive [5]. The labor pool itself is also thinning on the government's count [6].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

Neither document proves what the pairing implies. The backlog is a record of demand already contracted. It says nothing about whether the promised productivity arrives, and the disclosure warns that contracts can be adjusted for inflation and revalidation [1]. Procter & Gamble's cut is a plan running to 2030, not a done deal, and its productivity projection is the company's own [4]. What both documents do establish is direction. Capital is contracted forward; headcount is committed down. Whether the savings hold at contract prices, or fade after the first cuts, is a question neither company must answer. The buyer pays either way.
