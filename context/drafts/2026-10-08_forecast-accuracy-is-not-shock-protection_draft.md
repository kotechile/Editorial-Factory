<!-- lead -->
A new benchmark of live deployments reports that an Artificial Intelligence (AI) forecast beat the forecast each company already ran in 15 of 15 cases, cutting error by 32% on average [1]. The same month, the world's climate-risk analysts put a nearly trillion-dollar price tag on an El Niño that is not set to peak until December [2][3]. My read: both numbers land on the same planning meeting, and only one of them is something a better model can fix.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The benchmark measures something narrow and useful. It compares an AI forecast against the forecast a company already trusted — the spreadsheet, the consensus number from Sales and Operations Planning (S&OP), or a planner's own call — on that company's own data [1]. On that test the AI won every time, by 14% to 56%.

That is a real gain. It is also a gain against reducible error — the pattern noise a model can learn from the past.

The El Niño is not that. Verisk Maplecroft's September 30 report warns that this year's event is "already the strongest ever recorded in terms of Pacific Ocean temperatures," and that a repeat of the 1997/98 El Niño — which triggered an estimated 5.7 trillion US dollars in losses — is possible [2].

What I keep circling is that teams read an accuracy benchmark as risk cover, when the benchmark never tested them against a break in the pattern. A tighter forecast does not sit between a copper mine and its port.

## By the numbers

- **32% — Average error cut:** Across 15 live deployments, the AI forecast beat the incumbent's error in each company's own metric, with reductions from 14% to 56% [1].
- **5.7 trillion US dollars — 1997/98 El Niño cost:** The template Verisk Maplecroft says this year could repeat or amplify, roughly three times the 2008 financial crisis hit to global output [2].
- **$986 billion — 2027 loss estimate:** Oxford Economics puts near-term global losses near a trillion dollars in 2027, and up to $7.2 trillion over six years [3].
- **70% — Manual effort removed:** Planning effort fell by 70% on average in the benchmark, and one firm found three-quarters of its forecast volume needed no edit at all [1].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The firms closest to the risk are already splitting the two jobs. TJX (the discount-retail group behind T.J. Maxx and Marshalls) told investors in September that it leaned on its holding capability to line up seasonal merchandise with actual weather-driven demand [5] — a buffer answer, not a model answer.

Gartner's own October 5–6 planning summit points at the same seam. Its session notes warn that teams chase higher forecast accuracy, then watch factories overreact when a monthly plan collides with a weekly schedule, creating "phantom demand spikes" and emergency runs [4].

Three things I am watching next:
- **The buffer line:** Whether the same budget that funds a demand-sensing upgrade also funds the extra capacity a record El Niño may call for, or whether one quietly pays for the other.
- **The exposure map:** Which sourcing teams have re-quoted Latin American supply of copper, coffee, soy, maize and salmon before the December peak, and which are still planning on last year's bands [2].
- **The decision clock:** How fast a firm can act once a signal moves — the gap Gartner flags between spotting a problem and responding to it [4].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

I could be wrong to frame these as one story. A firm that only sells stable, local goods may never feel the El Niño, and its accuracy upgrade is a clean win on its own terms [1].

The catch is what the benchmark can prove. Its wins come from shrinking error on patterns the model has already seen. A record anomaly is, by definition, outside that set.

So the two spends are not rivals. Accurate math and held buffer solve different problems, and a plan that funds only one is exposed on the other.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A first-of-its-kind benchmark says an AI forecast beat the one a company already ran in 15 of 15 live deployments, cutting error 32% on average. The same month, risk analysts warned a record El Niño — on the 1997/98 template — is not set to peak until December.
- **Why It Matters:** Forecast accuracy is being sold and read as risk reduction. The benchmark's wins come from reducible, historical error, which is not the same thing as cover against a break in the pattern.
- **What I'd Watch:** I am watching how planning teams split the two jobs.
  - **The buffer:** Held capacity and inventory that absorb a shock no forecast can see.
  - **The clock:** How fast a firm can act once a signal moves, which Gartner flags as the real cost.
  - **The exposure map:** Which supply lines into Latin America have been re-quoted before the December peak.
- **The Catch:** The accuracy gain is real but bounded. Better math and more buffer solve different problems, so a plan that funds only one is exposed on the other.
