<!-- lead -->
Over 70% of vested startup stock options are never exercised, according to new data from Carta published September 10 [1]. The option grant at the center of your offer letter is, for most employees, paper wealth that never converts to a single dollar.

<!-- tension -->
**Why it matters:** Equity is sold as the startup's headline benefit, but Carta's data shows it mostly goes unclaimed — and the variable that separates exercisers from non-exercisers isn't grant size, it's a retirement account.

Employees with a 401(k) plan through their employer exercise at 26.1%, versus 22.8% for those without [1][2]. The gap looks small until you realize what it represents: a financial cushion is what gives someone the freedom to hand over cash for shares they may never be able to sell. Close to half of US companies on Carta's platform offer no 401(k) at all [1]. So most startup employees are betting their long-term security on a single, illiquid, concentrated asset that may never pay out.

**The big picture:** A 401(k) is liquid at retirement, diversified, and independent of any single transaction. Equity is the opposite — illiquid, concentrated, and dependent on a liquidity event that may never come [1]. Carta and Vestwell's joint data shows the two don't substitute for each other; they compound.

**By the numbers:**
- **70%+ unexercised:** Over 70% of vested option grants are never exercised, and the ones that are exercised often don't generate meaningful wealth [1][2].
- **26.1% vs 22.8%:** Having a 401(k) lifts the exercise rate by 3.3 points — not from a bigger grant, but from a financial cushion [1][2].
- **~Half with no 401(k):** Close to half of US companies on Carta offer no plan; below 25 employees only 39% do, rising to 49% at 25–100 [1].
- **$100k by year four:** The median 401(k) saver earning $200k+ holds more than $100k after four years; under-$75k earners save just $1,825 in year one [1][2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
The playbook for anyone holding or weighing startup equity:

- **Negotiate the 401(k) match as hard as the option grant.** The match funds the cash you'll need to exercise options when the window opens. Ask about the match and its vesting in the same breath you ask about the strike price.
- **Model the exercise cost before you sign.** Exercising costs cash — strike price times shares, plus tax. If the company offers no 401(k) and you can't self-fund exercise, the grant is effectively vapor. Run that math before you count options as income.
- **Book unexercised equity at zero.** Until you exercise and hold the shares, an option is an option, not an asset. Discount it accordingly in any offer comparison against liquid public stock.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
**The catch:** A 401(k) doesn't make bad options good. Exercising underwater options — where the strike price sits above the current value — or paying alternative minimum tax on a paper gain that later evaporates can still burn you [2]. And the 26.1% vs 22.8% gap is correlation, not causation: the employees who exercise may simply be better paid and more secure to begin with. The data says financial stability enables exercise; it does not say exercise is always the right move.

<!-- tldr -->
- **The Big Shift:** Carta's September 10 data shows over 70% of vested startup options are never exercised, and that a 401(k) — not a bigger grant — is what separates employees who cash in from those who don't.
- **Why It Matters:** Paper equity that never converts is worthless. Close to half of US startups offer no 401(k), so most employees are betting their retirement on one illiquid, concentrated asset.
- **The Winning Moves:**
  - **Negotiate the 401(k) match:** It funds the cash you need to exercise options when the window opens.
  - **Model exercise cost upfront:** Strike price plus tax is the real price of your equity; if you can't fund it, the grant is vapor.
  - **Book options at zero:** Until exercised, treat unexercised equity as an option, not an asset, in any comp comparison.
- **The Catch:** A 401(k) doesn't make underwater options smart to exercise, and the exercise-rate gap is partly correlation with higher pay and security — not proof that exercise is always the right move.
