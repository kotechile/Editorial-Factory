<!-- lead -->
New York City (NYC) buys night deliveries now, and the money comes from the trucks. The city will hand more than half a million dollars to up to 14 businesses so they shift freight into the 7 p.m. to 6 a.m. window [1][3]. The diesel those trucks burn at midnight is a different ledger, and it bills the same at noon as it does at 1 a.m.: California was above $8 a gallon in the week of Oct. 5, and the national average sat $3.711 above a year ago [4][6].

The saving the city is buying is real. Carriers that shift a route into the off-hours cut operating costs 35% to 45%, and save $30,000 to $50,000 a year on each converted route [5].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

I've been reading New York's freight paperwork all week, and the two halves of this story sit on different ledgers. The city is buying the cheapest lever it owns: the hour a truck runs [1][3]. The toll on entering Manhattan's business district pays businesses to move deliveries out of the daytime traffic that made the toll necessary.

The plan counts trucks, not dollars. The goal is 5,000 off-hour locations by 2040, which the city reads as 62,000 trucks shifted out of peak [1]. The saving is counted in time. Faster travel and fewer parking fines are where the 35% to 45% comes from [5].

The cost that is closing small carriers is counted in gallons, and the hour does not enter it. California averaged $8.09 on Monday, a cent above the week before, after September weeks near $8.25 [6]. The national figure was $6.199 for the week of Oct. 5, down from $6.529 two weeks earlier but $3.711 above the $2.488 of a year ago [4]. A fuel surcharge covers only about 70% to 75% of that for the smallest operators, by the California Trucking Association's own account [6].

## By the numbers

- **$5 million — off-hour incentive pool:** NYC DOT (the New York City Department of Transportation) is giving out toll money, through the Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA), to businesses that commit to a year of off-hour deliveries and share their data [1][3].
- **$30,000–$50,000 — saved per converted route:** carriers that replace a regular-hour route with off-hour delivery cut operating costs 35% to 45%, and bank that saving every year, on the program's own case record [5].
- **$6.199 — U.S. diesel, week of Oct. 5:** the national average, down from $6.529 two weeks earlier, still $3.711 above the $2.488 of a year ago [4].
- **$8.082 — California diesel:** the highest state reading in that same table, with the statewide average at $8.09 the following Monday and September weeks near $8.25 [4][6].

## What I'd watch:

Fleets are already answering the fuel line with tools the clock cannot touch. United Natural Foods is hedging its diesel and running route software across close to 50 distribution centers and 2,000 vans [7]. It is hunting fewer miles, not different hours. Costco offset higher fuel surcharges through lower contract rates, and McCormick & Co. through its own cost program [7].

- **The second phase:** applications are open on a rolling basis, and the first phase spreads more than $500,000 across up to 14 businesses [1]. I want to see whether the money converts routes or just buys quiet pallet trucks and cameras [1][3].
- **The contract line:** surcharges cover 70% to 75% of the fuel cost for small carriers [6]. The part I keep circling is whether that gap moves into the next round of rates or stays with the operator.
- **The tax argument:** California's freight groups asked Governor Gavin Newsom for help, and the state's Farm Bureau reckons dropping the state diesel tax would save 73 cents a gallon [6]. The federal order that moved dyed diesel onto public roads defers taxes, but it changes little in a state that restricts that fuel anyway [6].
- **The modes that skip the road:** Walmart and Wing are adding Denver and Seattle next year on the way to more than 270 stores in 2027 [8]. Best Buy put electric yard switchers to work at two California distribution centers [9]. Curious how much of the last mile's answer turns out to be fewer trucks rather than later ones.
- **The 2027 price:** a University of Pittsburgh at Bradford energy researcher expects elevated diesel well into 2027 while damaged refining capacity is rebuilt [6]. If that holds, a fifteen-year plan and a one-year bill stay on different clocks.

## The catch

The off-hour saving is not a myth, and that is the uncomfortable part. It is real and it is measured. It comes from time — faster travel, fewer fines, more stops per shift — not from a cheaper gallon [5].

The truck still burns what it burns, mile for mile. I could be wrong about how much of the bill a carrier can pass on, but the surcharge math suggests the smallest operators absorb a quarter of it [6].

Night work has its own costs, and the incentive list is an honest inventory of them: quiet pallet trucks and backup alarms, plus security retrofits so a driver can leave goods unattended [1][3]. The payments are one-time, and the pledge is a year of off-hour deliveries plus data sharing with the city [3].

The carbon gain is real too. A 2023 study puts daytime truck emissions 15% to 18% above the night hours, when traffic eases [2]. That is a traffic effect: the same truck spending less of its day idling. A carbon ledger that counts gallons per mile will not show it.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** New York City is paying businesses, out of toll revenue, to move deliveries into the 7 p.m. to 6 a.m. window — up to $5 million, with more than half a million dollars going to as many as 14 businesses in the first phase [1][3].
- **Why It Matters:** The saving the program buys is measured in time — 35% to 45% off carrier operating costs on a converted route, $30,000 to $50,000 a year — while the cost squeezing small fleets is measured in gallons: $6.199 nationally and $8.09 in California, with surcharges covering only 70% to 75% of it [4][5][6].
- **What I'd Watch:** How the second phase spends its money, and whether the fuel line moves into the next round of contracts instead of staying with the operator.
  - **Off-hour deliveries:** moving a route into the night, when streets are clear, so a driver covers more stops in one shift — that is the source of the documented saving [2][5].
  - **Fuel hedging:** a price lock on future diesel, which settles your cost without changing what the truck burns [7].
  - **Mode and depot swaps:** drones and electric yard tractors take the road and the diesel out of the job rather than rescheduling them [8][9].
- **The Catch:** The payments are one-time, night work brings noise, security and staffing costs the incentives only partly cover, and the same truck still burns the same diesel per mile — the hour changes the route, not the fuel [1][3].
