<!-- lead -->
The shares you have held for more than a year can leave your account as a charitable gift at their full market price, and the gain inside them never becomes income. On a $150,000 gift of employer stock that cost you $40,000, that is $110,000 of gain the tax code never sees. At the top long-term rate plus the 3.8 percent surtax on investment income, it is $26,180 you do not pay [1][5][6]. A check for the same amount cannot match it, because writing that check means selling the shares first and paying the tax on the way out.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

What strikes me is how much of the advantage rests on one label. The code calls a gift "capital gain property" when the asset's sale at market value would have produced long-term gain, and for a vested share that means a holding period longer than one year [3][4]. Give a lot younger than that and the deduction falls back to what you paid, because the statute removes the gain that would have been short-term [3].

Clear that line and the ceiling becomes a share of income rather than a dollar cap. A gift of such shares to a public charity counts only up to 30 percent of your contribution base, which the statute defines as adjusted gross income without a loss carryback [3]. The rest is not lost, which is the part most holders never hear: the excess is treated as a gift made in each of the five years that follow, in order of time [3].

The rule behind those limits does the same sum with dollar figures. Its example allows $6,000 of capital gain property against a $20,000 contribution base, which is the 30 percent ceiling applied to a real number [2]. I read that example twice, because it is the only place in the rulebook where the share turns into money.

## By the numbers

- **30 percent — Ceiling on the year:** a gift of shares to a public charity counts up to 30 percent of adjusted gross income, so a $400,000 base carries $120,000 of gift value this year [3].
- **$30,000 — The spillover:** the amount above that ceiling is not lost; it is treated as a gift made in each of the five following years [3].
- **$22,000 — What the 50 percent box leaves:** Publication 526's example gives land worth $28,000 with a basis of $22,000, and making the election reduces the deduction to that basis [1].

## What I'd watch:

The lots that qualify are the ones held longest, and in a grant that vests over four years those are usually the lots with the smallest cost basis. So the gift takes out the most embedded gain per share, which is a sum rather than a strategy: the same dollar of deduction removes a larger untaxed gain from the oldest lot [3][4]. What I keep circling is how rarely that ordering gets chosen on purpose.

- **The transfer happens in kind:** the shares move to the charity without being sold first, and that is what keeps the gain unrealized [1].
- **The paperwork is the short form:** publicly traded securities go on Section A of Form 8283 once the year's noncash total passes $500, not into the appraisal section [1].
- **The year is the year of delivery:** the deduction belongs to the year the property is actually handed over, which for most holders means the decision lands in the last weeks of December [1].

Before any of that matters, the position itself has to be worth shrinking, and [a hold-versus-sell model](/calculators/rsu-hold-vs-sell/) puts the concentration and the vest schedule in one place.

## The catch

Where I've landed is that the advantage is real, bounded, and easy to lose to a form. Three limits sit under it.

- **A floor sits in the same paragraph.** The code text also carries a 0.5 percent floor: the deduction is allowed only to the extent the year's gifts exceed half a percent of that base [3]. This piece does not compute it, and it trims the first dollars off every gift.
- **A loser is the wrong gift.** Where the shares are worth less than you paid, the deduction is limited to market value and the fall in price cannot be deducted at all [1]. Selling and giving the cash is the route that keeps that loss usable, because the gift route does not.
- **An overstatement is priced.** Claim 150 percent or more of the correct value and the penalty is 20 percent of the tax you underpaid, once that shortfall passes $5,000 [1].

And the deduction is not a refund. What it is worth depends on the rate you pay, which none of these sources sets for you.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** Employer shares can leave your account as a gift at full market value, so the gain inside them is never taxed, which cash cannot do.
**The number:** 30 percent of adjusted gross income caps the year's deduction for shares, and the excess waits up to five years.
**The risk:** Shares held a year or less deduct at cost, and the box that lifts the ceiling cuts the deduction back to basis.
