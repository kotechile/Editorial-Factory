A public adjuster's fee comes out of the check your insurer pays, not out of the extra dollars the adjuster wins [1]. So a 20 percent deal only pays for itself once the offer grows by 25 percent, a gap the contract hides. Florida caps that share at 20 percent, New York at 12.5, and the trade body that tracks claims says adjusters charge up to 15 [1][2][4]. The cap is not the whole story, because your deductible comes off the check before any share applies [1].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Public adjusting is a licensed trade with one job: argue a loss up with the insurer that owes it. The owner pays the adjuster direct, which is why Florida, New York and Louisiana each put a number in law [1][2][3].

The part I keep circling is how differently the three caps treat the base. Florida sets a ladder, and the rung you land on turns on two facts: how fast the insurer paid, and whether the Governor declared a disaster. New York treats the fee as one set rate, with a narrow break for claims reopened later. Louisiana kills the conflict instead of capping it, paying the adjuster by the hour when the insurer moves fast [1][2][3].

| | Cap | What it is charged on | Timing rules |
|---|---|---|---|
| Florida | 20 percent, and 10 percent for claims from an event under a Governor's emergency order during the year after it | Claim payments to the owner, less lawyer fees and costs, and less the deductible | One percent where the insurer pays the policy limit and does so within 14 days of the loss or 10 days of the contract; nothing at all on a part of the claim paid or promised before the contract was signed |
| New York | 12.5 percent | The money paid out, for work done | Up to 20 percent on a claim reopened later, but only while the whole fee stays at or below 12.5 percent of the full payment |
| Louisiana | A share of each payment, under a written fee deal | Each check the insurer issues | Where the insurer pays or promises in writing to pay the policy limit within seventy-two hours of the loss being reported, the adjuster is paid for time and costs rather than a share |

Two details in the Florida text matter more than the cap. The fee can't rest on the deductible part of a claim. It also can't rest on hotel or rental money unless the owner signs a separate form [1]. Both rules shrink the base the share is counted on.

## By the numbers

- **25 percent — the lift a fifth of the payout needs:** an owner who keeps 80 cents of each dollar needs the offer to hit 1.25 times the old one before the fee pays for itself [1].
- **$4,012 — the fee on an average claim:** a fifth of the $20,062 average paid per home claim in 2023, from the trade body's loss data [1][5].
- **$2,508 — the same claim at the New York cap:** that state's rate on the same average claim, about $1,500 less to the adjuster [2][5].
- **14.3 percent — the lift at that cap:** one divided by 0.875 is 1.1428, so the offer must grow by about a seventh [2].

## What I'd watch:

Three clauses decide what the contract is worth, and only one of them is the rate.

- **The base.** A fee on money the insurer was always going to pay is a tax on your own claim, so the first question is what the adjuster counts as his share [1].
- **Whether an offer already exists.** Florida bars a fee on any part of the claim the insurer had already paid or promised before the contract date, so an adjuster who shows up after the first quote can't bill for money already on its way [1].
- **The exit.** A no-questions cancel right runs for 10 days after signing, and a written quote that fails to arrive within 60 days lets the owner cancel at no cost [1].
- **The deductible side of the same page.** The deductible sets what the insurer pays before any cap applies, and it is worth pricing beside the fee; that break-even sits in [the deductible half of the declarations page](/your-5-deductible-risks-your-claim/).

## The catch

My read is that the caps are fair and the sums still favor one side: the adjuster gets paid whether or not his work moved the number.

The break-even assumes the lift is real and down to the adjuster. None of the three states prints a figure for the average lift a public adjuster wins on a home claim, and no source behind this piece puts a number on it [1][2][3]. So your own first offer is the only start point the sum can run against.

A policy-limit payout is the clearest case. When the insurer has already promised the full limit there is nothing to argue up, which is why Florida drops the fee to one percent and Louisiana pays for time only [1][3]. Those rungs exist because a share deal would otherwise charge well for a payout that was never in doubt.

Timing decides which rule applies at all. Louisiana counts from the moment the loss is reported, so the same contract can pay hourly on one claim and a share on the next, based on whether the insurer moved inside that window [3].

What none of this measures is a denied claim being reopened. A claim closed with no pay is where a share deal is easiest to defend, because the other choice is nothing rather than a smaller check.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The fee is a share of the insurer's check, not of the extra money an adjuster wins, so the deal is priced against the whole payout.
- **Why It Matters:** At a normal cut the payout has to grow by more than the fee itself before the owner is made whole, and the deductible and the hotel money sit outside the base.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The base:** what the share is counted on, and whether the deductible and living costs are inside or outside it.
  - **The prior offer:** whether money was already paid or promised before the contract date, because one state allows no fee on it.
  - **The exit:** the cancel window and the written-quote deadline that make the contract reversible.
- **The Catch:** No printed figure measures the average lift a public adjuster wins, and the caps differ enough by state that one claim carries three prices.
