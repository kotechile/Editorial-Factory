Elect the foreign earned income exclusion and the tax you paid abroad on the excluded pay stops counting. The Internal Revenue Service says it in one line: once you elect the exclusion, you can't take a foreign tax credit or deduction for foreign taxes on income you choose to exclude [6]. So the exclusion is a swap, not a gift, and the price is set by how much of your pay the cap covers. For 2025 that cap is $130,000 [2]. It moves each year because the law starts at $80,000 and indexes the figure [1]. The credit it pushes aside has no dollar ceiling, carries unused amounts forward ten years [4], and can still be picked on a return filed years ago [5]. That gap is the whole decision.

## The big picture:

The two reliefs get sold as a choice, and they are not the same kind of tool. The exclusion is an amount: a slice of pay abroad removed from the return, with a housing add-on bolted to it. The credit is a price rule: a dollar-for-dollar offset, limited by the US tax on your foreign-source income, with a ten-year shelf life for the part you cannot use this year [3][4].

My read is that most people abroad take the exclusion because it is the one the internet explains, and Form 2555 looks like a receipt next to the credit's sums. That is a real gain, and it is not the one that shows up in the math.

The math is a comparison of two rates. If your host country taxes the excluded slice at a higher rate than the US would have taxed it, the exclusion throws away a credit worth more than the saving it delivers. If the host country taxes that slice at a lower rate, the credit was close to worthless and the exclusion wins outright. The cap decides how much of the pay sits on the losing side of that test, which is why one election is fine for a contractor in a low-tax country and costly for a salaried executive in a high-tax one.

What the tidy version drops is a sentence that changes the conclusion. The two reliefs can run on different slices at once. A high earner may exclude up to the year's limit and then credit the foreign taxes paid on the part of the pay that was not excluded [6]. So the exclusion does not kill the credit outright, only on the income it covers, which for anyone earning near the cap is most of the pay.

The timing is the other half. Credit you cannot use is not lost when the year ends; it goes back one year and forward ten, applied to the earliest year first, and that span cannot be extended [3][4]. An exclusion carries nowhere. The choice itself also stays open longer than most filers assume, which is the one piece of good news here [5].

## By the numbers

- **$130,000 — 2025 exclusion cap:** The top foreign earned income exclusion on the 2025 return, up on the prior year because the law's $80,000 base is indexed to inflation [1][2].
- **$39,000 and $106.85 — 2025 housing limit:** The housing relief is capped at 30% of the exclusion, so $39,000 for a full qualifying year or $106.85 per day for a partial one [2].
- **10 years — credit shelf life:** Unused foreign tax goes back one year and forward ten, earliest year first, and the span cannot be extended even when one of those years has no credit to use [3][4].
- **$300 — small-claim forfeit:** Foreign taxes of $300 or less, $600 on a joint return, can be claimed on a short form instead of the full one, but that election bars moving that year's tax to any other year [3].

## What I'd watch:

- **The amended-return route.** Publication 514's own case is a filer who deducted 2014 foreign taxes and then, in February 2025, filed an amended 2014 return taking the credit instead, a move the publication calls timely because it fell inside the window [5]. That window runs ten years from the regular due date of the return, and it works both ways between credit and deduction [5].
- **The stacking pattern.** The high-earner route in Publication 54 is spelled out: exclude up to the limit, then credit the foreign tax on the pay above it [6]. An exclusion-only filer never sees that mix, and it needs no revoked election to use.
- **The revocation lock.** Revoking is easy; coming back is not. A filer who revokes and then wants the same exclusion again inside five years has to ask the tax authority for a ruling first [6].
- **The short-form tail.** The simpler election is fine while the foreign tax is small, but the same paragraph that allows it blocks that year's tax from moving to any other year, even if it later proves worth carrying [3].

## The catch

The exclusion's ease is worth real money, and I would not pretend otherwise. It needs no foreign-source sums, no split categories and no apportioned costs, while the credit needs all three before it yields a number [3]. A filer whose foreign tax is small, or whose host country levies no income tax, gets next to nothing from the credit and a clean, if smaller, gain from the exclusion.

Some costs are tied together and never show up in the side-by-side. Electing the exclusion rules out the additional child tax credit for that year [6], and the housing relief only arrives attached to the exclusion elections, so the $39,000 add-on cannot be taken on its own [2].

The honest limit is that the sum is an estimate. Rates change, currencies move, and the foreign tax on an excluded slice usually has to be split rather than read off a payslip. Where the two tools come out close, the tiebreaker is not the math but the paperwork you are willing to defend years later.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The foreign earned income exclusion reads less like relief and more like a trade. It strips a capped slice of pay from the return, $130,000 for 2025, and in return gives up the foreign tax credit on exactly that slice.
- **Why It Matters:** The credit has no dollar ceiling and unused amounts last ten years, so an exclusion taken in a high-tax country can cost more than the tax it saves, and the loss stays invisible on the return that reports it.
- **What I'd Watch:** The rules that decide the outcome for a filer already abroad.
  - **The amended-return window:** Ten years from the regular due date to pick the credit instead of the deduction, which is how an old exclusion year gets a second look.
  - **The stacking route:** Excluding up to the cap and crediting the foreign tax on the pay above it, which keeps both reliefs working on different slices.
  - **The revocation lock:** Five years and a ruling request before a revoked exclusion can be elected again.
- **The Catch:** The credit needs foreign-source sums and separate categories that the exclusion skips, and electing the exclusion also rules out the additional child tax credit for that year. In a low-tax host country the exclusion is usually the better tool.
