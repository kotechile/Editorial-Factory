<!-- lead -->
Walmart's CFO dropped a $2.9 billion detail onto an August 20 earnings call. The retailer was eligible for a refund on IEEPA tariffs the Supreme Court struck down in February, and "substantially all" of that cash had already landed. Yet when Calcbench ran the numbers a week later, the $2.9 billion figure appeared nowhere in Walmart's SEC filings or investor deck. It existed entirely as spoken audio. [3]

Across 101 public companies, a quiet windfall is materializing. Importers disclosed $18.7 billion in gross refunds by late August, with the eleven largest claiming roughly $12 billion of the total. [3]

<!-- tension -->
The Supreme Court's February 2026 ruling in Learning Resources v. Trump voided the "Liberation Day" and fentanyl-related levies, stripping the president's authority to impose tariffs under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. [1] But a legal victory does not instantly wire cash. Customs and Border Protection collected roughly $166 billion in IEEPA duties before the court intervened. [2] By late June, CBP's new CAPE system had accepted $104 billion for processing and sent $71 billion to Treasury for disbursement. [2] Penn Wharton estimates the final clawback could hit $175 billion. [6]

This capital is bypassing smaller players and landing on the balance sheets of massive importers. Caterpillar padded its second-quarter operating profit with $392 million in expected IEEPA recoveries. [5] Deere recognized $382 million through its fiscal third quarter, which ended Aug. 2. [4] CBP is prioritizing unliquidated and reconciliation entries, which together represent about $130 billion of the total. [2] Companies large enough to file protective actions and push their entries through Phases I and II of the rollout are getting paid now.

Smaller importers face a brick wall. Phase III covers finally-liquidated entries whose protest windows have closed. CBP has not scheduled this phase. At a June hearing, the agency indicated only importers of record who filed protective actions before the Court of International Trade will see a dime. [2] The Justice Department is appealing the universal-refund order to restrict payouts to parties that sued. [2] If you paid the duty and skipped the protective action, your refund might never materialize.

<!-- tactical-insight -->
Three moves for an ops leader this quarter.

First, target your vendors' tariff windfalls for immediate price concessions. Large suppliers are booking these refunds as non-recurring add-backs, masking them behind record margins. Caterpillar recovered $392 million in the quarter. [5] Deere still models about $1.1 billion in fiscal-2026 direct tariff expense — a cost $382 million in refunds cushions but does not erase. [4] When a major supplier reports an unexpected margin bump, isolate the tariff-refund portion before accepting baseline pricing for next quarter. If they secured a Phase I or II refund on materials they sold you, that money is leverage to claw back pass-through you already paid. Demand your share on your next sourcing call.

Second, grade your own refund exposure now, before Phase III opens. If you are the importer of record on any IEEPA-paid entry, find out whether it was unliquidated (Phase I) or a reconciliation entry (Phase II) — those are the ones already moving. If it is finally liquidated and you never filed a protective suit, you are currently outside the process, and the clock is bounded by a two-year statute of limitations whose start date nobody has settled. [2] That is a call to your customs broker, not a quarter-end scramble.

Third, read "record margin" skeptically. Because these recoveries are so often booked as one-time add-backs, they are easy to miss: Walmart's single largest figure was only spoken on a call, Apple's roughly $2.19 billion showed up through gross margin, and several companies tucked the number into a non-GAAP reconciliation rather than a headline. [3] Before you extrapolate a supplier's margin into next quarter, ask what portion was tariff-refund one-timing.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
A one-time cash injection does not alter your structural supply chain costs. The Supreme Court erased the IEEPA levies, but Section 232 duties on steel and aluminum and Section 301 duties on Chinese goods remain entirely intact. [2] Deere still models about $1.1 billion in direct tariff expense for fiscal 2026. [4] Massive OEMs and retailers are pocketing this year's refunds while the underlying tariff architecture stays firmly in place. Treat the clawback as a temporary reconciliation window. It repairs cash flow for a single quarter, but it offers zero relief from the permanent cost of moving goods across borders.
