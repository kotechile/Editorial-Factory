<!-- lead -->
Heating oil sold for $6.19 a gallon in the week of October 19, up from $3.53 a year earlier [1]. The federal answer to that price is 315,000 barrels lent from the Northeast emergency reserve, about one day of what oil-heated homes burn in a winter [3]. The trap sits on the other side: the way out of the fuel, a heat pump and the work around it, also got more expensive this month [4].

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

- **The shift:** Heating oil reached $6.19 a gallon.
- **The number:** The season would cost $2,488 at that price on the government's 402-gallon estimate.
- **The risk:** Install materials rose 3% to 15% while reserve barrels go to suppliers.

<!-- tension -->
## Why the cheap answer got dearer

The Energy Information Administration (EIA), the federal energy statistics agency, published its winter outlook on October 6 [2]. It put heating oil at $5.26 a gallon for the season, or $2,115 for a household burning 402 gallons [2]. The Department of Energy then set an October 27 bid deadline and a November 13 delivery deadline for the reserve loan [3]. I read the weekly table twice before I trusted the gap: actual prices ran $6.05, $6.14, and $6.19 through October, above the $5.79 the agency expected for November [1].

The reason sits in the fuel. Heating oil is the same refined product as diesel, and distillate has been the tightest part of the crude barrel all year [2]. Refining slowed, imports cost more, and stockpiles are thin going into winter [2]. The Northeast also has little pipeline capacity, so cheap natural gas cannot supplant dear oil there [2]. That leaves a local price set by the world market and moved through limited delivery capacity.

The Department of Energy offered fuel companies up to 315,000 barrels from sites in South Portland, Maine; Boston, Massachusetts; and New Haven, Connecticut [3]. Borrowers return the same volume plus a fee before the winter of 2027–28 [3]. That is a loan to suppliers, not a price cap for households.

The other option is to stop buying the fuel. A heat pump conversion needs materials—duct, gas piping, dampers, filters, and a tankless water heater—and those materials rose 3% to 15% at twelve manufacturers this month [4]. Most disclosed increases sit between 3% and 8%, and two more take effect later in November [4].

|---|---|---|---|

| Switch to a heat pump | No published installed price | Parts up 3% to 15% [4] | Panel, contractor and timing |

## By the numbers

- **39.6 million barrels:** Oil-heated homes burn that much over five winter months [2].
- **4.13 million households:** The group that heats mainly with oil, most of them in the Northeast [2].

## What I'd watch

The first test is the weekly price. A loan of 315,000 barrels against 262,000 barrels burned each day is a small test of a large price, and the weekly survey should settle it within a month [1]. If the price holds near $6, the release changed the mood rather than the supply [1].

Two things sit behind that delivery date. Borrowers owe the fuel back with a fee before the next winter, which leaves the reserve thinner for 2027 [3]. The agency's next outlook will put fresh prices next to the October ones [2].

What I keep circling is how little the replacement market has moved. Oil warm-air furnace shipments rose 27.1% in August to 3,052 units, a six-month streak, while gas warm-air furnaces fell 4.3% [5]. The trade press cautions that a base that small says little about the country [5]. The detail fits a narrower story: households replacing the box they already own, not a national turn back to oil.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch

The price can fall as fast as it rose. Heating oil tracks diesel. The EIA warns that switching to a cheaper fuel "would not necessarily result in lower expenditures for their household", because houses and equipment differ from any average [2].

The reserve carries a return obligation, not a price cap. What I would want before comparing is the number no agency prints: the installed cost of a heat pump in a given house.
