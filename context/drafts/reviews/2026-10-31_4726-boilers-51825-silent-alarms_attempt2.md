On 2026-10-29, the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) recalled 4,726 gas boilers in the United States and 2,261 in Canada. A cracked component can allow unburned gas to pool inside a sealed cabinet until the front cover blows off. That exact same day, the agency warned that 51,825 combination smoke and carbon monoxide (CO) alarms sold online might never trigger. The joint recall notice tells anyone still running the boiler to keep a working smoke and carbon monoxide alarm in the house until a technician fits the repair kit [1][2]. Read side by side, the two documents describe a grim loop. An appliance can fill its own cabinet with gas, and a batch of silent electronics was sold to stand guard over it.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

An alarm is a measuring instrument, yet most households treat it like a permanent fixture. The plastic shell carries a formal standard number — Underwriters Laboratories (UL) 2034 for carbon monoxide and UL 217 for smoke — but the sensor inside is a component the standard classifies as limited-life [5]. Underwriters Laboratories began requiring an end-of-life signal in 2009 for a plain reason. The sensor wears out while the outer casing still looks pristine.

Manufacturers disagree on how long that takes, which is a warning sign of its own. Kidde puts its alarms at seven to ten years, while First Alert says five to seven and that a unit at the end of that window should be replaced [5][9]. Kidde states bluntly that once an alarm reaches the end of its life, it cannot detect carbon monoxide at all. 

I read the dual filings three times to grasp the timing of these twin failures. The agency needed an alarm to stand in for a boiler it could not fix on the spot. At the exact same time, it warned that a huge share of the alarms people bought for that job might stay quiet [2][3]. That is the exact same blind spot an imager suffers when it scans a wall's surface instead of the void behind it — [what a cheap imager can and cannot prove](/your-250-imager-needs-15-degrees/).

## By the numbers

- **34 — Cracked condensate dishes:** the total known part failures by 2026-10-16, resulting in one reported injury in Canada and one incident without injury in the United States [2].
- **7 — Reports of silence:** the number of times the recalled alarms failed to alert users to the presence of smoke or fire [3].
- **$11 to $107 — Sold price band:** the range those combination alarms commanded on Amazon and eBay between November 2023 and October 2026 [3].
- **36.4% to 50% — Homes with a CO alarm:** the spread CPSC published in a March 2014 lab report, which pulled from surveys conducted in 2011 and 2009 [7].

## What I'd watch:

The boiler hazard runs on a strict clock. The recalled units went into homes from May 2021 to August 2025, the fix is an upgrade kit fitted by a certified technician, and heating season has already begun [1][2]. One of the reported injuries happened to a professional servicing a unit, who sustained a broken jaw according to the trade press [8].

- **Where the repair queue lands:** A free kit still has to be booked. Until it is, the recall notice says the boiler that keeps running needs a working alarm beside it.
- **What the second warning covers:** A separate same-day warning for model YHW-542COM-R publishes no unit count, so its reach remains unknown. Those units sold from September 2025 through June 2026 at $18 to $168, generating two reports of silence [4].
- **Whether the warned models keep selling:** CPSC says not to sell or give the units away, but both models moved through massive open marketplaces [3][4].
- **What the box says:** The agency advises buying only units that meet UL 2034 and UL 217, which is printed on the package rather than proved by the price tag [3].

My main concern for homeowners is the false confidence built into the monthly test routine [6]. Pressing the test button verifies the electronics and the sounder, but it never puts carbon monoxide in front of the sensor.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

The documented fault in both warnings involves smoke and fire, rather than carbon monoxide. CPSC writes that model JZA-540COM and the second model "fail to alarm in the presence of hazardous smoke and fire" [3][4]. Neither notice says the CO side was tested and failed. That gap matters, because the quickest assumption is that CO sensing is broken across a whole category of imports.

The scale of the broader problem is softer than it looks, too. The working estimate that 36.4 to 50 percent of homes contain a CO alarm relies on old surveys taken in 2011 and 2009, which were bundled into a lab report published in March 2014 [7]. I suspect the market has shifted toward better coverage since those surveys, making this a slow measurement gap rather than a fresh collapse.

A certified alarm is not an absolute guarantee either. CPSC's own chamber tests found units firing outside the required time windows in some cases, which is a gap a standard tries to limit rather than remove [7]. The recall and the warnings describe the outer tails of product failure, and those tails are where the true danger sits.

**The shift:** A safety agency leaned on home alarms to cover a boiler it could not fix that day, while warning that 51,825 combination alarms might never sound.
