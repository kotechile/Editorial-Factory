Four robot mower models hold the only federal approval granted to a consumer yard machine for the 2027 American market. All four belong to a single manufacturer. Husqvarna lost 334 million krona last quarter, following a July decision that placed foreign-made robots on a national security list. Your next automated mower requires one of those specific approvals before a store can legally sell it. That government register is public, and right now, it offers no alternatives.

## The big picture:

Husqvarna sells machines into two very different markets, making its latest earnings report read like a split screen. Group sales dropped 5 percent organically in the quarter, and the forest and garden division fell 10 percent [1]. The company blamed the mowing weakness on bad weather, elevated dealer stock in Europe, and competitive pressure [1]. Executives answered with targeted price increases, which is a standard response when a market leader feels squeezed [1].

America presents a distinct regulatory landscape. On 28 July, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) added foreign-made advanced robotic devices to a list of equipment blocked from required sales approvals [2][3]. A manufacturer escapes this list only by securing a conditional approval from the Department of War, which the communications agency then publishes [2]. The rule catches any moving machine over 4.4 pounds that features a sensor and software to steer it [3]. The device must also carry a network link of at least 200 kilobits a second [3].

The published register lists seven robot approvals in total, with the rest governing floor cleaners and factory vehicles. Only one entry covers a yard machine: Husqvarna's 305v iQ, 310v iQ, 420v iQ and 440v iQ, cleared on 9 September for a 2027 launch in the US [2][4]. Four of those seven approvals arrived on or after 16 October, showing a fast-moving queue that bypasses the garden aisle entirely. I view this timing as a massive stroke of luck. Just as the manufacturer struggled to maintain pricing in Europe, its four platforms became the only legal yard options in America.

## By the numbers

- **334 million krona — Quarterly loss:** the group operating result fell to a loss of 334 million krona from an income of 141 million a year earlier, representing a -3.7 percent margin against 1.5 percent [1].
- **4 of 7 — Yard machines on the register:** the federal agency lists seven robot approvals, and the four Husqvarna mowers are the only consumer yard items among them [2].
- **65 percent — US content bar:** a mower avoids the list if it counts as US-made, meaning domestic parts exceed 65 percent of cost through 2028 and 75 percent from 2029 [3].
- **$800 to $2,500 — What buyers pay:** the automated mowers in Consumer Reports' current ratings run from about $800 to more than $2,500, compared to $400 to $800 for its top battery walk-behind mowers [5].

## What I'd watch:

The regulatory register updates through public notices, making the next entry the vital signal for buyers. An approval for a second mower brand would mean the 2027 season brings genuine choice rather than four variations of a single design. Until a rival name appears on that list, it cannot appear on a store shelf [2]. A market restricted to one approved supplier gives that incumbent tremendous power to hold a premium price, while older inventory approved before 28 July handles the discount demand [1][3][5]. The federal agency can also revoke older clearances, a tool it used in June 2026, adding another layer of risk to existing stock [2].

This dynamic does not signal the end of the incumbent's broader business. The corporate group still expects 3 billion krona of gross savings by the end of 2028, with about 935 million booked so far [1]. America is already the region where the company sells the most professional robot mowers [4]. It puts machines on more than 500 golf courses and booked 24 percent of group sales there in 2025 [4]. My focus is entirely on the consumer queue: whether a competing brand ever breaks the current monopoly on that federal list.

## The catch

Consumer Reports told its readers the government banned the sale of foreign-made robots, though it correctly noted the restriction spares mowers already owned or approved [5]. The federal agency uses narrower language: new models face a block, but previously approved models keep flowing to hardware stores [3]. If you already own an automated mower, or buy a current model this season, none of this friction reaches your lawn.

The sudden scarcity applies solely to next year's models. I keep coming back to the invisible side of the federal register: it publishes approvals rather than applications, leaving us blind to how many competitors asked and failed. The expectation that a price war will stall relies entirely on a public list showing one approved yard machine today. A competitor could secure clearance tomorrow, altering the market before the spring buying season begins. The rated acreage on a spec sheet is already a best-case figure, and [the gap between rated and usable area](/your-1-acre-mowers-04-acre-trap/) is the other place a buyer's math goes wrong.

## At a glance

**The shift:** New foreign-made robot mower models require explicit US approval, and the public register currently holds just one consumer yard brand.
**The number:** The government granted seven approvals, four of them since 16 October, featuring only Husqvarna's four mowers.
**The risk:** Waiting for a cheaper 2027 model means betting on a regulatory queue you cannot see.
