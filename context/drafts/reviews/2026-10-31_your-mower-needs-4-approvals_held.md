<!-- lead -->
Four robot yard models hold the only US government pass granted for the 2027 home market, and all four belong to one company. Husqvarna lost 334 million krona last quarter, after robots built abroad went onto a national-security list in July, and your next mower needs one of those clearances before a store can sell it. That public list shows exactly who gets to play.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The Swedish company sells into both markets, making its latest earnings read like a split screen. Sales fell 5 percent organically in the quarter, and the forest and garden arm dropped 10 percent [1]. The group blamed bad weather, tough rivals, and dealers holding far too much stock across Europe, answering with targeted price hikes—the classic, predictable defense of a market leader suddenly squeezed on cost [1].

The regulatory picture in America looks completely different right now. On 28 July the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) put "advanced robotic devices" built abroad on a list of gear banned from sale unless they secure an exemption [2][3]. A maker can escape that block by securing a conditional pass from the Department of War, and the agency posts each one it grants [2]. The rule catches any moving yard helper over 4.4 pounds, provided it carries a perception sensor, a network link of at least 200 kilobits a second, and the software that steers it [3]. New designs are blocked, while models cleared before the change keep selling [3].

I read the table three times. It lists seven robot clearances, and just one single entry covers the yard: Husqvarna's 305v iQ, 310v iQ, 420v iQ and 440v iQ, granted on 9 September for a 2027 launch in the US market [2][4]. The rest of the items are floor cleaners and factory carts. Four of the seven arrived on or after 16 October. The line moves quickly, but it just bypasses the garden aisle. In the very month the leader admitted it cannot hold prices down in Europe, its four platforms stood alone with a pass in America.

## By the numbers

- **334 million krona — Quarterly loss:** the group operating result fell to a loss of 334 million krona from income of 141 million a year earlier, a -3.7 percent margin against 1.5 percent [1].
- **4 of 7 — Yard models cleared:** the FCC lists seven robot passes, and the four Swedish mowers are the only home garden gear among them [2].
- **65 percent — US content bar:** a mower dodges the list if it counts as domestic, meaning local parts above 65 percent of cost through 2028 and 75 percent from 2029 [3].
- **$800 to $2,500 — What buyers pay:** the mowers in Consumer Reports' current ratings run from about $800 to more than $2,500, against $400 to $800 for its top battery walk-behind mowers [5].

## What I'd watch:

The public record updates by notice, making the next entry vital. A nod for a second brand would mean 2027 brings a genuine choice for buyers, rather than four takes on a single design.

- **The next name:** a new brand appears here before hitting stores [2].
- **A premium:** a market with one supplier is the easiest place to hold prices [1].
- **Stock of older models:** everything cleared before 28 July can still be legally imported and sold, which is exactly where this season's retail deals sit right now [3][5].
- **Any move against old passes:** the agency can also halt the import of gear it already cleared, using a formal process written into its own rules and deployed in June 2026 to trap older covered gear [2].

The market leader is hardly finished. The group still expects 3 billion krona of gross savings by the end of 2028, with about 935 million booked so far [1]. America is also where it already sells the most professional robot mowers, with more than 500 golf courses on its platforms and 24 percent of group sales booked there in 2025 [4]. What I am watching is narrower: whether a rival ever joins a list that currently holds one name.

## The catch

Consumer Reports tells readers the government "banned the sale" of robots built abroad, then adds that the ban skips mowers already owned or already cleared [5]. The agency uses narrower words, clarifying that new models cannot get a pass while older models keep flowing to stores [3]. If you own one today, none of this reaches you.

The shortage sits in next year's market. My reading rests heavily on what this document leaves out: it records passes, not applications, so nobody outside the agency knows how many rivals asked and were turned down. Because no source in this run publishes a rival price, the claim that the price war will fail to arrive is a judgment about a queue, not a hard forecast. The rated acreage on a spec sheet is already a best-case figure, making [the gap between rated and usable area](/your-1-acre-mowers-04-acre-trap/) the other obvious place a buyer's math goes completely wrong.

## At a glance

**The shift:** New robot mowers built abroad need their own US pass, and the public record holds just one home yard brand.
**The number:** Seven passes granted, four of them since 16 October, and only the four Swedish mowers.
**The risk:** Waiting for a cheaper 2027 model is a bet on a queue you cannot see.
