The engine maker prints the bill before you ever add it up. Briggs & Stratton, whose small engines sit under most walk-behind mowers, asks for an oil change after the first 5 hours of use, then every 50 hours or once a year, whichever comes first [1]. A Honda dealer's storage guide says fuel left in the tank cannot stay there past 90 days of inactivity, and anything between 30 and 90 days needs stabilizer and treated gasoline [2]. That is the schedule a gas mower hands you before it cuts a blade of grass. The battery mower it competes against has no oil, no fuel, and no page — Consumer Reports' testers measured the class average run time at 45 to 50 minutes per charge, enough for a quarter acre [3]. The trade is a 45-minute ceiling against a service schedule that never reaches zero.

## At a glance

This article compares the upkeep a gas walk-behind mower demands against the cordless mowers that can finish the same lawn on one charge. For homeowners weighing the switch, the finding is that the gas machine's oil and fuel schedule is the real cost the battery mower removes.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture: the schedule you inherited

The 50-hour number is not editorial advice — it is the engine maker's own service interval, printed on the maintenance page. Briggs & Stratton asks for the first oil change after 5 hours of use, then annually or every 50 hours, whichever arrives first, and the same page notes that air-cooled engines burn about an ounce of oil per cylinder per hour [1]. On a suburban lot the machine runs 20 to 30 hours a season, so the binding clause is "annually": the calendar, not the hour meter, triggers the work. Oil, a spark plug, an air filter and stabilizer land in one spring box, on the same Saturday the machine refuses to start.

The fuel rule is the part nobody reads until the damage is done. Nichols Equipment, a Honda outdoor-power dealer, splits storage into windows: 30 to 90 days means adding stabilizer to fresh gasoline and running the engine for 10 minutes so the treated fuel reaches the carburetor; past 90 days, the fuel system must be emptied, tank included [2]. The guide is explicit that a stabilizer will not revive fuel that has already gone stale [2]. I read that sentence twice, because it inverts the usual assumption: the product sold as the fix only works as a preventive. The spring no-start is cheap to prevent and expensive to discover, because the fuel was aging on a calendar the owner never saw.

## By the numbers

- Briggs & Stratton sets the oil-change interval at 50 hours of use or once a year, whichever comes first, after a first change at 5 hours [1].
- Gasoline may not sit in a stored mower past 90 days of inactivity [2].
- Battery walk-behinds in Consumer Reports' tests averaged 45 to 50 minutes of run time per charge [3].

## What I'd watch: what the cordless actually removes

Buyers price the wrong side of this comparison. The usual framing is what the cordless gives up — run time, peak torque, the ability to refill from a jerry can. The gas machine's price of admission is paid after the sale, on a schedule: oil every 50 hours or every year, an air filter, a spark plug, and fuel discipline inside the 90-day window [1][2]. The battery mower's price is paid in planning instead. Consumer Reports' testers found the class averaging 45 to 50 minutes per charge, with two-thirds of the electric walk-behinds they rated able to finish a quarter acre on one charge [3]. A 7.5 amp-hour pack stores 420 watt-hours of that work [5]. If your lot fits inside one charge, the maintenance line is gone: no oil to change, no fuel to rotate, no spring Saturday owed to a carburetor.

The running cost makes the contrast explicit. A quarter acre takes roughly 40 to 50 minutes of cutting, and the U.S. grid averages 0.81 pounds of carbon dioxide per kilowatt-hour, so a season of charges adds up to a few pounds of CO2 and a few dollars at retail rates [4]. One bill is metered; the other is scheduled. What I am watching next is whether the class-average run time keeps creeping upward, because every added minute widens the set of lawns the schedule-free option can cover — the same arithmetic running through the rest of our [outdoor power equipment](/categories/outdoor-power-equipment/) coverage.

## The catch

The maintenance tax is small, and honesty about its size matters. A season's oil, plug, filter and stabilizer cost a few tens of dollars and one working hour, so the schedule alone does not close the price gap with a battery mower in a single year. What it changes is the character of ownership. The gas machine degrades when it is ignored — fuel first, per the 90-day rule — while the battery mower ages in its pack, losing capacity over years of use rather than weeks of neglect [2][3]. Consumer Reports still calls power the arena where gas is unbeatable: a cordless that stalls in tall, wet grass has not been out-maintained, it has been out-torqued [3].

So the cordless is not schedule-free so much as differently scheduled. My reading, held loosely: if your lawn fits a quarter acre and you would skip the oil change anyway, the cordless deletes a recurring chore you were failing to do. If your lot needs multiple charges or your grass runs heavy, the gas machine's upkeep is the salary you pay for its torque — and the schedule is the least of what you owe it.
