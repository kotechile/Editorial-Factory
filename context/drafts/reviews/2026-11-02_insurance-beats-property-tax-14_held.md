<!-- lead -->

Home insurance now runs 14% above the property-tax bill in two-thirds of U.S. counties, according to a Bloomberg Intelligence study published on October 19. [1] Tomorrow, voters in seven states decide 12 property-tax measures. Florida's Amendment 3 and Oklahoma's State Question 847 are the big ones. None of those ballot questions touches the insurance premium. [2] [3]

Both bills land on one household budget, but only one has kept pace with the cost of living. Property taxes rose only slightly more than inflation after 2020, the Lincoln Institute of Land Policy reported on October 21, even as home prices climbed more than 50%. [4]

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The revolt is real, and it is aimed at the slower of the two lines. Over a decade, homeowner policies are up 45% in real terms. The tax line, by contrast, has barely outrun overall prices since 2020, even as home values rose more than 50%, the Lincoln Institute reported on October 21. [1] [4]

Weather, not a vote, moved the premium: hail and winter storms are pushing rates up across Colorado, Utah and Minnesota, while in the Southeast the premium now runs 2.5 to 4 times the tax levy. That is a risk bill, and no ballot item reprices it. [1]

What the ballot offers is a transfer more than a cut. If a town shields part of one home's value, the rest of the bill moves to renters, shops and second homes, because the levy is set by the revenue the town needs. The Tax Foundation says the same about this year's batch: a higher shield or a tighter cap makes other properties carry more of the bill. [2]

Florida, too, is the cleanest test of that logic. Amendment 3 lifts the homestead exemption for non-school taxes to $150,000 next year and $250,000 in 2028, while the $25,000 school tax break stays put. A $275,000 home would owe city or county tax on only $25,000 of value. The state's current break tops out at $50,000, and Adam Langley of the Lincoln Institute puts a workable statewide figure at $20,000 to $40,000. Florida's version is odd in one way: it mandates the high figure with no state money to make towns whole. [2] [4]

I keep coming back to the mismatch: a voter can move the tax, while the premium is the line that answers to risk and has no ballot.

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## By the numbers

- **14% — Insurance over tax:** Owners pay 14% more for home insurance than for property tax at the county level; adding auto cover lifts the gap to 30%. [1]
- **75% — Counties where the pair wins:** With car cover added, the combined insurance bill tops the property tax in 75% of U.S. counties. [1]
- **79% — Nebraska's decade:** Homeowner policies there rose 79% in real terms, against 45% nationally. [1]
- **1.75% — Oklahoma's new cap:** State Question 847 cuts the yearly cap on assessed homestead value from 3% to 1.75%, and on other property from 5% to 4%. [2]

## What I'd watch:

Three things decide whether the relief shows up as money.

- **The county, not the country.** Two-thirds of counties is an average of averages. The 14% gap is small where taxes are high and wide where they are low, which is why the Southeast looks nothing like New Jersey. [1] [4]
- **The levy behind the break.** A bigger break changes who pays, not how much the town must spend. Property taxes fund 28.4% of local spending nationally—more than half in New Hampshire, Connecticut, New Jersey and Maine, and under 15% in Arkansas, Alabama and Nevada. [4]
- **The repair backlog.** A LendingTree survey published on October 26 found 34% of owners delayed a repair in the past two years. Of those still needing work, 32% cannot afford it, 23% say quotes are too high and 3% blame the difficulty of getting a loan. The median job costs $2,113 in real terms, up 76.1% since 2015. A professional job runs $4,103; the same work by the owner runs $845. [5] [6]

I want to know whether insurers credit that work, because a roof and a water shut-off are the two items that turn up in claims, and [the measured cost of one slow leak](/silent-15400-home-loss/) is $15,400.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The comparison is a county average, and averages hide the two cases that matter. In a high-tax Northeast county, the tax bill still dwarfs the insurance premium. In the Southeast, the premium can run 2.5 to 4 times the tax, and no exemption closes that gap. [1] [4]

There is also a timing problem. All 12 measures are still undecided as of today. A homestead exemption that passes in Florida does not lower a levy; it moves one. If town budgets hold, the relief for a $275,000 home arrives as a smaller share of a bill that keeps growing. The owner who expected a cut sees a smaller increase. [2] [4]

Property taxes are the one housing cost a voter can move, and 45 of 58 such measures passed from 2020 through 2025. My reading of that record is that people wanted a lever they could control more than a line item they could lower. [3]

## At a glance

**The shift:** Twelve property-tax measures reach ballots tomorrow; insurance, now the larger bill in two-thirds of counties, is on none of them.
**The number:** 14% — the average insurance premium's lead over property tax.
**The risk:** A homestead exemption moves a levy rather than cutting it.
