GXO Logistics (GXO) says its humanoid robots have moved more than 100,000 totes at a Georgia warehouse, and this week's news that two more sites will follow carried no price for a single tote. Thirteen days earlier, AutoStore began selling a generative AI tool that builds the payback case for buying its systems. That software ships alongside the vendor's own estimates for saved floor space and avoided downtime. Put the two releases together and a quiet handover emerges. The numbers your payback math rests on now come from the company selling you the machines, while the one figure that would settle the question is the exact figure the tote count skips.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture

Payback math for warehouse automation requires four distinct inputs. It requires the price of the gear, the work a site completes in a year, the cost of the labor it replaces, and the uptime the machines deliver. Vendors have always provided buyers with the first number. What changed this month is that they now sell the other three as products, built from a set of lookalike projects rather than from the concrete reality of your own building.

AutoStore's release spells out the extent of this repackaging. CubeExplore is a tool that leans on benchmarks and data from similar projects to help partners and prospective buyers build a stronger case, while CubeStudio tests layouts before anything is built. CubeFleet monitors the system to manage ongoing upkeep, and on AutoStore's own estimate, it can cut surprise downtime by 35% to 45%. FlexBinsPort is put at 10% to 15% better use of space. Each figure might be perfectly fair, yet every single one of those estimates comes straight from the side of the table that is waiting to get paid.

Pio Pal sells robotic picking to smaller firms as a lighter commitment under robots as a service (RaaS). It promises up to 540 order lines an hour at one station. That represents a gain in clarity, since a price per line is easier to check than a spreadsheet of guesses, but it also buries those same assumptions deep inside a binding contract where the buyer sees the final rate without seeing the required utilization.

GXO's release provides the other half of the shift. It reports work done, citing more than 100,000 totes at Flowery Branch and more than 65,000 hours of real use across Agility's commercial sites, alongside a manufacturing plant sized for up to 10,000 a year. It reports scale, with more than 1,000 sites and 200 million square feet. It holds no money figure at all, and the word cost never appears.

One firm publishes how much work a robot did, the other sells a model of how much work a robot will do, and neither publishes what one unit of that work costs. I read the pair of announcements as a shift in financial control rather than deliberate concealment. An operator who signs a multi-year deal ends up paying down a plan they never measured, at a rate they cannot take apart, all because the foundation of their business case was authored by the vendor. That creates the central clash between the two announcements.

## By the numbers

- **100,000-plus — Totes moved:** the count GXO gives for its first commercial humanoid site, in Georgia, where the robots have worked since June 2024 [1].
- **65,000-plus — Hours logged:** real-world run time Agility reports across its commercial sites, given as a company total with no fleet count to divide it by [1].
- **35% to 45% — Cut in surprise downtime, vendor estimate:** what AutoStore says its upkeep alerts can do, offered as an input to the case rather than as a measured result [2].
- **540 — Order lines an hour:** the rate AutoStore quotes for its lighter picking offer at one station, for orders that are already picked [2].

## What I'd watch

- **The first measured cost per tote.** Neither release will produce this figure, because the number lives in a logistics provider's customer deal or in a buyer's own time study. Both of those documents stay strictly private by default.
- **The audit clause in the next large deal.** A buyer who cannot take a rate apart will sooner or later ask for the throughput guess, the site baseline, and the list of what was left out. The reply to that request is the true test of the vendor's model.
- **The term behind "lighter commitment".** AutoStore uses this phrase for Pio Pal, avoiding the word short. The exit terms are where a mid-market operator now carries the risk that a fixed purchase used to carry.
- **Factory output sold ahead of the proof.** Agility's plant is built for up to 10,000 robots a year while its public record is 65,000 hours of work. I am watching whether that capacity gets placed through pilot programs rather than through repeat, measured deals.

## The catch

The honest limit here is that vendors often know more than their buyers, and I keep returning to this imbalance when reviewing the numbers. A storage supplier that has modeled two thousand sites holds a stronger view of a typical project than a mid-market operator with no throughput baseline of its own, meaning that dismissing that accumulated knowledge would be a significant mistake. A buyer who insists on measuring everything alone will take far longer and almost certainly land on a worse first guess.

Renting the machines through RaaS does not remove the model. It moves the same guesses into a rate, which is easier to read on a monthly bill but no easier to verify on the floor. The 100,000 totes are also real work: a humanoid that has handled that volume in a live building has shown it can run, which counts for something.

What the count cannot show is that the site pays. No party in either release publishes a payback, a yearly saving, or a cost per tote, meaning any figure a reader has seen for humanoid economics came from a model authored by someone with a stake in the outcome. The one number worth building yourself is the cost of the work you already do. It remains the only input no vendor can hand you.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** In the same fortnight, AutoStore began selling a generative AI tool that builds the payback case for its own systems, and GXO expanded a humanoid fleet whose public proof is a count of totes moved. The case for warehouse automation now comes from the seller.
- **Why It Matters:** A payback rests on how busy the machines are, how often they break, how much space they save, and how much labor they replace. All four of those inputs are now vendor estimates. An operator that takes them at face value is paying down someone else's guesses, which explains how a fleet can look proven at 100,000 totes while carrying no rate anyone can defend.
- **What I'd Watch:** The first buyer that asks for the guesses in writing. That request is what turns a vendor model into a decision a finance desk can stand behind.
  - **The site baseline:** the operator's own count of units handled per year, which no set of lookalike projects can supply.
  - **The loaded labor rate:** wage plus attendance, overtime, and turnover, the figure a vendor estimate tends to understate.
  - **The contract terms:** length, exit, and repricing clauses, which decide who holds the throughput risk when the rate moves.
- **The Catch:** Vendor benchmarks are better than most buyers' guesses, and robots as a service makes a rate visible where a capital number used to hide. Neither fixes the deeper problem, which is that nobody in this market publishes what a unit of warehouse work costs.
