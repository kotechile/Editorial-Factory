Shares held for more than a year can leave your account as a charitable gift at their full market price. This ensures the gain inside them never becomes taxable income. On a $150,000 gift of employer stock that cost you $40,000, that is $110,000 of gain the tax code never sees. At the top long-term rate plus the 3.8 percent surtax on investment income, it is $26,180 you do not pay [1][5][6]. A cash check for the same amount cannot match this outcome. Giving cash means selling the shares first and paying the tax on the way out.

## The big picture:

The statute calls a gift capital gain property when selling the asset at market value would have produced a long-term gain. This requires a holding period longer than one year [3][4]. Give a lot younger than that and the deduction falls back to what you paid, because the law removes the gain that would have been short-term [3]. I find it fascinating how a single day on the calendar dictates whether you deduct the market price or your own cost.

Clear that one-year line and the ceiling becomes a share of income rather than a dollar cap. A gift of such shares to a public charity counts only up to 30 percent of your contribution base. The statute defines this base as your adjusted gross income without a loss carryback [3]. The uncredited balance is never lost, as the excess acts as a gift made in each of the five years that follow, in order of time [3].

The rule behind those limits works the same math with dollar figures. Its example allows $6,000 of capital gain property against a $20,000 contribution base, which is the 30 percent ceiling applied to a real number [2].

## By the numbers

- **30 percent — Ceiling on the year:** A gift of shares to a public charity counts up to 30 percent of adjusted gross income, so a $400,000 base carries $120,000 of gift value this year [3].
- **$30,000 — The spillover:** The amount above that ceiling is not lost; it is treated as a gift made in each of the five following years [3].
- **$22,000 — What the 50 percent box leaves:** Publication 526's example gives land worth $28,000 with a basis of $22,000, and making the election reduces the deduction to that basis [1].

## What I'd watch:

The lots that qualify are the ones held longest. In a grant that vests over four years, those oldest shares usually carry the smallest cost basis. Choosing these specific lots removes the most embedded gain per share, maximizing the untaxed value transferred [3][4]. My focus is always on the final weeks of the year, when most holders rush to finalize these transfers.

- **The transfer happens in kind:** The shares move to the charity without a prior sale, keeping the gain unrealized [1].
- **The paperwork is the short form:** Publicly traded securities go on Section A of Form 8283 once the year's noncash total passes $500, bypassing the appraisal section [1].
- **The year is the year of delivery:** The deduction belongs to the year the property reaches the charity [1].

Before any of that matters, the position itself must be worth shrinking. A [hold-versus-sell model](/calculators/rsu-hold-vs-sell/) puts the concentration and the vest schedule in one place.

## The catch

Three distinct limits sit beneath this entire strategy.

- **A floor sits in the same paragraph.** The code text also carries a 0.5 percent floor: the deduction is allowed only to the extent the year's gifts exceed half a percent of that base [3].
- **A loser is the wrong gift.** Where the shares are worth less than you paid, the deduction is limited to market value and the fall in price cannot be deducted at all [1].
- **An overstatement is priced.** Claim 150 percent or more of the correct value and the penalty is 20 percent of the tax you underpaid, once that shortfall passes $5,000 [1].

My reading of these rules is that a careless transfer can easily cost more than it saves. Remember that the deduction is never a direct refund. Its true worth depends on the tax rate you pay, which none of these sources sets for you.

## At a glance

**The shift:** Employer shares can leave your account as a gift at full market value, so the gain inside them is never taxed, which cash cannot do.
**The number:** 30 percent of adjusted gross income caps the year's deduction for shares, and the excess waits up to five years.
**The risk:** Shares held a year or less deduct at cost, and the box that lifts the ceiling cuts the deduction back to basis.
