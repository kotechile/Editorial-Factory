<!-- lead -->
The price of warehouse space in the United States has split in two. In September the gap between the cheapest quarter of the market and the dearest was $3.79 a pallet, against $1.06 in July [2]. That gap now sets your fill rate — the share of order lines a warehouse can hand over complete.

The gap opened because cut-price bids from logistics firms pulled the cheap end down while scarce space in tight cities pushed the dear end up [2].

For anyone accountable for a fill rate, this changes the problem, because a fill rate depends on stock already sitting in the right building. Adding stock at a site that has just repriced upward raises the cost of the promise without improving the odds of keeping it.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Committed warehouse space is shrinking and repricing up while short-term space is plentiful but unevenly priced.
- **The number:** 3.6 times — how much wider the pallet-price gap grew since July.
- **The risk:** A fill target at the same site pays your fastest-rising cost.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Flexe published its third-quarter market report on October 21 [2]. The Logistics Managers' Index (LMI), released on November 3, followed with its October survey of supply chain managers [1].

Six universities and the Council of Supply Chain Management Professionals (CSCMP) run the index, which polls supply chain managers every month [1]. Its scores are diffusion figures: above 50 means growth, below 50 means shrinkage, so the readings show direction and speed rather than size [1].

Inventory levels jumped to 66.9, their fastest growth since September 2022, while inventory costs rose to 88.8, the highest since March 2022 [1]. Warehouse capacity shrank again at 43.5, the sixth shrinking month in nine this year [1].

The capacity reading splits in a way that matters for service: firms upstream read 37.5, while firms downstream read 50.0, meaning no change at all [1]. Smaller firms read 40.0, against 48.3 for larger ones, and the space that is disappearing sits on the factory side of the chain, not at the retailer that already owns its sheds.

I kept returning to the split inside the capacity figure, because it tells me which buyers are feeling the squeeze.

The report names the cause directly in the survey's own words: "Firms are now pulling inventories in as quickly as they can — often at high costs — in an effort to capitalize on what they hope will be a blockbuster holiday season" [1].

Storage prices in the survey rose to 80.7, the fastest since May 2022 [1]. The short-term market moved unevenly across 21 cities, while new warehouse supply is still arriving fast and gross completions reached almost 75 million square feet in the quarter [2].

| Market | September spot price | Change over the quarter |
|---|---|---|
| National index | $14.14 a pallet | +2.7% |
| Dallas | $10.97 a pallet | -9.2% |
| Lehigh Valley | $11.33 a pallet | +6.4% |

Developers started another 88 million square feet, the most since mid-2023 [3], so space is not short in the country; it is short where the stock needs to stand.

## By the numbers

- **70.7:** The Logistics Managers' Index headline reading in October, up 0.5 from September's 70.2 and the second-highest in four years, behind June's 71.1 [1].
- **$0.47 a pallet:** How much spot stayed above national asking rent in September [2].
- **257.5:** Aggregate logistics costs in the index, up 11.4 from September and the highest since March 2022 [1].

## What I'd watch:

Nine of the 21 markets Flexe tracks are rated Buyer's, six Neutral, and six Seller's [2]. Eight markets changed rating in the quarter, so the local answer to "can I get space near my customers" now moves faster than the national one [2]. Flexe's demand pressure measure rose from 30 to 38 as imports held up, which means the leverage buyers gained over the summer is already narrowing.

The release says shippers are still bringing goods in but avoiding long-term commitments, and it carries CoStar's expectation that vacancy will peak early in 2027, not fall through next year [3]. That gives buyers a deadline for turning flexibility into committed space.

What I am watching is whether buyers who rented short-term space this quarter lock in the sites they need before the leverage disappears.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The honest limit is that the two numbers describe different things. One is a survey of what happened to a respondent's own costs last month, while the other is what short-term space cleared for in 21 markets. Both can be right, and neither proves the other wrong.

The publisher has an interest too, because Flexe sells access to the market whose prices it indexes, so its report is a commercial house describing its own market even where the data is measured [2].

There is also a gap the survey cannot price, because space you can rent in two weeks is not automatically a warehouse that can ship an order complete in the same two weeks. What I find hard to square is that one quarter produced both the cheapest spot space in some cities and the fastest-rising storage costs in the survey. Both are true, and the difference is the contract you sign.
