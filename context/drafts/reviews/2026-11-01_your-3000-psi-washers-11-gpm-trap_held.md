Greenworks sells its 3,000-PSI pressure washer with two numbers that never meet: 3,000 pounds per square inch (PSI) at 1.1 gallons per minute (GPM), and 2.0 GPM at 100 PSI [1]. That second clause is a trap for buyers who assume the big flow happens at high pressure. The machine is only quick where it is not cleaning. The 2,300-PSI Gen 2 at $312.99 moves 1.2 GPM at its own top pressure on the same 14-amp motor, which makes it the better driveway buy for most houses [2]. The 3,000-PSI Gen 2 at $349.99 is the one to take if stained concrete is the job and a second pass is acceptable [1].

## The big picture:

Pressure and flow do different jobs, and only one of them sets how long a surface takes. Pressure is what lifts a stain off concrete or strips a film of algae off siding, while flow is the volume of water carrying that loosened dirt away [1][6]. A nozzle that narrows the spray raises pressure and cuts flow at the same time. This inverse relationship explains why the two headline numbers sit at opposite ends of one setting.

Greenworks writes the condition into the feature list on all three pages, in the same sentence that carries the claim. The 3,000-PSI model reads 3,000 PSI at 1.1 GPM, then adds 2.0 GPM at 100 PSI in brackets. The two smaller machines do the same with their own figures [1][2][3]. DeWalt publishes the identical qualification in the footnotes of a competing unit, where maximum pressure is measured at the 15-degree nozzle and maximum flow at the soap nozzle [5]. My read on this industry practice is that vendors know buyers skim for the highest numbers, even if they belong to a low-pressure rinse.

What moves up the ladder is pressure, from 2,300 to 3,000, while the flow at that pressure stays at 1.2 gallons a minute and then falls to 1.1 at the top [1][2]. All three machines carry a 14-amp brushless motor, a 25-foot hose, a 35-foot cord and five nozzles [1][2][3]. A buyer comparing the three is choosing how hard the water hits, not how fast the work goes.

Popular Mechanics shows how the construction travels. Its review lists the 2,700-PSI Greenworks at 2,700 psi and 2.3 GPM with no condition attached to either, and the same page prices it at $379 [6]. That is not an error in the review, which also says a 1 GPM machine leaves a large patio or long siding taking a little longer [6]. It is the same pair of numbers the maker prints, minus the clause that explains them.

## By the numbers

- **$260.83 — Cost per gallon a minute at pressure:** the 2,300-PSI machine's price divided by the 1.2 GPM it delivers at 2,300 PSI, compared to $316.66 for the 2,700 and $318.17 for the 3,000 at 1.1 GPM [1][2][3].
- **$0.117 — Cost per rated PSI:** the 3,000-PSI unit is the cheapest per pound of pressure at $0.117, sitting against $0.136 for the 2,300 and $0.141 for the 2,700, driven by the steepest markdown in the lineup [1][2][3].
- **8.3% — Water the top rung gives up:** the drop from 1.2 GPM to 1.1 GPM at working pressure, a fall that runs backward against the 30.4% rise in pressure across the ladder [1][2].
- **$37.00 — The step that buys less flow:** the price gap between the 2,300 and the 3,000, paying for 700 more PSI but losing one tenth of a gallon a minute of water at pressure [1][2].

## What I'd watch:

The three machines differ on three things that change the buy, and none of them is the number on the box. The first is what the water strips. A driveway head and the cheapest machine are matched to each other, since 2,300 PSI is the rating of the brand's own 12-inch rotating surface cleaner [2][4]. The second is the pace across a surface, where the 2,300 and the 2,700 deliver the same 1.2 GPM and the 3,000 gives up a tenth of a gallon [1][2][3]. The third is what the extra pressure does to soft wood, where a narrower, harder stream marks a deck faster than it cleans it.

I am keeping a close eye on how these sale tags flip the expected retail hierarchy. The 3,000 sits $30.00 below the 2,700 today, and the 2,300 sits $37.00 below the 3,000, so the lineup reads cheapest to dearest as 2,300, 3,000, 2,700 [1][2][3]. A reader who ranks the boxes by PSI and buys the middle one pays the most for the same flow as the cheapest.

The storefront also sells a second 3,000-PSI listing under an older model number, and that number is what to check at checkout rather than the series name. A prior-generation 3,000-PSI listing on the same store sits at $424.99 with the same headline numbers, $75.00 above the Gen 2 [1]. The 2,700 also ships as a bundle with a 15-inch cleaner and a wand extension for $479.98 [7].

## The catch

The math breaks. Greenworks sells a universal 12-inch rotating surface cleaner for $39.99, and its specification block gives the maximum pressure as 2,300 PSI [4]. That rating perfectly matches the cheapest machine but sits 700 PSI below the top rung. A buyer who wants both has to run the 3,000 at part pressure or accept an accessory working above its stated ceiling.

I suspect most homeowners underestimate how much a missing tenth of a gallon matters on a sloping driveway, where water pools and a pass takes longer anyway. What the pages do not carry is the one number that would settle it: the flow each machine delivers through the 15-degree nozzle specifically. The maker prints flow at peak pressure and flow at 100 PSI, and nothing in between. The middle of the curve stays a guess for every buyer of every brand here.

## At a glance

**The shift:** The manufacturer's specification separates peak pressure from peak flow, attaching the high flow rate to a low-pressure rinse.
**The number:** 1.1 GPM at 3,000 PSI, trailing the 1.2 GPM delivered by the $312.99 machine below it.
**The risk:** The surface cleaner built for driveway work is rated to 2,300 PSI, leaving it unmatched for the top two machines.
