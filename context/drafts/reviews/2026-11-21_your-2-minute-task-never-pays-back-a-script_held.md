<!-- lead -->
A 2-minute weekly task pays back 104 minutes a year. A script that needs a fix once a quarter burns 120 minutes of upkeep alone. The script does not break even. It loses year after year, and it feels like progress the whole time.

This is not a corner case. The oldest measured breakdown of software work found that 48.0% of staff hours went to keeping old systems alive, not building new ones [2]. A home script inherits that shape with none of the budget.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

This article prices the build-versus-upkeep trade for hobbyist automation scripts. For anyone tempted to script a small recurring task, it finds the upkeep hour — not the build hour — decides the trade, and most small tasks never clear the bar.

<!-- tension -->

## Upkeep is the real price

The upkeep never shows up in anyone's plan, and I have caught myself skipping it every time I open an editor. The hours you spend writing a script are the cheap part. The hours it charges forever are what decide the trade. In the 1978 survey of 69 data processing shops, keeping and improving old systems took 48.0% of annual staff hours [2]. Your script wants the same attention. It just bills you on weekends.

Now run the numbers. A 2-minute weekly task returns 104 minutes a year. A script that eats 30 minutes a quarter consumes 120. The upkeep alone outruns the task, so there is no payback date at all. Even a script that never needs a touch has to amortize an 8-hour build, which takes 240 weeks — about 4.6 years — before a 2-minute weekly task repays it. Give the script a five-year life with quarterly upkeep, and the task must be worth at least 4.2 minutes a week to cover the build and the fixes.

## By the numbers

- Quarterly upkeep of 30 minutes burns 120 minutes a year.
- The break-even threshold is 4.2 minutes a week over a five-year script life.
- Zapier Professional costs $239.88 a year, or 5.0 hours at the median wage.

## The rule that settles it

Put the hours on one line and the call makes itself. Take the minutes you spend keeping the script alive each year, add the build spread over its life, divide by 52. If the task sits below that line, do it by hand and keep the weekend. If it sits above, automate — for scale or to remove a step you always fumble, not for fun.

The paid tier sits on the other side, and it is priced in hours, not dollars. Zapier Professional starts from $19.99 a month, which is $239.88 a year at the vendor's list price [3]. At the 2025 median wage of $47.66, that subscription costs 5.0 hours of your own time [1]. A pipeline whose upkeep runs past 5 hours a year costs more than the subscription it replaced — the same move that priced the maintenance hour in [Your Standby Bill Hides 62 Watts](/your-standby-bill-hides-62-watts/).

The verdicts are dull, and that dullness is the point. Most small tasks deserve "hand." A few big ones deserve a script with no moving parts. Brittle chains across many services deserve the $19.99. Same rule, three inputs.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## What the rule cannot carry

This prices time, and time is not the only reason people automate — that is a limit of the model, not a new claim. A script that runs unwatched overnight buys reliability the minute count does not see. The 48.0% figure is also fifty years old and describes big IT shops, not homelabs; I use it because no newer measured count says its shape is wrong [2].

The wage has its own limit. $47.66 is a national median for a paid role [1]; no one pays a hobbyist for Saturday hours. So the rule prices cost of time, not cash. That is the point. The hours come out of the same weekend the projects do, and a script that eats more of the year than the task it replaced is a hobby dressed as a win.
