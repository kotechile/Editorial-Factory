<!-- lead -->

A failed compressor puts three numbers on your repair bill: the part, the labour, and the gas. The gas is the one that sets the next decade, because it carries the system's regulatory limit. Most American systems built since the mid-2000s run on R-410A, and the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) lists that gas at a global warming potential of 2,090 [2]. New residential air conditioners and heat pumps have been held to a limit of 700 since 2025, and those two figures pull a repair decision in opposite directions [1].

That gap matters more than the efficiency rating on the quote. A repair that refills an old coil puts money into a fluid whose national supply is being cut by statute, while a replacement has to use a fluid that clears the limit. The number to read on a bid is the refrigerant, not the efficiency rating beside it.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** The refrigerant class now sets a system's service life, not the efficiency rating.
- **The number:** 2,090 — the warming potential of the gas in most older systems.
- **The risk:** The limit governs new equipment, not the gas sold to service the old.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Congress set the national phasedown in the American Innovation and Manufacturing (AIM) Act of 2020. The EPA rule that implements it was published on October 24, 2023 [6]. The provision that reaches a homeowner began on January 1, 2025, when the agency barred installing a residential or light-commercial air conditioner or heat pump with a fluid at a warming potential of 700 or above [7]. Variable refrigerant flow systems sit on a later schedule, because that subsector needed its own dates [7]. The agency then allowed stock built or imported before the 2025 date to be installed until January 1, 2026, so warehouses would not be stuck with it [6].

The cap works on supply: it limits how much of these gases may be made and used in a year, measured against the average volume from 2011 through 2013 [5]. The permitted share is 60 percent through 2028 [5]. It falls to 30 percent from 2029 and reaches 15 percent from 2036 [5]. A coil that leaks after 2029 will draw on a smaller allowance than it would have a year earlier. Nothing in that schedule asks how old the equipment is.

I read the supply table three times before the gap landed: a system's build year and the year its gas gets scarce are not the same thing. The effect lands unevenly, too, because an owner who replaces a working unit pays once and leaves the question behind. An owner who repairs a failed compressor is buying gas for a box that still has years in it. The value of that repair now rests on a supply schedule written in Washington.

## By the numbers

- **470 — the replacement gas:** R-454B's warming potential, in the mildly flammable (A2L) class [3].
- **2,090 — the gas in the old box:** R-410A's warming potential, in safety group A1 [2].
- **30 percent — the later allowance:** the statute permits this share from 2029, against 60 percent in 2024 through 2028 [5].

## What I'd watch:

Three details in a bid tell you which side of the line a system sits on, and none of them is the efficiency rating. The first is the fluid named on the data plate, because R-410A and R-454B cannot be swapped for one another. The second is the build date: a "new" R-410A unit sold today can only be assembled from parts made or imported before January 1, 2025 [7]. The agency's allowance for that older stock ran out in January 2026, and that is the date I would ask a contractor to speak to [6].

The third is handling, because the substitutes the EPA lists for this sector are mildly flammable (A2L), and that is why the tools and the training around them changed [3]. The 2029 step is the one to watch, because it carries a date rather than a trend. The allowance halves at that point, inside the service life of anything bought this year [5]. Variable refrigerant flow systems have their own ladder: the limit arrives in 2026, and pre-2026 parts may be installed through 2027 [7]. The agency reopened parts of the rule in May 2026, so the dates are worth checking rather than assuming [6].

A temperature split across the coil separates a metering fault from a dead compressor, and it comes before any decision about what gas goes back in. ## The catch

What I find hard to square is how little of this restricts what a household may own. The EPA lists R-410A as an acceptable substitute, so recharging an existing system remains legal. The limit applies to making, importing, and installing new equipment, not to the gas a service truck carries [1]. A reader who hears a ban on their own condenser has heard it wrong. The same table that carries the 2,090 figure also carries the word acceptable [2].

The other limit is that no public source prices the gas at the counter. The scarcity case rests on the statute's own percentages, and a forecast of what the fluid will cost next winter would be the first number to go stale [5]. So the useful output is a question rather than a number: which fluid does the equipment take? The legal restriction sits in a federal rule, and the version current as of November 5, 2026 is the one to read before signing anything [7].
