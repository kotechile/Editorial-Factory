Alphabet spent $51.4 billion on property and equipment last quarter, mostly on computing for artificial intelligence [5]. It reported negative cash flow of $9.1 billion and bought back no shares at all, against $39.8 billion of stock retired in the first nine months of last year [1]. In the same three months it raised $42.9 billion of common stock, $19.1 billion of preferred stock and $28.7 billion of senior notes [1]. Its chief financial officer also took an $8.2 billion hit on one holding, the SpaceX stake [5]. For your portfolio, that buyback gap is the trade. The exit now clears at $0, into a market where the company sells stock instead of buying it, and the de-risking trade has moved off-screen.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

I reviewed both filings this week, and the cash flow statements reveal a sudden pivot. A buyback is the quiet half of an equity grant, because it retires about as many shares as vesting creates. Covering the tax bill used to leave the share count roughly flat. Alphabet retired $39.8 billion of stock in the first nine months of 2025, and nothing at all in the same stretch of 2026 [1]. The count rose from 12,088 million to 12,294 million, about 1.7% on my own division [1]. The growth rate that decides whether holding still beats selling is what these grants turn on, and a hold-versus-sell model is built to compute it.

Amazon shows the same shift from the cash side. Over twelve months it took in $168.3 billion of cash from operations and spent $215.2 billion on equipment [2]. That leaves a gap of $46.9 billion by my subtraction. In the quarter it spent $77.3 billion on equipment, against $35.1 billion a year earlier [2]. It borrowed $115.1 billion long term in nine months, and $48.1 billion of that landed in the last three [2]. The case for holding the stock once rested on cash the company could hand back, but now it rests on lenders.

The regulator has been opening a second door at the same moment. On September 30 the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) proposed rules to widen access to private markets [3]. The package covers interval funds, which are pools that buy back only a slice of their shares each quarter, and it asks whether a professional credential should be enough to qualify as an accredited investor, the status that decides who may buy private funds [3]. The credentials named run from a Certified Public Accountant licence to a Chartered Financial Analyst charter and a financial planner certification [3].

The chair framed the plan around savers' "post-tax, pre-retirement dollars", and tied it to an executive order on alternative assets inside 401(k) plans [3]. That is the account most holders would reach for when they want the diversified sleeve they think they lack. The same day showed the other side of that door, when the SEC charged a fund adviser and its chief executive over funds holding SpaceX and other shares of companies not yet public [4]. The complaint says the firm sent investors statements that inflated their account values [4]. It also says one fund lost "nearly $3,000,000" after the men charged ignored a capital call, which is a demand for cash that a private fund makes on the people invested in it [4].

## By the numbers

- **−$9.1 billion — Alphabet's quarterly cash flow:** negative for a second quarter running, after $(5.9) billion in the second quarter and a positive $24.6 billion in the last quarter of 2025; twelve-month cash flow is $19.7 billion against $159.8 billion of capital spending [1].
- **$0 — Alphabet's buybacks:** nothing bought back in the quarter or in nine months, against $11.5 billion and $39.8 billion a year earlier, while the share count rose 1.7% [1].
- **$46.9 billion — Amazon's spending gap:** twelve-month cash from operations of $168.3 billion against $215.2 billion of equipment, with $115.1 billion of long-term debt raised in nine months [2].
- **$75,000 — the 2027 savings cap:** Milliman's final estimate for the annual addition limit, about $3,000 above this year, and the Internal Revenue Service has not published the figure yet [6].

## What I'd watch:

The companies asking for patience are running single-stock books of their own. Alphabet's SpaceX position is what one holding looks like when it moves, and its purchase orders run years before any of that spending earns a dollar [5]. I am watching the funding choice carefully, because the company raised stock, preferred stock and debt while sitting on $265.1 billion of cash and securities [1].

- **The retirement channel:** the chair named 401(k) accounts as the target for alternative assets, and that is where most holders keep the money they already think is diversified [3].
- **Capital calls:** a call is a cash bill that arrives whether or not the fund's stated value has moved, and ignoring one is how a position gets lost [4].
- **The debt line:** Amazon's nine-month borrowing and Alphabet's jump from $46.5 billion to $125.1 billion of long-term debt both fund spending that no longer fits inside cash from operations [1][2].

## The catch

None of this is a cash shortage, and the balance sheets are not strained. Alphabet closed the quarter with $265.1 billion of cash and securities and took in $42.3 billion of cash from operations, so the negative figure reflects where it chose to put money [1]. One quarter of zero buybacks can reverse, and the filing states no intention either way [1].

My reading is that the fraud case targets one adviser rather than passing a verdict on interval funds, and the proposal may change before it is adopted [3][4]. What holds after those caveats is narrower and still useful. The two routes out of a single-stock position no longer differ much in what they own. They differ in how often the price is set, and only one of them can send a bill.

The index is not the escape either. The ten largest companies were 40.7% of the Standard & Poor's 500 at the end of 2025, against a band of about 18% to 23% from 1990 to 2015, and were expected to earn about 32% of its profits [7]. At midday on October 29 the Nasdaq Composite was down 1.17% while the broad index was down 0.27% [8]. A sleeve that holds those ten names is a lighter version of the same bet.

## At a glance

**The shift:** Alphabet's buyback went to zero while it sold stock and borrowed, so the shares an award creates now dilute holders instead of being retired.
**The number:** $0 of buybacks in nine months, against $39.8 billion a year earlier.
**The risk:** the private funds opening to smaller investors are priced quarterly and can lose a position on a capital call.
