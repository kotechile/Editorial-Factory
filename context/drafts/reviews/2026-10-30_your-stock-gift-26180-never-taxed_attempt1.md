The shares you have held for more than a year can leave your account as a charitable gift at their full market price, and the gain inside them never becomes income. On a $150,000 gift of employer stock that cost you $40,000, that is $110,000 of gain the tax code never sees. At the top long-term rate plus the 3.8 percent surtax on investment income, it is $26,180 you do not pay [1][5][6]. A check for the same amount cannot match this outcome. Writing that check means selling the shares first and paying the tax on the way out.

## The big picture:

I read the tax code's definition of capital gain property as a hard boundary. The label applies when selling an asset at market value would produce long-term gain, which for a vested share means a holding period longer than one year [3][4]. Give a lot younger than that and the deduction falls back to what you paid, because the statute removes the gain that would have been short-term [3].

Once you clear that holding period, the ceiling becomes a share of your income rather than a strict dollar cap. A gift of such shares to a public charity counts only up to 30 percent of your contribution base, which the statute defines as adjusted gross income without a loss carryback [3]. The rule behind those limits does the exact same sum with dollar figures. Its example allows $6,000 of capital gain property against a $20,000 contribution base, which is the 30 percent ceiling applied to a real number [2]. The excess value is never lost, as the tax code treats it as a gift made in each of the five following years [3].

## By the numbers

- **30 percent — Ceiling on the year:** A gift of shares to a public charity counts up to 30 percent of adjusted gross income, so a $400,000 base carries $120,000 of gift value this year [3].
- **$30,000 — The spillover:** The amount above that ceiling is not lost; it is treated as a gift made in each of the five following years [3].
- **$22,000 — What the 50 percent box leaves:** Publication 526's example gives land worth $28,000 with a basis of $22,000, and making the election reduces the deduction to that basis [1].

## What I'd watch:

The lots that qualify for the best treatment are the ones held longest. In a grant that vests over four years, those are usually the shares with the smallest cost basis. The gift takes out the most embedded gain per share. This means the same dollar of deduction removes a larger untaxed gain from the oldest lot [3][4]. What I watch closely is the mechanics of the transfer itself.

- **The transfer happens in kind:** The shares move to the charity without being sold first, and that is what keeps the gain unrealized [1].
- **The paperwork is the short form:** Publicly traded securities go on Section A of Form 8283 once the year's noncash total passes $500, not into the appraisal section [1].
- **The year is the year of delivery:** The deduction belongs to the year the property is handed over, which for most holders means the decision lands in the last weeks of December [1].

Before any of that matters, the position itself has to be worth shrinking. A [hold-versus-sell model](/calculators/rsu-hold-vs-sell/) puts the concentration and the vest schedule in one place.

## The catch

My reading is that the forms hold traps for the unwary, as three strict limits constrain the advantage of giving shares.

- **A floor sits in the same paragraph.** The code text also carries a 0.5 percent floor: the deduction is allowed only to the extent the year's gifts exceed half a percent of that base [3]. This calculation trims the first dollars off every gift.
- **A loser is the wrong gift.** Where the shares are worth less than you paid, the deduction is limited to market value and the fall in price cannot be deducted at all [1]. Selling and giving the cash is the route that keeps that loss usable, because the gift route does not.
- **An overstatement is priced.** Claim 150 percent or more of the correct value and the penalty is 20 percent of the tax you underpaid, once that shortfall passes $5,000 [1].

The deduction is never a direct refund. What it is worth depends on the marginal rate you pay, which none of these sources sets for you.

## At a glance

**The shift:** Employer shares can leave your account as a gift at full market value, so the gain inside them is never taxed, which cash cannot do.
**The number:** 30 percent of adjusted gross income caps the year's deduction for shares, and the excess waits up to five years.
**The risk:** Shares held a year or less deduct at cost, and the box that lifts the ceiling cuts the deduction back to basis.
