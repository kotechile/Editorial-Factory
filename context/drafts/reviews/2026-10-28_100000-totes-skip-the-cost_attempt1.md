GXO Logistics (GXO), the world's largest contract logistics provider, says its humanoid robots have moved more than 100,000 totes at one Georgia warehouse. This week's news that two more sites will follow carried no price for a single tote. Thirteen days earlier, AutoStore began shipping a tool that builds the payback case for buying AutoStore systems, alongside its own estimates for downtime avoided and space saved. Put the two releases side by side and a quiet handover emerges. The inputs your payback math rests on now come from the company selling you the machines. The one figure that would close the loop, what a unit of work costs, is the figure the tote count skips.

## The big picture

Payback math for warehouse automation needs four inputs. It requires the price of the gear, the work a site does in a year, the cost of the displaced labor, and the uptime the machines deliver. Vendors have always given buyers the first number. What changed this month is that they now sell the other three as products, built from comparable project data rather than from your building.

AutoStore's release shows this shift plainly. CubeExplore is a generative artificial intelligence (GenAI)-powered tool that leans on benchmarks to help partners and future buyers build stronger cases, while CubeStudio adds an artificial intelligence (AI)-powered design assistant to test layouts. CubeFleet watches upkeep and, on AutoStore's own estimate, can cut surprise downtime by 35% to 45%. FlexBinsPort is put at 10% to 15% better use of space. 

The same release moves this authorship into rate cards. Pio Pal sells robotic picking to smaller businesses as a lighter commitment under Robotics-as-a-Service (RaaS), at up to 540 order lines an hour at the workstation. That puts the vendor's guesses inside a contract, where the buyer sees the rate per line but still cannot see the use or downtime it is built on.

GXO's release is the other half of the fence. It reports work done: more than 100,000 totes at Flowery Branch, and more than 65,000 hours of real-world operation across Agility Robotics' commercial sites. It reports what the robots can do and the plant that will build them, sized for annual production capacity of up to 10,000 a year. It reports scale, with more than 1,000 sites and 200 million square feet. The release states no unit price, no rate, and no payback.

I read both announcements as the quiet reshaping of an approval process, rather than a pricing dispute. An operator who signs a multi-year deal ends up paying down a plan it never measured. When the model and the efficiency estimates all come from the seller, the buyer's financial control degrades into math on someone else's numbers.

## By the numbers

- **100,000-plus** — Totes moved: the count GXO gives for its first commercial humanoid deployment, in Georgia [1].
- **65,000-plus** — Hours of real-world operation: the time logged across Agility's commercial sites, given as a total with no fleet count to divide it by [1].
- **10,000** — Robots a year: the annual production capacity Agility's new manufacturing facility is sized for [1].
- **35% to 45%** — Cut in surprise downtime: what AutoStore estimates its CubeFleet alerts can achieve [2].
- **10% to 15%** — Better use of space: the storage improvement AutoStore estimates for FlexBinsPort [2].
- **540** — Order lines an hour: the rate AutoStore quotes at the workstation for its Pio Pal lighter picking offer [2].
- **200 million** — Square feet: the scale GXO reports across its network of more than 1,000 sites [1].

## What I'd watch

- **The first measured cost per tote.** Both releases skip this metric because the number lives in a logistics provider's customer deal or in a buyer's own time study. Both stay private by default.
- **The audit clause in the next large deal.** A buyer who cannot take a rate apart will eventually ask for the throughput guess, the site baseline, and the list of what was left out. The reply to that request tests the model.
- **The term behind the lighter commitment.** AutoStore calls Pio Pal a lower-commitment RaaS model, not a short one. Exit terms are where a mid-market operator now carries the risk that a fixed purchase used to hold.
- **Factory output sold ahead of the proof.** Agility's plant is built for up to 10,000 robots a year, while its public record is 65,000 hours of work. I am watching whether that capacity gets placed through pilot programs rather than repeat, measured deals.

## The catch

The honest limit here is that vendors often know more than their buyers. A storage supplier that has modeled two thousand systems has a stronger view of a typical project than a mid-market operator with no throughput baseline of its own. Ignoring that knowledge would be a mistake. A buyer who insists on measuring everything alone will take longer and land on a worse first guess.

Renting the machine does not remove the model either. It moves the same assumptions into a rate card, which is easier to read on a bill but no easier to verify. The 100,000 totes are also real work. A humanoid that has handled that volume in a live building has shown it can run, and that counts for something.

What the count cannot show is that the site pays. My view is that any humanoid economics figure a reader has seen came from a model, and every model has an author. The one number worth building yourself is the loaded cost of the work you already do, because it is the only input no vendor can hand you.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** In the same fortnight, AutoStore began shipping a GenAI tool that builds the payback case for its own systems, and GXO expanded a humanoid fleet whose public proof is a count of totes moved. The case for warehouse automation now comes from the seller.
- **Why It Matters:** A payback rests on how busy the machines are, how often they break, how much space they save, and how much labor they replace. All four are now vendor estimates. An operator that takes them at face value is paying down someone else's assumptions.
- **What I'd Watch:** Watch for the first buyer that asks for the baseline guesses in writing. That request turns a vendor model into a decision a finance desk can stand behind.
  - **The site baseline:** the operator's own count of units handled per year, which no set of comparable projects can supply.
  - **The loaded labor rate:** wage plus attendance, overtime, and turnover.
  - **The contract terms:** length, exit, and repricing clauses, which decide who holds the throughput risk when the rate moves.
- **The Catch:** Vendor benchmarks are often better than most buyers' guesses, and RaaS makes a rate visible where a capital number used to hide. Neither fixes the deeper problem, which is that nobody publishes what a unit of warehouse work costs.
