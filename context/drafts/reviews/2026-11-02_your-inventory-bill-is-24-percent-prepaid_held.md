Caterpillar put $4.1 billion of cash into stock in the first nine months of 2026, twice the drain a year earlier, and its operating cash still rose 30%. Buyers had handed over $4.0 billion in advance payments for machines that had not shipped, and the advances balance reached $5,329 million, equal to 24% of the $22,214 million of stock on the books [1]. The cash-flow line for those payments added $4,002 million this year against $1,649 million a year earlier [1]. Tractor Supply added 11.2% to its stock in the same stretch with no such line behind it, and its operating cash fell by a third [2]. The same build landed on opposite sides of the cash statement.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Both firms had a reason to carry more goods. The maker's stock rose 22.5% while its nine-month sales rose 21%, so the build tracked the order book [1]. The size of the build is not the part that matters; the money under it is. Customer advances are payments a buyer makes before a machine ships, and the maker books them as a contract liability rather than revenue [4]. That balance rose to $5,329 million from $3,314 million at the start of the year [1]. I looked for a word like "financing" in both filings and found it nowhere near the stock lines, though the two builds are the same choice priced two ways.

The retailer's stock build had no buyer standing behind it. Its stock rose 11.2% while sales rose 3.6%; average stock per store went to $1,290.8 thousand from $1,231.9 thousand, and turns slipped to 3.08 from 3.24 over nine months [2]. Same-store sales rose 0.4% against 3.9% a year earlier, and that is the demand the extra goods were ordered to meet [2]. The retailer borrowed instead: long-term debt rose to $2,230.3 million from $1,744.2 million [2].

## By the numbers

- **162 days vs 129 — stock on hand:** The maker's $22,214 million of stock against nine-month cost of sales is 162 days of goods, and the retailer's $3,618.8 million against its own cost line is 129 days, both worked out from the closing balances [1][2].
- **$5,329 million — customer advances:** The maker's money paid before shipment, up from $3,314 million at the start of the year, with $4,002 million arriving on the cash-flow line against $1,649 million [1].
- **30% up, 33% down — cash from operations:** The maker's nine-month figure rose to $10,593 million from $8,148 million while the retailer's fell to $882.6 million from $1,310.4 million [1][2].
- **3.75% to 4% — policy rate:** The Federal Reserve held this range on Oct. 28, on a 10–2 vote, with two members preferring a quarter-point rise [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

Three lines answer the question next quarter, and each one shows up in the next filing. The first line to follow is the maker's advances balance. If it stops growing while stock keeps rising, the funding has moved from customer deposits to the firm's own balance sheet, and the cash gain reverses [1]. The second is dealer stock, and it matters for the sales line. The maker's mining and heavy-construction sales rose "primarily due to the impact from changes in dealer inventories," so part of that volume was dealers stocking rather than end users buying; a dealer drawdown would reach the sales line first [1].

The third is payables, the other zero-cost source in the chain. The retailer's accounts payable rose to $1,659.9 million from $1,549.2 million, which covered $110.7 million of a $529.8 million build; the rest came from cash and borrowed money [2]. That is not a scandal, it is the terms a retailer can use, and the borrowing shows up in the long-term debt line. I am watching whether the December meeting raises the price of that borrowed part, since two members already voted for a quarter-point rise [3]. The policy rate is also the smallest piece of the carrying cost of stock, which is why any self-funded build costs more than the interest line shows.

## The catch

The comparison is a demonstration, not a market study of two identical firms. An equipment maker can book advances because it sells years before it builds, while a retailer sells at the counter and cannot take a deposit on a bag of feed. So 24% describes the maker's business model, not a target another firm can copy. My view is that the share still matters, because the other choice is your own cash, and the price of your own cash is published every six weeks.

An advance is not free, because it carries a ship date. The buyer has paid for a machine that must be delivered, so the cash behind the build is a promise to finish it. If demand softens, the firm holding the deposits must ship into the weaker market. The maker's quarter shows the other side of the same margin: $128 million of tariff refunds lifted operating profit while material costs including tariffs still ran through the factory [1].

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** Stock builds now arrive funded two ways, by customer deposits or by the balance sheet.

**The number:** Customers paid for 24% of the maker's stock before it shipped.

**The risk:** A deposit is a promise to ship, and a self-funded build pays the policy rate.
