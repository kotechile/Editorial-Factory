GXO Logistics (GXO), the world's largest pure-play contract freight provider, says its walking machines have moved more than 100,000 totes at one Georgia shed. This week's news that two more sheds will follow carried no price for a single tote. Thirteen days earlier, AutoStore began selling a tool that builds the business case for buying its cube-storage setups. That tool ships next to the vendor's own math on the stops and floor space a buyer will save. Put the two updates side by side and a quiet handover shows up. The numbers your payoff rests on now come directly from the company selling you the gear, replacing the hard data a finance desk used to demand before signing a check. The one figure that would settle the question—what a unit of work costs—is the exact figure the tote count skips.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture

A return on machines needs four inputs. It needs the price of the gear, the work a shed does in a year, the cost of the staff it replaces once attendance and turnover are counted, and the uptime the machines deliver. Sellers have always given buyers the first one. What changed this month is that they now sell the other three as products, built from a set of lookalike projects rather than from your building.

AutoStore states this plainly. CubeExplore is a tool that leans on benchmarks and broad data from similar projects to help partners and would-be buyers build a stronger case without measuring their own floors. CubeStudio tests layouts before anything is built. CubeFleet tracks upkeep and, on AutoStore's own math, can drop surprise stops by 35% to 45%. FlexBinsPort is credited with a 10% to 15% gain in space use. Each figure may be fair, and each one comes from the side that gets paid.

The same notice shows how far the repackaging goes. Pio Pal pitches piece picking to smaller firms as a lighter commitment under robots as a service (RaaS), reaching up to 540 order lines an hour at a single station. That is a gain in clarity. A price per line is easier to check than a sheet of guesses. It also puts those same guesses inside a deal, where the buyer sees the rate and still cannot see how busy the machines must be for that rate to hold.

GXO is the other half. It logs the work done: more than 100,000 totes at Flowery Branch, and more than 65,000 hours of live run time across Agility's customer sites. It notes what the machines can do and the factory that will build them, planned for up to 10,000 units a year. It logs a massive footprint, spanning more than 1,000 sheds and 200 million square feet. It holds no money figure at all, and the word cost never appears.

That is the clash. One firm publishes how much work a machine did, the other sells a model of how much work a machine will do, and neither publishes what one unit of that work costs. I read this silence as a structural shift rather than a secret. Under a service pact, the buyer never sees a capital number at all. A buyer who signs a multi-year deal ends up paying down a plan it never measured, at a rate it cannot take apart.

## By the numbers

- **100,000 — Totes moved:** the volume GXO claims for its first live site in Georgia, where the machines have worked since June 2024 [1].
- **65,000 — Hours logged:** the live run time Agility notes across its customer sites, given as a total with no fleet count to divide it by [1].
- **35% to 45% — Cut in surprise stops:** what AutoStore says its upkeep alerts can achieve, offered as an input to the case [2].
- **10% to 15% — Gain in space use:** the storage bump AutoStore projects for its new bins port, offered as a guess rather than a measured result [2].
- **540 — Order lines an hour:** the speed AutoStore quotes for its lighter picking offer at one station [2].
- **10,000 — Robots a year:** the planned capacity for the plant that will build the walking machines [1].
- **1,000 — Sites:** the network scale GXO commands, spanning more than 200 million square feet [1].

## What I'd watch

- **The first measured cost per tote.** I am watching for a buyer to publish this, because the number lives in a freight provider's customer deal or in a firm's own time study, and both stay private by default.
- **The audit clause in the next large deal.** A buyer who cannot take a rate apart will sooner or later ask for the throughput guess, the site floor, and the list of what was left out. The reply to that request is the true test of the model.
- **The term behind "lighter commitment".** AutoStore uses this phrase for Pio Pal, rather than calling it a short deal. The exit terms are exactly where a mid-market firm now carries the heavy risk that a massive fixed purchase used to carry in the past.
- **Factory output sold ahead of the proof.** Agility sized its plant for up to 10,000 robots a year while its public record is 65,000 hours of work. The question is whether that capacity gets placed through pilots rather than through repeat, measured deals.

## The catch

The honest limit here is that sellers often know more than their buyers. A storage supplier that has modelled two thousand sites has a stronger view of a typical project than a mid-market firm with no throughput data of its own. Shrugging off that knowledge would be a mistake. A buyer who insists on measuring everything alone will take longer and land on a worse first guess.

Renting the machine does not remove the model either. It moves the same guesses into a rate, which is easier to read on a bill and no easier to check. The 100,000 totes are real work. A walking machine that has handled that volume in a live building has shown it can run, and that is not nothing.

What the count cannot show is that the site pays. My view is that since no party publishes a return or a cost per tote, any figure a reader has seen for robot economics came from a model. Every model of this kind has an author. The one number worth building yourself is the cost of the work you already do, because it is the only input no vendor can hand you.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** In the same fortnight, AutoStore began selling a GenAI tool that builds the business case for its own systems, and GXO expanded a robot fleet whose public proof is a count of totes moved. The case for warehouse tech now comes from the seller.
- **Why It Matters:** A return rests on how busy the machines are, how often they break, how much space they save, and how much staff they replace. All four are now vendor math. A firm that takes them at face value is paying down someone else's guesses. This is how a fleet can look proven at 100,000 totes and still carry no rate anyone can defend.
- **What I'd Watch:** The first buyer that asks for the guesses in writing. That request is what turns a vendor model into a decision a finance desk can stand behind.
  - **The site start:** the firm's own count of units handled per year, which no set of lookalike projects can supply.
  - **The loaded staff rate:** wage plus attendance, overtime, and turnover, the figure a vendor guess tends to understate.
  - **The pact terms:** length, exit, and repricing clauses, which decide who holds the throughput risk when the rate moves.
- **The Catch:** Vendor benchmarks are better than most buyers' guesses, and RaaS makes a rate visible where a capital number used to hide. Neither fixes the deeper problem. Nobody in this market publishes what a unit of warehouse work costs.
