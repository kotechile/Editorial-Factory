A Maine judge ordered a builder to pay a homeowner $630,500 back. The checks come at $125 a week, which pays the debt off in 97 years, before any interest [1]. The family won the case and still watched the payments stop for more than six months; the first check since then arrived this week [1]. The lesson for anyone hiring a builder is blunt. A court order is not money in your hand, and the only cash you can hold is the cash you have not paid yet.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

A Maine family's $281,000 camp renovation stalled, and a judge ordered the builder to repay $630,500. This article follows the case and Maine's own records to show why collection, not the verdict, is the real fight.

<!-- tension -->

## The order was real. The money was not.

In 2022, Adam Goode hired a builder a friend had praised, on a fixed-price deal of about $281,000 to rebuild his family's camp in Raymond, Maine down to the studs [1]. A year later the camp was still unfit to live in. Then came a text asking for "$20,000 more to continue your project." The family stopped paying. A second builder found a roof and ceiling letting water into the house, with no vapor barrier underneath [1].

In court, records showed the company the builder claimed as his — Timberline Construction of Windham — was not active in the state's registry [1]. It had been struck off years before the deal was signed. A judge found the builder broke the state's home-contract law repeatedly and awarded more than $630,500 plus interest [1]. The order set repayment at $125 a week, and it warned that missed checks could bring a contempt charge and jail.

The mechanism here is structural, and I keep landing on the same fact: nothing screened the person behind the name. Maine does not license home builders at all [2]. A company struck from the registry can still take deposits, and a judgment can only collect from someone who still has assets. The state's own words say how often that fails: even after wins in civil suits and criminal theft cases, Maine has been unable to collect a large share of the money its courts ordered paid, because the builders were bankrupt, broke for good, or gone [2]. The attorney general's office receives more complaints about builders than about any other kind of business [2]. The verdict ends the argument. It does not end the problem.

## By the numbers

- **$630,500 plus interest:** The pay-back a Maine judge ordered against the camp's builder [1].
- **$125 a week:** The agreed payment, which retires the award in 97 years without interest [1].
- **1/3 of the deal price:** Maine's cap on a home-build deposit — about $93,666 on this $281,000 job [2].

## Where the leverage sits

The state's own offices point at the answer and act on it. The attorney general's Consumer Mediation Program will tell a caller whether a given builder has drawn complaints, and it urges that check before work starts [2]. State law already builds the escrow shape into every deal over $3,000: the contract must be written, the deposit capped at a third, start and end dates set down, and every change order signed by both sides on paper [2]. A homeowner who keeps the unpaid balance large — the last payment held until someone independent inspects the work — does on purpose what the Goode family could only do after the fact: hold money while leverage still exists. The bond and insurance lines on a bid are a smaller backstop than most assume; a bond's ceiling and its claim clocks are fixed no matter what the job costs [3]. What I would do first, before any deposit moves, is the free part: search the registry for the company that is about to sign.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

Every one of these controls buys leverage, not certainty, and my reading of the state's own page is that it concedes the point [2]. A deposit capped at a third still leaves two-thirds of a job exposed while the work runs. A written contract is only as strong as the live company behind it. Maine lists theft convictions where collection still failed in large part [2]. What survives is modest: know who you are paying before the deposit moves, keep the last payment large, and treat a win in court as the start of a collection fight, not the end of one. The Goode family's first check in six months arrived this week. At this pace, the last one lands around the year 2123.
