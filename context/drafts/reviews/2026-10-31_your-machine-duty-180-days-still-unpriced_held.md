The duty on the robots and machine tools a reshoring project buys was neither set nor dropped this week. It went to trade talks. A Commerce Department review found the country imports most of the equipment its own factories run on [1]. The order signed on 28 October asks for a progress report in 180 days and names no rate, which leaves that equipment line still unpriced. The largest capital item in a factory build now sits against a duty nobody has set. The next decision is due in late April 2027.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Section 232, the national-security tariff law, used to mean a duty within weeks, but this week it meant a memo. The legal design is what matters most for a buyer calculating costs. The president kept tariffs, quotas, and fees in reserve, alongside a plan to pay for more output at home [1]. He can use them if no deal lands in 180 days, or if one lands and fails. I read the order as a warning rather than a reprieve, because a pause is not a zero. A zero is a number a planner can type into a cell, while an open threat leaves the spreadsheet blank.

The order states the country makes less robotics and industrial equipment than it uses [1]. That gap is vast, and tracked private investment in new factories reached $2.091 trillion as of 26 October [4]. The volume of construction makes an open rate expensive. Manufacturers and foreign investors announced 247,000 factory jobs through reshoring and Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) in 2025, and more than 2.3 million since 2010 [5]. High-tech goods made up 90% of those pledges, the highest share the group has recorded [5]. Those are the projects that buy robots and machine tools.

Planners are trying to price this risk. The Reshoring Initiative's annual report, out on 23 October, found that 40% of Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs) now use total cost of ownership analysis to compare home and offshore supply, up from 30% a year earlier [5]. That is a large shift in how companies measure vendor risk, and it happened in the same month the machine tariff rate went open.

## By the numbers

- **180 days — Review date:** the trade office and the Commerce Department must report by late April 2027, and tariffs, quotas, and fees stay on the table as remedies [1].
- **$86 billion — Equipment made at home:** that is what the sector produced in 2024, against about $7.1 trillion of factory goods shipped, the gap the review cites [1].
- **40% — OEMs on total cost:** up from 30% a year earlier, the share comparing home and offshore supply on total cost rather than on price [5].
- **16% to 33% — Contract makers quoting:** the share of contract manufacturers quoting reshoring work roughly doubled in a year [5].

## What I'd watch:

- **A report date is not an action date.** The order sets a day for a status report, not a day for a new entry rule. Trade counsel advises that importers should not touch codes on the strength of it [3]. I am watching how vendors price their April deliveries, because a model treating the 180-day mark as a hard tariff deadline misreads the paper.
- **Whether the habit holds.** The president has deferred Section 232 action three times in three months. The aircraft and wind files are the places to look if the machinery talks stall [2].
- **When pledges turn into steel.** The group counted 64 conditional projects in 2025 and found 14 had partly or fully converted by mid-2026 [5]. Pledges lead, but buildings prove the trend.
- **Who pays for home capacity.** The White House says it may fund domestic output as one remedy [1]. In the same week, the Agriculture Department put $573 million of proposed money behind eight fertilizer plants worth more than $1.3 billion [6].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The missing rate is a problem for builders, but my view is that the pause keeps projects moving that a sudden tax would kill. Trade talks can end in a deal that removes the threat. If they do, any hedge a buyer put in place reads as insurance bought at the top of the market. 

The employment forecasts carry their own tension. Policy uncertainty was the top concern, named by 57% of respondents. The group also projects about 309,000 jobs for 2026 off first-half numbers, and says a summer surge could carry the year to 400,000 [5]. The 14-of-64 conversion rate provides the grounded weight against those high estimates.

## At a glance

**The shift:** The duty on robots and machine tools went to talks instead of being set, with tariffs and quotas still in reserve.
**The number:** 180 days — the report date, after which the remedies come back.
**The risk:** The equipment line has no rate, so a cost model built now carries an unpriced liability.
