A hundred dollars an hour gets quoted against 2,080 hours a year, but no contract bills that many, because a contractor's year has unpaid days built into it. Billed 1,840 times, the rate brings in $184,000, and the self-employment tax takes 14.13% of that before anything else [3]. What remains, once a family health premium is paid at the price an employer used to pay, is the equivalent of a $153,783 salary [5]. The $54,000 between that and the $208,000 the multiplication promises is the part of a contract offer that gets quoted and never priced.

## At a glance

- **The shift:** A contract rate is priced by a multiplier, not an hourly-to-annual guess.
- **The number:** 1.35×, what the health premium alone adds to the 1.18× floor.
- **The risk:** The 1.5× everyone quotes is a month of bench time, not a tax rule.

<!-- tension -->

## The tax is the smaller half

Two payroll systems sit behind the two ways of being paid. An employee pays social security at 6.2% and Medicare at 1.45%, and the employer pays the same again on its own account [2]. That symmetry is the whole of the "double tax" story, and it is where the folklore stops.

A contractor pays both halves at once. The self-employment tax is 15.3%, charged on 92.35% of net earnings rather than on all of them [3]. The statute splits it into 12.4% for social security and 2.9% for Medicare [4].

On the 2025 median wage for software developers, $135,980, the employee's share is $10,402 [1]. The contractor's share on the same income is $19,213, before the deduction [3]. Half of that tax is deductible before income tax is computed, so the contractor's real cost falls. At a 32% bracket — the reader's bracket, not a published figure — it falls to about $16,139. The wedge is $5,737, or 4.2% of the salary. The rate a contractor quotes is a gross figure, and everything an employer used to pay on the side has to come out of it instead.

I keep coming back to how small that is. Four points of pay is a rounding error beside the second input, which is time nobody invoices. Thirty days off — eleven federal holidays, fifteen vacation days and four sick days — turns a 2,080-hour year into 1,840 billable hours. Replacing $141,717 of gross across those hours needs $77.02 an hour, or 1.178 times the $65.38 salary rate [1].

That is the floor. The tax argument is usually the thing used to justify it, and it is not what sets it.

## By the numbers

- **$135,980 — the median that sets the test:** The 2025 annual wage for software developers, and the base both sides start from [1].
- **$10,402 — an employee's payroll tax:** Social security at 6.2% and Medicare at 1.45% applied to that median [2].
- **$19,213 — the same income as a contractor:** The 15.3% self-employment rate on 92.35% of it, half of which is deductible [3].

## Where the multiplier comes from

The employer's side of a health plan is the biggest single line in a package, and the survey publishes it [5]. Family coverage averaged $26,993 in 2025, workers contributed $6,850 of that, and the employer paid the remaining $20,143. Carried into the gross a contractor has to replace, it lifts the rate to $87.97 — 1.345 times the salary rate. A 4% retirement match adds $5,439 and takes it to 1.391. An unpaid month between engagements covers the rest: at 1,687 billable hours the same package needs $99.19, or 1.52 times. None of those lines appear on the invoice a client sees, which is why a rate gets negotiated as though they did not exist.

What strikes me is that the cap inverts the story most people tell. The extra social security a contractor pays stops at 6.2% of the wage base, which is $11,439 at the 2026 limit of $184,500, while Medicare's 1.45% runs to the last dollar [2]. So the payroll-tax penalty is a fixed dollar amount: 4.2% of the median developer's pay, and about 6% of a $250,000 package. The better the rate, the weaker the tax case for a contractor premium, and the more of the multiplier is the price of a slow month.

## The catch

My read is that the arithmetic is honest about its inputs and still says little about one reader in particular. The 32% bracket is an assumption, the premium is family coverage rather than the plan in the offer, the match is a placeholder, and thirty days off is generous or stingy depending on the employer. Shift any one of them and the multiplier moves by a couple of points.

The medians are the larger caveat. The labour department's wage figure describes a job, not a person, and a contract at $100 an hour in a city where the salary median is $170,000 is a different trade from the same rate against a national median [1]. Utilisation matters more than any of it: 1,840 billable hours assumes steady work, and a quarter at 60% utilisation raises the break-even rate far more than the tax code does. That is the input nobody puts in the quote.

Two things sit outside the calculation. A contract carries no equity line — no grant, no vesting calendar, no second decision at a liquidity event — and this piece puts no dollar value on that absence, because there is no honest one. It also leaves out the self-employed health-insurance deduction, which narrows the gap a little in the contractor's favour.

What I take from it is narrower than a rule of thumb. The 1.5× premium is real, and most of it pays for risk and unbilled time. The 1.18× floor is the only part an offer letter can settle, and it is much smaller than the folklore suggests.
