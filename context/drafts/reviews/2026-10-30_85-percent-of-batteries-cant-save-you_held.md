<!-- lead -->
On the morning of 14 October, Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E) updated a wildfire shutoff list by adding 25,000 homes and taking 7,300 off it. By that afternoon, 88,000 customers across 29 counties were facing an outage scheduled to run four days as dry offshore winds approached the coast, and nearly 5,500 of those customers depend on constant power for medical equipment [1]. The same state charges 29.75 cents a kilowatt-hour for household power, which ranks third-highest in the country [2]. That price highlights a gap in the backup plan. About 85 percent of California's grid batteries sit on the utility's side of the meter, where they cannot save your house [3].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

A wildfire shutoff targets specific power lines rather than individual houses. Southern California Edison (SCE) warns customers that they might stand in calm air and still lose power, provided the line feeding their block crosses a windy, high fire risk area before reaching their exact street address [4]. The utility turns off that specific circuit and leaves it dead until crews can walk the route to inspect the poles.

I read the state's capacity breakdown twice before the gap made sense. The California Energy Commission (CEC) counts 21,112 megawatts of battery storage across the state, and the vast majority of it operates as industrial plant [3]. Nearly 16,000 megawatts come from 310 big sites inside the state, and another 2,000 megawatts are piped in from utility-scale facilities in Nevada and Arizona [3]. Those massive systems shuffle wholesale electricity around the wider grid, but they do not push power back into a local line that the utility just opened on purpose.

The remaining capacity is small. It represents the only hardware that keeps a household fridge cold. The commission counts about 3,000 megawatts spread over more than 300,000 systems at homes, schools, farms and shops, noting that these units "can provide backup power during outages" [3]. That division means roughly 14 percent of the fleet sits on the customer side of the meter, right where a shutoff lands.

The economics explain why this split persists. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) tracks the state's August household rate at 29.75 cents, compared to a national average of 18.16, which makes the local power 64 percent more expensive [2]. Only Hawaii and Maine charge more [2]. A grid battery earns capacity payments and a guaranteed return through the rate base. A battery on a garage wall earns only what its owner avoids paying, relying on a net billing tariff that the state's regulator calls "usually lower than the retail rate" [5].

## By the numbers

- **88,000 — Homes put on notice:** The 14 October list from PG&E covered 29 counties, about 5,500 Medical Baseline homes and six Tribal governments, prompting the utility to open more than 40 help centres across 23 counties [1].
- **10,410 — Cut three days earlier:** The utility cut 10,410 customers inside a 12,200-customer, 17-county zone, then warned that restored homes "could experience another outage" [6].
- **29.75 cents — The household rate:** The August figure ranks third-highest in the country and sits 64 percent above the 18.16-cent U.S. average [2].
- **21,112 megawatts — The battery fleet:** Nearly 16,000 megawatts sit in 310 big in-state plants and 2,000 more arrive from out of state, leaving about 3,000 megawatts in 300,000 small systems [3].

## What I'd watch:

The gap between a warning notice and a dark house is where the financial impact hides. A list is not an outage, and only a severed connection empties a freezer. I am watching the post-event reports to see how many homes endured the full four days.

- **The household share:** Finding 3,000 out of 21,112 megawatts is the math that decides whether a shutoff is survivable on a normal street.
- **The missing credit:** The federal clean energy credit stopped applying to spending after 31 December 2025, and the final installation date strictly controls the rule, so a 2026 job gets zero tax benefit even if the homeowner paid for the equipment earlier [7].
- **The export rate:** The regulator notes that sales back to the grid usually fetch less than retail, meaning a battery earns its keep mostly by running the house [5].
- **Medical Baseline homes:** About 5,500 were inside one week of notices, representing the locations where four days without power becomes a severe health event [1].

The rest of the problem comes down to raw arithmetic. A bank sized against a year of bills instead of critical loads runs out first, which explains why two houses on one exact line get different results from the same hardware.

## The catch

The honest counterweight is that the state's electricity price eased slightly even as its reliability exposure held firm. August's 29.75 cents is 3.8 percent below the 30.92 cents of a year earlier, while the U.S. average rose 3.4 percent [2]. My reading is that the cost story here is thinner than the reliability story, and I could be wrong about how much of that price dip survives the winter.

Shutoffs also achieve their main goal. A line that stays live through a severe wind event is a line that can start a wildfire, and the utility taking public anger is the one that successfully dodged the spark. What the public record lacks is a household plan that treats the small battery as basic gear rather than an optional extra.

The three financial levers remain separate and should stay that way. A retail rate, an export credit and an expired tax break are evaluated side by side, never added up, and none of them offsets another.
