<!-- lead -->
The shingle makers have accused their insurers' own research arm of bad science. The Asphalt Roofing Manufacturers Association (ARMA), the trade group for shingle producers, said the study behind a new 15-year limit rests on "non-standard testing protocols, unrepresentative grab-sampling, and incomplete probabilistic modeling," as reported by Insurance Journal [4]. The limit takes effect on January 1, 2027. After that, an asphalt roof past 15 years of age cannot hold the FORTIFIED designation — the certificate behind premium discounts in the states that reward it. The certificate expires, not the roof.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
This article covers the roofing industry's public attack on the insurance institute behind the FORTIFIED standard, and the new 15-year age limit that set it off. It concerns homeowners whose premium discounts ride on the certificate. The rule forces no roof replacement — but Florida shows what insurers do with age rules anyway.

<!-- tension -->
## The rule is narrow. The fear is not.

The sequence runs one way. The Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (IBHS), a research group funded by insurers, issued its bulletin on September 29, 2026. The words are plain: "designation eligibility is limited to 15 years from the initial FORTIFIED designation date" for a new roof, or from the documented shingle installation date for an existing one [1]. The limit applies only to applications filed on or after January 1, 2027. It does not shorten any term issued before that date [1].

ARMA answered in public on November 9. The trade group called the institute's conclusions "fundamentally compromised" and urged insurers not to use the bulletin for broad underwriting restrictions [4].

That gap is the whole story. IBHS insists the limit "does not establish asphalt shingle service life" and requires no replacement at any age [1]. The program's own page says the upgrades "remain in place" even after a home ages out of eligibility [2]. ARMA is not reacting to what the bulletin says. It is reacting to what insurers do with documents like it — and Florida is the precedent both sides carry.

Florida carriers began dropping roofs older than ten years solely on age in the early 2000s, and some shingle warranties ran 30 years [4]. Lawmakers answered in 2022 with a law that bars insurers from non-renewing homeowner policies on roofs under 15 years old solely for age, and requires continued coverage where an inspection finds at least five years of useful life left [4]. Only a handful of states, including Texas and Virginia, have similar rules [4]. I read that history twice before the fight made sense to me: the shingle makers watched insurers turn an age number into a cancellation letter once already, and no law in most states would stop them from doing it again.

## By the numbers
- **15 years:** the age limit on FORTIFIED asphalt-shingle eligibility in the September bulletin [1].
- **100,000 designations:** the milestone IBHS announced in May 2026, over half earned in the prior three years [3].

## Where this bites, and who is watching

The people closest to the story are blunt about the stakes. Security First Insurance, one of Florida's largest property carriers, prices on roof age and condition. Its president, Melissa Burt DeVriese, called age "the single-largest rate factor," and her company offers no premium discount for FORTIFIED homes [4]. The reason is the building code: FORTIFIED standards are close to what Florida's code already requires for roof coverings, decking and wind-resistant construction, so the certificate buys little where the code carries the load [4].

For a homeowner with an aging asphalt roof, the rule couples two clocks. The discount lasts only as long as the designation, and the designation now has an age ceiling. The paperwork can bite harder than the calendar: a roof with no documented installation date gets one term of up to five years, and no re-designation until the shingles are replaced [1]. The install invoice is the document that keeps the clock honest. The discount math behind [your 49% discount isn't all roof](/your-49-discount-isnt-all-roof/) already showed that a state mandate which looks like a windfall is partly a reinsurance story; this limit is the same discount, now with an expiry date.

What I am watching next is whether any carrier cites the bulletin in an underwriting rule the way Florida carriers once cited age alone. In most states nothing bars it. ARMA's public letter is, in effect, a record that the industry expects someone to try.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch
Both sides are arguing past the certificate. IBHS is right that the bulletin mandates nothing — no roof must come off at the age limit, and metal or tile roofs face no such limit [1]. ARMA is right that the distinction may not survive contact with insurers; it did not in Florida. The institute works for those same insurers, so the shingle makers are arguing with their biggest customers. One Florida contractor made the case in a sentence: his own 32-year-old shingles weathered Hurricane Milton with loose shingles as the only damage, and he wants shingle quality, not age, to drive the risk question [4]. My reading, held loosely, is that the test his argument faces first is not a storm but a certificate expiry — and that the fight will be settled in underwriting manuals, not in either side's press releases.
