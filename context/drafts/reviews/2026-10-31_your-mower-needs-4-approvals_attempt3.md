<!-- lead -->
Four robot mower models hold the only federal approval given to a consumer yard machine for the 2027 American market, and all four belong to one company. Husqvarna lost 334 million krona last quarter, after foreign-made robots went onto a national-security list in July. Your next mower needs one of those approvals before a store can sell it, and that federal list is public.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Husqvarna sells into both markets, so its financial report reads as a split screen. Group sales fell 5 percent organically in the quarter, and the forest and garden arm dropped 10 percent [1]. The company blamed the mowing weakness on bad weather, competitive headwinds, and dealers holding too much stock in Europe [1]. It answered with targeted price increases to offset the squeeze [1].

The American market presents a different landscape for the company. On 28 July the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) put foreign-made advanced robotic devices on a list of gear that cannot get the approval needed to be sold there [2][3]. A maker can escape that list by securing a Conditional Approval from the Department of War, and the FCC posts each one it grants [2]. The rule catches a mower: a moving machine over 4.4 pounds, with a sensor, a network link of at least 200 kilobits a second, and software that steers it [3].

I read the register twice to confirm the pattern. It lists seven robot approvals, and one covers a yard machine: Husqvarna's 305v iQ, 310v iQ, 420v iQ and 440v iQ, cleared on 9 September for a 2027 launch in the US [2][4]. The remaining items on that list are commercial floor cleaners and factory vehicles. Four of the seven came on or after 16 October, so the queue moves quickly, though not through the mower aisle. At the exact moment the market leader struggles to hold pricing in Europe, its four platforms are the only yard machines with permission to enter America.

## By the numbers

- **334 million krona — Quarterly loss:** the group operating result fell to a loss of 334 million krona from income of 141 million a year earlier, a -3.7 percent margin against 1.5 percent [1].
- **4 of 7 — Yard machines on the register:** the FCC lists seven robot approvals, and Husqvarna's four mowers are the only consumer yard gear among them [2].
- **65 percent — US content bar:** a mower dodges the list if it counts as US-made, meaning domestic parts above 65 percent of cost through 2028 and 75 percent from 2029 [3].
- **$800 to $2,500 — What buyers pay:** the mowers in Consumer Reports' current ratings run from about $800 to more than $2,500, against $400 to $800 for its top battery walk-behind mowers [5].

## What I'd watch:

The register changes by public notice, making the next entry the strongest signal for the upcoming season. An approval for a second mower brand would mean 2027 brings a choice rather than four variations on a single design.

- **The next name on the list:** a new brand appears on the federal register before it ever reaches a store [2].
- **A premium on the four:** a market with one approved supplier is the easiest place to maintain a high price [1].
- **Stock of approved models:** everything approved before 28 July can still be imported and sold, which is where this season's deals sit [3][5].
- **Any move against old approvals:** the FCC can stop the import of gear it already approved through a process in its own rules, used in June 2026 for older covered gear [2].

I am watching the December numbers to see if a competitor breaks the monopoly. The incumbent manufacturer is far from finished with its broader strategy. The group still expects 3 billion krona of gross savings by the end of 2028, with about 935 million booked so far [1]. America is also where it already sells the most professional robot mowers, with more than 500 golf courses on its machines and 24 percent of group sales booked there in 2025 [4].

## The catch

Consumer Reports tells readers the government banned the sale of foreign-made robots including robot mowers, then adds that the ban skips mowers already owned or already approved [5]. The agency rules are narrower: new models cannot get approval, while older approved models keep flowing to stores [3]. If you own one, or buy one this season, none of this affects you.

The looming scarcity sits entirely in next year's catalog rather than today's store aisles. My reading rests on a public list that shows one yard machine, but that register records approvals rather than applications, so nobody outside the government knows how many brands asked and were turned down. Because no source in this run publishes a competitor price, the idea that a price war will not arrive on schedule is a judgment about a queue, not a firm forecast. The rated acreage on a spec sheet is already a best-case figure, and [the gap between rated and usable area](/your-1-acre-mowers-04-acre-trap/) is the other place a buyer's math goes wrong.

## At a glance

**The shift:** New foreign-made robot mower models need their own US approval, and the register holds one consumer yard brand.
**The number:** Seven approvals, four of them since 16 October, and only Husqvarna's four mowers.
**The risk:** Waiting for a cheaper 2027 model is a bet on a queue you cannot see.
