## At a glance

A Black Friday sale has pushed Rision thermal cameras to gift prices for homeowners chasing winter heat loss, in the same month a lab-built cloak proved an infrared picture can be staged — and the maker's own guide calls the image a lead, not a verdict.

## The month physics learned to fake the picture

The cloak came first, on 26 October, in the journal Nature Communications [1]. Shelly Zhang's team at Illinois, with co-authors in Denmark, 3D-printed an aluminum lattice that conducts heat well, then cast a rubbery material into the gaps that conducts it badly [1]. The two materials together bend heat flow around whatever sits inside the shell. Put between a hot side and a cold side, the device read to an infrared camera "much as it would if no object were present" — and unlike earlier cloaks, it works from virtually any direction [1]. Zhang's framing is the part worth keeping: the work is about "hiding and protecting information that is carried by heat" [1].

I read that sentence twice, because it is a camera maker's competitor describing what a thermal image is made of. The picture is news about surfaces, not truth about structure. Nobody is hiding your missing insulation behind a printed shell. But it is now formally true that what a heat camera draws can be staged on purpose, in metal, from any angle.

Then came the price. On 19 November, the brand Rision opened a sale running to 30 November across its whole thermal camera line on Amazon and its own site [2]. The headline item is the Rision Magic, a 24-gram camera that plugs into a phone by USB-C and reads from −4°F to 1,112°F; its deal price is $109, cut 45% from the list price of $199 [2]. The standalone handheld with its own screen, the Torix, falls to $149 [2]. The release sells both straight at winter worry — cameras that reveal surface differences "around windows, doors and exterior walls, helping identify areas to check for air leaks or gaps in insulation" [2].

## What the people who read these images actually do

The vendor's own advice page is the first witness against its own sale. An unusual cold area "may suggest where to investigate, but further checks may be needed," the guide says, and "a cold spot alone doesn't prove that insulation is missing" [3]. It also quotes the Sustainable Energy Authority of Ireland on the roof: a home can lose up to 30% of its heat up there, where no casual scan of a bedroom reaches [3]. The company selling the camera is telling buyers the image nominates, and something else convicts.

Working inspectors say the same thing with case files attached. Guidance posted on 16 November restates what Britain's new-home warranty body, the NHBC Foundation, has long taught: a thermal image is a "map of surface temperature," useful only when indoor and outdoor air are far apart in temperature [4]. The same document records an owner who sealed the attic leaks the scan flagged, then found the infrared image looked barely different afterward [4]. The picture could not even score its own repairs. What I'm watching next is whether the flood of cheap cameras produces the matching wave of second-tool checks — the draft strip at a window, the smoke test at a gap — or a winter of homeowners acting on a screen number alone [4].

## The catch

The sale price is honest about one thing and silent about another. $109 is a fair price for a clue generator, and that is all the image has ever been. What changed this month is not the price. It is that physics now shows the picture can be erased with a printed shell, while the maker's own guide refuses to call it a diagnosis. My reading, held loosely: the camera earns its keep the day it flags a cold bay you would never have guessed, and stops being useful the day you let it replace the second tool.
