<!-- lead -->
Amazon said on September 24 it will spend more than $100 million on a 585,000-square-foot plant in Greenwood, Indiana, to build the equipment that powers its fulfillment and robotics network. Four days later, Walmart said it will spend more than $300 million on a fulfillment center in Turtlecreek Township, Ohio — a building for the televisions, furniture and other oversized goods that no picking robot handles today.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Both companies are buying the same thing — faster, cheaper order flow — and the two checks went to opposite places. Amazon is spending on the machine; Walmart is spending on the box around the goods the machine cannot lift.

I keep coming back to how close these two announcements landed. Amazon's came on Sept. 24 [1]; Walmart's came on Sept. 28 [2]. Read together, they say something neither release says on its own: automation payback is no longer one number you can spread across a network. It splits by what you are moving.

Amazon has already built more than one million robots in the United States. Those robots now work across more than 300 facilities and touch 75% of the orders Amazon delivers worldwide [1]. Making the machines in-house is how Amazon keeps the cost of each one falling — capital spent to make the next round of capital cheaper.

Walmart's $300 million went the other way. The Ohio site is aimed at "larger, non-sortable items, including televisions, furniture and other oversized merchandise" [2]. Those are the goods a robot grid cannot handle. An autonomous mobile robot (AMR) moves a tote, not a sofa. A fixed tower of automated storage and retrieval shelves holds small items, not a mattress. So Walmart is buying concrete and labor, not robot cells.

## By the numbers

- **$100M+ — Amazon's new robot plant:** Amazon will invest more than $100 million in a 585,000-square-foot Indiana factory to produce equipment for its fulfillment and robotics network [1].
- **1 million+ — Robots already built:** Amazon says it has made more than one million robots in the U.S., now working across more than 300 facilities and touching 75% of the orders it delivers [1].
- **$300M — Walmart's bulky-goods bet:** Walmart will invest more than $300 million in a Turtlecreek Township, Ohio, center for non-sortable goods, adding more than 300 jobs [2].
- **3.75% to 4% — The rate that re-prices payback:** The Federal Reserve raised rates a quarter point on Sept. 16, its first hike since 2023, lifting the hurdle for every long-payback system [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

My read is that the wage-inflation story that sold the last wave of automation does not decide this split. Stock-keeping unit (SKU) shape does. Uniform, fast-moving goods fit a robot cell; big-and-bulky goods still want a building and people.

- **Flexible fleets over fixed towers:** Operators renting AMRs under Robotics-as-a-Service (RaaS) keep the option to walk away; a fixed AS/RS tower ties the payback curve to one building.
- **Segment the model:** The teams I would back are cutting their return model per SKU class — robot-ready aisles, oversized aisles — instead of one blended automation number across the whole network.
- **The rate sets the bar:** With the Fed at 3.75% to 4% [3], the shortest payback wins the capital. That favours rented fleets and process discipline over multi-year fixed builds.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

I could be wrong, but the split is not clean. Walmart already runs robots in parts of its network, and Amazon's new plant makes components, not a finished warehouse. So this is a story about where each dollar lands, not about who is for automation and who is against it.

The honest limit: neither release gives a payback figure. The split is my reading of two capital decisions, not a number either company published.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** In the same week, Amazon put $100 million-plus into building its own robots while Walmart put $300 million into a building for the oversized goods those robots cannot pick.
- **Why It Matters:** Automation payback has split by SKU geometry, so one blended automation return across a network now misprices the capex and the capital it ties up.
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether operators start modelling payback per SKU class instead of per site.
  - **Flexible fleets:** Rented AMR fleets keep the exit open, so a bad bet is a lease, not a stranded tower.
  - **Per-SKU models:** Splitting the return model by robot-ready and oversized aisles, instead of one blended network number.
  - **The rate bar:** A 3.75% to 4% policy rate rewards the shortest payback, which favours rented automation over fixed builds.
- **The Catch:** Neither company published a payback figure, and Walmart already automates elsewhere — this is a read of two capital decisions, not a disclosed number.
