<!-- lead -->
Walmart told investors this week that its 30-minute deliveries almost tripled, because store staff can pick an order in about 15 minutes and still make the drive [1]. In the same week, Target said its shelves held more of what shoppers came for, up a full point against last year, with AI tools keeping stock in place [2]. Both retailers now make the service an order feels inside the store. That sits past the on-time in-full (OTIF) scorecard, which counts the orders a supplier delivers on time and complete, and never the pick.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
Walmart and Target reported earnings days apart, and both moved fulfillment into stores — Walmart with store-picked speed, Target with shelf-side stock. For operations leaders, the service target now sits past the dock, on a shelf line the supplier scorecard never reads.

<!-- tension -->
## Fulfillment moved into the store
The sequence is short. On November 19, Walmart reported the quarter ended October 31 [1]. US online sales grew 23%, and orders shipped from stores rose over 40% [1]. Rush deliveries rose more than 50% [1]. Walmart U.S. chief David Guggina then named the limit. The network reaches 60% of US homes within 30 minutes, "and mind you, that assumes a 15-minute pick time in the store" [1]. Pick faster, he said, and Walmart reaches more customers inside the promise [1]. Picking speed owns half of that 30-minute window.

Target made the same move two days earlier, from the shelf side. Its own earnings summary says shelf stock improved by a full point year over year [2]. The gains sat on the items shoppers buy most, where a gap hurts most [2]. The named causes are AI tools that "help teams improve availability and keep shelves stocked", plus store pay and training [2]. Target also said it is taking "a comprehensive look at how inventory moves through our entire network" [2].

What I keep coming back to is what neither call said. Stores fill over 80% of Walmart's US online orders, marketplace orders included [1]. So the promise a shopper feels rests on picking speed and shelf stock. Those are two levers a supplier neither staffs nor controls. A case that clears the distribution center on time can still sit on a shelf a picker cannot reach in 15 minutes. The remedy is spending on the retailer's side of the wall. Walmart picks faster [1]. Target buys shelf stock with AI and store pay [2]. No supplier term changed.

## By the numbers
- **15-minute pick:** The in-store time Walmart's 30-minute delivery promise assumes, on its own disclosure [1].
- **80% of US online orders:** The share Walmart fills from stores, marketplace orders included [1].
- **$70.6 billion inventory:** Walmart's global stock level, up 8.1% in the quarter that bought the speed [1].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## Where the real target now sits
The people closest to the story are repositioning. Walmart's CFO tied the speed gains to demand — "faster delivery speeds are leading to higher customer and member conversion" [1]. Speed is a growth lever, not a cost line, so expect the pick window to tighten rather than relax. Target framed its gain as the compounding return on store pay and training [2]. What I am watching is whether these levers surface in supplier terms. If the pick window becomes the gate, then case design and shelf-facing become part of the service an order experiences. The same-day delivery race already undid a decade of route-optimization economics once — see [The Same-Day Delivery Race Is Undoing a Decade of Route Optimization](/same-day-race-undoes-route-optimization/) — and a pick-window limit does the same to a dock-first OTIF program. Neither retailer has announced a new chargeback to match the store-side target. Target's fine mechanics remain the 3%-of-goods structure this desk covered on November 2 [3]. The pressure arrives as expectation, not yet as invoice.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch
The supplier-side metrics are not wrong. They measure the half of the promise the supplier can cause. A case delivered on time and in full did its job by the letter of the contract, and the dock-side trigger is still the one the retailer enforces. What I find genuinely unresolved is the drift between the two targets. Walmart raised picking speed on its side [1]. Target raised shelf stock on its side [2]. Neither asked suppliers to change anything. A supplier who reads a 98% scorecard as "service is fine" is reading a metric the shopper never sees. The hedge is cheap. Ask your retail accounts where the pick happens and whether cases ship shelf-ready. The answer is the first number of the new target.
