A renewal notice arrives with $420,000 of Coverage A — the dwelling limit — on a house that would cost $600,000 to rebuild this year. The owner has to decide whether to buy $60,000 more of limit or carry the difference another season, because nothing re-tests that number until someone re-runs the bill to rebuild. Most carriers ask for a dwelling limit worth 80 percent of replacement cost, the current price of rebuilding with like materials [1]. Below that line the policy is not cut by a flat penalty. It pays the greater of two numbers: the actual cash value of the damage, or the repair bill less the deductible, times the share of the 80 percent target the limit covers [4]. Here that second number is $57,500 × 0.875 = $50,312.50, so a limit at 70 percent of rebuild cost still pays 87.5 percent of a $60,000 repair, and leaves 12.5 percent of it unpaid.

## The line isn't on your policy

The test uses replacement value at the time of loss, not the value at the time the policy was written [3]. That one clause carries most of the risk. Rebuild costs move with lumber, labour and building code. The limit on the declarations page moves only when a renewal notice changes it. A house insured to the line in one year can sit under it a few years later, with no claim and no improvement.

The arithmetic is simple once the target is named. On this house the line sits at $480,000, and the limit covers 87.5 percent of it. My read is that the ratio is the part people miss. The same fraction applies to a small kitchen fire as to a burned-out second floor, so the penalty grows with the damage instead of arriving as a step.

Which of the two numbers binds depends on wear. Older work carries a low actual cash value, so the ratio is the larger number and it sets the payout. Newer work carries little depreciation, so actual cash value can come close to the repair bill and bind instead. Either way the carrier pays no more than the limit, and that matters most in the case nobody wants to run. A total loss pays $420,000 against a $600,000 rebuild, leaving $180,000 short, and no ratio softens it.

## By the numbers

- **$8,000 versus $5,000 — One roof, two settlements:** Texas's guide prices a $10,000 replacement; with a $2,000 deductible a replacement-cost policy pays $8,000, and the same roof at an actual cash value of $7,000 pays $5,000 [2].
- **75% — The ratio in a worked claim:** where the limit is three-quarters of the required amount, a $49,000 loss after the deductible settles at $36,750, and the remainder is the owner's [4][5].
- **$20,062 — Average paid claim (2023):** the industry's mean homeowners claim in 2023, at a frequency of 5.33 claims per 100 house-years, is the size of loss the ratio runs on day to day [6].

## Where the trade looks first

The people who price these policies start with a rebuild estimate, not the market value on the listing. Adjusting Today's analysis of the clause says why. Coverage is measured against what it costs to repair or replace with like kind and quality, which is not what a buyer would pay for the house and land together [4]. Two houses on one street can need different limits for that reason alone.

The second thing they check is the size of the correction. Moving the limit up to the line is a $60,000 increase in Coverage A, or 14.3 percent more dwelling coverage. It turns every partial loss from a discounted payout into a full one. What the extra cover costs in premium is a question for the carrier's rate sheet, and no regulator page on this beat prices it, so the owner weighs the increase against a quote.

What I keep circling is how little of the decision is about the disaster. A homeowner who clears the line buys a full payout on the small claims that actually arrive. The large claim is the one that would otherwise leave a gap. The site's [coverage-gap calculator](/calculators/insurance-coverage-gap/) runs the same ratio on a reader's own limit, which turns the clause into a number.

## The catch

Two limits on this analysis are worth stating plainly. The greater-of rule is the standard form's loss-settlement condition as Texas regulators summarised it and as the adjusting trade quotes it, and forms differ. The same order notes that one alternative form pays less when the owner elects not to repair, so the policy in hand governs, not the summary [3]. The clause also says nothing about what a carrier will charge for the extra limit, and a homeowner who clears the line pays for cover on a loss that may never come.

My read on the trade: the line is a floor for full replacement-cost settlement, not a recommendation. An owner can reasonably choose a lower limit and accept a proportional payout on ordinary damage. What is hard to defend is carrying the total-loss gap by accident, because that shortfall is the whole distance between the limit and the rebuild, and it arrives with no formula to argue about.

## At a glance

**The shift:** Below the 80 percent line, a claim settles on the greater of two numbers, not a flat penalty.
**The number:** A 70 percent limit still pays 87.5 percent of a partial repair.
**The risk:** The same limit leaves the total-loss gap uncovered.
