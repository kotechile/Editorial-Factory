<!-- lead -->
An American can leave a child **$15,480,000** before the federal estate tax applies. A former American who is a covered expatriate can hand the same child **$20,000** a year before a 40% tax falls on the child instead [1]. Both figures sit in one document, which the Service published on 5 November after indexing almost seventy provisions for 2027 [2]. Neither section mentions the other.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

- **The shift:** 2027's indexed figures set the year's ceiling on gifts from a former citizen.
- **The number:** the allowance is a thousandth of what a citizen may leave.
- **The risk:** the tax belongs to the heir, who may never hear of it.

<!-- tension -->
## Sixteen years, then a form

Congress added section 2801 to the tax code in 2008, to keep renunciation from doubling as an estate-tax exit [3]. Treasury wrote its first regulations only in January 2025, and a form to file with them appeared this year [5]. A tax with no form is a tax nobody files.

The rule is narrow. The section borrows the top rate from the estate-tax table, forty cents on the dollar, and applies it to what a US citizen or resident receives from a covered expatriate [4]. The person who receives pays, not the person who gave [3]. A house, a portfolio, a share of a business: whatever crosses to an American heir becomes a charge in the heir's hands, above a small annual allowance.

My reading is that this is the least discussed half of the exit decision. Departure is one event. You mark everything to market, take the exclusion the Service prints each year, file, pay, and finish [1]. Section 2801 does not finish. It waits for the next transfer, then bills the relative. That heir was not in the room for the choice, and may not learn of it until an accountant asks where the house came from.

The relief is real, and it matters more than the rate. Property shown on a timely filed gift or estate return of the expatriate sits outside the charge [3]. So do transfers that would carry a marital or charitable deduction, and any inheritance tax already paid abroad reduces what is owed. The section reaches value that escaped a transfer tax somewhere else.

<!-- by-the-numbers -->
## By the numbers

- **$20,000 a year:** the covered gifts and bequests one US person may receive before the charge applies [1].
- **$940,000:** the 2027 exclusion on the leaver's own assets, up $30,000 from this year [1].
- **$192,000:** the tax on a $500,000 covered bequest received in 2027, after the annual subtraction [1].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## Who the section reaches

Three tests decide whether a former citizen counts as a covered expatriate, and 2027 moved two of them. The first is average annual net income tax above **$218,000** across the five years before departure, up from $211,000 [7]. The second is net worth of **$2 million** or more, a figure the instructions print with no annual adjustment line while the income figure has one [5]. The third is failing to certify five years of clean tax filings on Form 8854. That test reaches people of modest means, because it turns on paperwork rather than wealth [6].

So the gate is not simply wealth. A careful family with a small estate can pass all of it; a disorganised one cannot.

The allowance itself edges up by rounding, a thousand dollars at a time, so it climbs far slower than the estate-tax exclusion above it. I keep landing on the calendar rather than the rate. Form 708 comes due eighteen months after the year of receipt, so the first returns, for anything received in 2025, land in June 2027 [5]. Late filing and late payment carry the ordinary penalties. A trust funded by a covered expatriate can spread the same charge across its distributions by a ratio the regulations define [8]. The lawyers who work this corner now ask two questions first: was the transferor a covered expatriate at that moment, and did the transfer appear on a filed return.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch

The distance between the two figures is deliberate. The section was written to leave a departing family roughly where it stood before it left, by collecting at the American end what the estate tax would have taken at the border [3]. The allowance is the small annual nod to that idea, tied by statute to the gift-tax exclusion so that it moves whenever that does.

None of that tells you how much is actually reported. No source I read measures filings under the section. And because property that appeared on the expatriate's own return is outside the charge, the answer turns on two facts an heir often cannot prove alone: who the transferor was at the moment of the transfer, and what was filed [3]. The figure to carry away is not the rate. It is the pair — a citizen's exemption and a former citizen's allowance — and the gap between them, which is what a family pays for a decision taken once, years earlier, by someone else. Italy's proposed charge on the same instinct, [billing 26% to leave](/italy-bills-26-to-leave/), now sits at the other end of the corridor.
