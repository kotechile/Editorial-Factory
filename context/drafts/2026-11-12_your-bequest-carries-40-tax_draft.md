<!-- lead -->
On 5 November the Internal Revenue Service published next year's inflation numbers, and two of them answer the same question from opposite ends. An American may leave a child **$15,480,000** before the federal estate tax applies [1]. A former American who is a covered expatriate may hand the same child **$20,000** a year before a 40% tax falls — on the child [1][3]. Both figures sit in one revenue procedure, six sections apart, and neither acknowledges the other. The second is the one that outlives the passport.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

- **The shift:** the 2027 adjustments set next year's ceiling on what a former citizen may give an American relative.
- **The numbers:** $20,000 a year per recipient, against $15,480,000 for a citizen's estate.
- **The risk:** the tax is the recipient's, and the transfer does not mention it.

<!-- tension -->
## Sixteen years, then a form

The statute has been on the books since 17 June 2008, when Congress added section 2801 to stop renunciation from doubling as an estate-tax exit [3]. It sat without regulations until 14 January 2025, when Treasury published the first set, and it sat without a return until this year's forms cycle [5][8]. Sixteen years and seven months is a long time for a tax to exist and not be collectable on paper.

What the section does is narrow and mechanical. It takes the top rate in the estate-tax table, which is 40%, and applies it to the value of a covered gift or bequest — property a US citizen or resident receives by gift from a covered expatriate, or by reason of that person's death [3][4]. The taxpayer is the person who receives, not the person who gave [3]. A $500,000 bequest in 2027 leaves $480,000 chargeable after the annual subtraction, which is $192,000 owed by the heir [1][3].

My reading is that this is the least discussed half of the exit decision. The departure itself is a single event: mark everything to market, take the $940,000 exclusion, file, pay, done [1]. Section 2801 does not end at that airport. It waits for the next transfer, and it bills the relative — usually an adult child who was not in the room when the decision was made, and who may not learn of it until a tax preparer asks where the house came from.

There are real exceptions, and they matter more than the headline rate. Property shown on a timely filed gift or estate tax return of the expatriate is outside the charge, as are transfers that would carry a marital or charitable deduction, and any gift or estate tax already paid to a foreign country reduces the bill [3]. The section is aimed at value that escaped a transfer tax somewhere, not at every euro that crosses a border in a will.

<!-- by-the-numbers -->
## By the numbers

- **$20,000 a year:** the aggregate covered gifts and bequests one US person may receive before section 2801 applies [1].
- **$940,000:** the 2027 exclusion on the leaver's own assets, up $30,000 from this year [1][7].
- **$192,000:** the tax on a $500,000 covered bequest received in 2027, after the annual subtraction [1][3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## Who the section reaches

Three tests decide whether a former citizen is a covered expatriate, and the 2027 numbers moved two of them. Average annual net income tax above **$218,000** over the five years before departure, up from $211,000 [1][7]. Net worth of **$2 million** or more, a figure the instructions print without an annual adjustment line while the income figure carries one [5][6]. Or a failure to certify five years of clean compliance on Form 8854 — the test that reaches people with modest assets, because it is about paperwork rather than wealth [6].

The 2027 gift ceiling is $1,000 higher than the $19,000 that applied in 2025 and 2026, and the instructions say that amount is indexed for later years [5]. It rises by rounding, a thousand at a time, which is how it ends up at 1/774th of the estate-tax exclusion printed above it [1].

What I am watching is the filing calendar rather than the rate. Form 708 is due on the fifteenth day of the eighteenth month after the year of receipt, which puts the first returns — for anything received in 2025 — on 15 June 2027 [5]. A recipient who files late faces the ordinary late-filing and late-payment penalties, and a trust funded by a covered expatriate can spread the same charge across its distributions by a ratio the regulations define [5][8]. The estate lawyers who work this corner now ask two questions before anything else: was the transferor a covered expatriate at the moment of the transfer, and did the transfer appear on a filed return [3].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch

The gap between the two numbers is not an oversight in the drafting. Section 2801 was written to put a departing family in roughly the position it would have occupied had it stayed: the estate tax it avoided at the border is collected when the money reaches American hands [3]. The $20,000 is the small annual allowance for that, tied by statute to the gift-tax exclusion and therefore indexed the same way [1][3].

The honest limit is that none of this tells you how much is actually reported. Nothing in the record I read measures filings under the section, and the relief for property that appeared on the expatriate's own return means the charge depends on two facts a recipient often cannot establish alone: who the transferor was, and what was filed [3][5]. The number worth carrying away is not 40%. It is the pair — $15,480,000 and $20,000 — and the distance between them, which is the price a family pays for a decision made once, years earlier, by someone else. The departure pricing Italy proposed this month is the same instinct at the other end of the corridor: whoever leaves first now gets an invoice, and whoever inherits second gets the rest [9].
