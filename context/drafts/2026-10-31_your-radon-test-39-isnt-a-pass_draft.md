<!-- lead -->
A kit that comes back 3.9 picocuries per liter is not a pass, even though it sits under the number every homeowner quotes. The federal action line is 4 pCi/L, and the guidance that sets it says a house reading between 2 and 4 should consider fixing [2]. That band is narrower than the wobble in a two-day sample, which is why the reading matters less than the way it was taken. New York State sells a laboratory-analyzed kit for $12.25, and its instructions start with sealing the house, because the test is meant to be a time average rather than a snapshot [3].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

My read starts with what the device is actually measuring. Radon is soil gas that seeps in at the lowest part of a house, and the indoor concentration is a tug of war between how much seeps in and how much fresh air dilutes it.

That is why ventilation moves the number so easily, and why the effect dies the moment you close up. New York's mitigation guidance records that once windows, doors and vents are closed, radon concentrations most often return to previous values within about 12 hours [4]. A test run with a window cracked is not a low reading of your house. It is a reading of a different house, one you ventilated on purpose, and it will not reproduce next winter.

This is the same lesson as [an imager that needs a 15-degree spread](/your-250-imager-needs-15-degrees/): the instrument is not the problem, the conditions are. Three agencies agree on the shape of the protocol and disagree on its clock. New York's consumer page says doors and windows closed 24 hours before and during the test [3], while the Lung Association's kit instructions start closed-house conditions at least 12 hours prior [7]. Pick the stricter number and every instruction is satisfied.

## By the numbers

- **2,900 — Deaths among people who never smoked:** the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) puts radon lung-cancer deaths at about 21,000 a year, and roughly 2,900 of those are people who never smoked [1].
- **1.3 pCi/L — Risk under the line:** at a third of the action level, a never-smoker's lifetime lung-cancer risk is about 2 in 1,000, against 20 in 1,000 for a smoker at the same exposure [1].
- **two to seven days — Short-term sample:** the standard short-term test runs two to seven days, and the collector sits in the lowest level the family actually uses [7].
- **less than $1,000 — Quote floor:** Minnesota's health department warns that mitigation rates under $1,000 are often too good to be true, and that the installs behind them come with sub-standard workmanship [5].

## What I'd watch:

The decision made in a kitchen is usually "kit or monitor", and the protocol settles that before the hardware does. What I keep circling is how little the device brand changes the answer. A passive collector posted to a laboratory is a time average, and that is what the action line was written against.

- **What a lab does that a screen does not:** the $12.25 state kit collects passively and comes back analyzed [3], so the number covers the whole exposure rather than one moment on a counter.
- **The clock in the instructions:** seal for 24 hours before and during the test [3], which also satisfies the shorter 12-hour start in the published kit guidance [7].
- **Where it sits:** the lowest level regularly used, away from drafts and disturbance [7]. A basement you never enter is not the level the family breathes.
- **The install clock:** after a system goes in, retest within 30 days but no sooner than 24 hours with the fan running [4], then every two years; with no system, every five years [3].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

The honest limit is that one short test is one season. Both the state guidance and the Lung Association treat a short sample as an answer to "should I test again", not as a statement of the level the house holds all year [7]. A long-term test takes at least three months, and most people will not wait.

I also cannot price the fix for you, and neither page will. The health departments give a floor below which a quote should be treated as a warning [5], but no citable dollar range for a proper install, so three bids is the only responsible instruction and a single "average cost" figure is a guess wearing a number.

The last thing worth sitting with is what a sloppy test actually buys. A reading taken with the house open is not neutral; it is confident and wrong, and it will be repeated every time someone reruns the test the same way.

## At a glance

**The shift:** A radon number is being read as a verdict on a house when it is a sampling result.
**The number:** 4 pCi/L is the fix line, and 2 to 4 is "consider fixing".
**The risk:** A ventilated test reads low, then the level returns within about 12 hours of closing up.
