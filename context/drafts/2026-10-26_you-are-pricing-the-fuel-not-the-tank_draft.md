<!-- lead -->
The quote in your hand names a fuel and a tank size, and both are the wrong numbers. A 50-gallon gas water heater sold today can meet its federal efficiency floor at a score of 0.56, and that one number turns 2025's average home gas price of $15.34 per thousand cubic feet into about $26.26 for every million Btu of hot water the tank makes. The same heat costs about $22.04 from a heat pump tank running at the floor that starts on May 6, 2029, and a standard electric tank at today's floor costs $55 for the same heat, or $55.07 on the 2025 average price. The class does more work than the fuel.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Water heating runs all year, and it is the load where the equipment's own efficiency gap is widest. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) puts water heating, lighting and cooling together at 25% of home energy use in 2020, against 52% for heating and air conditioning. So the tank is a smaller slice of the bill than most owners assume, and a much larger slice of the loss they can fix.

The federal floors are where the gap shows. A mid-draw gas tank of 20 to 55 gallons, made before May 6, 2029, must score 0.6483 minus 0.0017 times its rated gallons, which is 0.5633 at 50 gallons [3]. A standard electric tank in the same band must score 0.9307 minus 0.0002 times gallons, or 0.9207 at 50 gallons [4]. From May 6, 2029 that electric floor jumps to 2.30 at every draw pattern, a level that plain heating elements cannot reach [5]. The rule is dated on manufacture, not on sale or setup.

My read is that this is the rare energy choice where the physics is better than the pitch suggests, and the pitch is where the money leaks. A tank replacement is also the moment [the big gas units' venting trap](/your-75000-btu-tank-venting-trap/) already covered, and it arrives once.

## By the numbers

- **$26.26 — Gas tank, per million Btu:** 2025 gas at $15.34 a thousand cubic feet is $14.79 a million Btu of fuel, and dividing by the 0.5633 floor gives $26.26 of hot water [1][3][7]. That division is the desk's own.
- **$22.04 — Heat pump tank, per million Btu:** 17.30 cents a kilowatt hour is $50.70 a million Btu of power, and dividing by the 2.30 floor for units made from May 2029 gives $22.04 [2][5][6].
- **$55.07 — Standard electric tank, per million Btu:** the same power divided by today's 0.9207 floor for a 50-gallon unit, which is 2.10 times the gas tank's cost [2][4][6].
- **20.6 cents — The break-even price:** the heat pump tank and the gas tank cost the same when home power reaches about 20.6 cents a kilowatt hour at $15.34 gas, and gas wins below about $12.88 a thousand cubic feet [1][2][3][5][7].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The quote is where the class gets chosen, and its wording says which comparison is being made.

- **The score line, not the fuel line:** a quote that reads "50-gallon gas, same as yours" is selling a class, and the class has a number on it. What I would want to see is that score printed beside the model, because the floor and the shelf unit are rarely the same figure [3].
- **The room a heat pump needs:** 1,000 cubic feet of air in a space that stays between 40 and 90 degrees all year, with a program warning that colder spots cut efficiency and can force a standard electric backup [8]. A closet or a cold garage corner is the usual block, and that is a fact about the house, not the box.
- **The local power price:** the 20.6-cent break-even moves with the reader's own rate, so a household well above the national average gets a different answer from the same two floors [2][5].
- **The 2029 date as a repair fact:** a standard electric tank fitted in 2026 is the last of its class to be made new, which matters when the same owner weighs a repair against a replacement in three years [5].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The honest limit is that the gap is narrow. The heat pump tank at the 2029 floor beats the gas tank by about 16%, and gas takes the lead back on any day when gas is cheap next to power. The break-even sits near the national average, so the answer really does differ by state [1][2][3][5].

I could be wrong about how much a household should weight that, because no row here carries a fitted price, and without one there is no payback in dollars to quote.

What the numbers do settle is the class to refuse. A standard electric tank at 0.9207 costs more than twice the gas tank per million Btu of heat, and no price in this set closes a 2.10-times gap. The part I keep circling is that the same tank is the cheapest box on the shelf, which is why it keeps going into houses that will carry the bill for a decade [2][4][6].

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The efficiency class a water heater has to meet does more to set the bill than the fuel it burns, and the class changes on May 6, 2029, when new electric tanks must hit a score of 2.30, a heat pump level.
- **Why It Matters:** At 2025 prices the same million Btu of hot water costs about $26.26 from a 50-gallon gas tank, $22.04 from a heat pump tank at the 2029 floor and $55.07 from a standard electric tank, so the class chosen at replacement sets a decade of bills.
- **What I'd Watch:** Two published prices and two legal floors now decide the answer, and the state spread is where it moves.
  - **The break-even price:** the power rate at which the heat pump tank and the gas tank cost the same, about 20.6 cents a kilowatt hour at 2025 gas prices.
  - **The room it needs:** the 1,000 cubic feet of air and the 40-to-90-degree range a heat pump needs to run at its rating.
  - **The May 2029 floor:** the date the electric class must reach 2.30, which ends the standard electric tank as a new option.
- **The Catch:** The heat pump edge is about 16% per million Btu of heat, not the halving the sales talk implies, and no source here carries a fitted price, so there is no dollar payback to promise.
