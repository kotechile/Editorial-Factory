<!-- lead -->
Your addition never meets the 3% cap. Florida's statute is close to blunt. A change, addition or improvement is valued at full market value on the first January 1 after it is substantially complete [1]. Only then does the state's three percent limit start to work on the total [2].

Michigan writes the same rule as arithmetic. Take last year's taxable value. Subtract losses. Multiply by the lesser of 1.05 or the inflation rate. Then add "all additions" [3].

Those two words carry the piece. The cap protects the number already on the roll. New work is bolted on top at full value, before any limit applies. The cap never covers the addition. It covers the base the addition joins.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Every capped state sells its cap as shelter for the long owner. On the existing value, it is. Oregon multiplies the prior year's assessed value by 103 percent and stops [4]. California compounds a base value by a factor that "in no event" tops two percent [5]. Florida takes the lower of three percent or the consumer price index change [2].

Then each state writes itself an exception for new work. California hands new construction a fresh base year. It counts any addition since the last lien date, or a change that is a "major rehabilitation" or that switches the use [6]. Oregon values new improvements at real market value and adds them in [7]. Florida values the change at full value, then tells the appraiser to correct later years if that date was missed [1].

Michigan says it plainest. "Additions" is a defined word. For taxes after 1994 it includes new construction [8]. The same section then narrows new construction to property "not in existence on the immediately preceding tax day and not replacement construction" [8].

Read that twice. A new sunroom did not exist last tax day. A roof, a condenser, a water heater — replacement work — is carved out by name. The statute that adds your addition to the roll says the work that keeps rain out of your walls is not the thing it adds.

My read: that carve-out is worth more to a capital plan than most resale tables. The projects this desk has priced before are the ones that get added. The mechanical and envelope renewals that cut the annual bill sit outside the same definition.

I keep coming back to the household that budgets the bid and stops there. The bid is one number. The carrying cost is a second one. It repeats every year the house is owned.

## By the numbers

- **Three percent — Florida's cap:** A homestead's assessed value cannot rise by more than the lower of three percent of the prior year's value or that year's consumer price index change. The improvement is valued at full market value before that limit resumes [2][1].
- **1.05 — Michigan's multiplier:** Taxable value is the prior year's taxable value minus losses, times the lesser of 1.05 or the inflation rate, "plus all additions" [3].
- **103 percent — Oregon's ceiling:** Maximum assessed value equals 103 percent of the prior year's assessed value, or 100 percent of the prior maximum, whichever is greater. New improvements enter at real market value [4][7].
- **Two percent — California's inflation factor:** The yearly adjustment to a base value may not top two percent of the prior year's value. New construction gets a new base year [5][6].

## What I'd watch:

- **The date the work finishes.** California's liens attach at 12:01 a.m. on January 1 [9]. Florida measures the first January 1 after the work is substantially complete [1]. December and January do not land in the same tax year.
- **Where the line sits.** A like-for-like swap is not an "addition" and not a "major rehabilitation" under California's definition [6]. A partial gut is the hard case. I would want that answer before the permit, not after.
- **The correction runs backward.** Florida does not just let the appraiser catch a missed improvement. It requires full value to be set for the year the work was complete, with later years corrected and back taxes figured under a separate section [1].
- **What a renewal is worth on the other side.** The same Michigan section that adds new construction leaves replacement work out [8]. I would want to know how many counties apply that to a full mechanical swap.

## The catch

Four statutes are not fifty. This piece rests on California, Florida, Oregon and Michigan because those were the primary texts I could fetch and read in full. They are not a national sample. A state with no cap has no drift to protect, and the full value of the work simply shows up.

The step is smaller than it sounds at first. The added value is counted once, at the county's rate. The cap then sets how fast that line grows, by two to five percent a year depending on the state. The number that matters is rate times added value, every year. It is not a share of the build cost.

I could be wrong about how much of this lands in practice. County rolls are not uniform. Work can move a value for reasons that have nothing to do with these definitions. What the statutes settle is the frame. The cap governs the base you already have. The improvement arrives at full value before it joins that base.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** In every capped state the yearly limit applies to last year's value, and a completed improvement is added on top at full market value. The cap limits the drift. The addition is not capped.
- **Why It Matters:** A permitted addition carries a cost that never sunsets: the county's rate on the added value, every year, growing at the state cap. That line is missing from the payback model most households build on the bid and the resale table.
- **What I'd Watch:** I am watching how these definitions get applied, because they decide which capital is assessable.
  - **Completion date:** The January 1 lien date in California and the first January 1 after substantial completion in Florida decide which tax year the step lands in.
  - **The line:** California reassesses an addition, a major rehabilitation, or a change of use. A like-for-like replacement is none of those.
  - **Replacement carve-out:** Michigan's own definition of new construction leaves replacement work out, which is the statutory version of the renewal-versus-addition split.
- **The Catch:** Four statutes are not a national survey. A state with no cap has nothing to protect, and county practice is not uniform. The statutes set the frame. The assessor sets the number.
