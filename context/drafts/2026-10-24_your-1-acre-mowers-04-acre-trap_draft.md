<!-- lead -->
Three numbers sit on one product page, and they do not agree. Husqvarna's Automower 420 iQ lists a lawn size of 1 acre with a ±20% tolerance. The same line gives an area capacity of 1 acre for systematic mowing and 0.5 acre for irregular mowing [1]. Take the bottom of the band and the irregular figure together, both of them the vendor's own, and the machine sold as a 1-acre mower covers 0.4 acres on a lot with a shed, a hedge line and a swing set on it. The machine lists at $3,299.99 [1]. That gap is the trap: a rating is a best case, and nobody quotes the worst one.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

I read the spec table as a duty-cycle claim dressed up as a measurement. Nothing on that page is a field trial. It says what the machine will hold in a busy yard, and the columns on either side of the headline say how much slack is built into the claim.

On the 420 iQ it cuts for 84 minutes and charges for 54 [1]. That is 60.9% of its own clock spent mowing. It also explains why the schedule matters more than the acreage on the marketing tile. A robot that must recharge every hour and a half spreads a full cut across the daylight, not across one afternoon.

Navimow makes the same admission on a different basis. Its sizing guide gives mowing area per charge against a recommended lawn size: 265 square metres per charge on the i208 LiDAR, a model sold for 800 to 2,000 square metres of lawn [3]. That is a three-fold gap at the bottom of its band, and 7.5-fold at the top. The buyer only gets the bigger number if the machine runs through several charge cycles in a day.

The price ladder runs the other way, and it is where the money hides. Kress lists a quarter-acre unit at $2,799.98 and a seven-acre unit at $15,699.98 [4]. Per rated acre that is $11,199.92 against $2,242.85, so the smallest model is five times dearer per acre than the largest. Going from a quarter-acre rating to a one-and-a-quarter-acre rating costs $1,400, about 50% more money for five times the capacity [4]. A buyer with 0.6 acres is often quoted the rung that is worst value per acre.

The slope column carries its own split, and the smaller number is buried in the table. The 420 iQ is rated for a 15% maximum slope at the boundary and 45% inside the mowing area [1]. The 435 iQ, the all-wheel-drive model, prints 50% at the boundary against 70% inside [2]. Navimow's ladder rises by model, from 45% on the i2 to 55% on the LiDAR Pro and 84% on the X4 [3]. The slopes people quote are the inside-the-yard ones. The vendor's own help answer manages the same trick, describing the 420 iQ as able to handle "steep slopes of up to 45% (approximately 24 degrees) inside the boundary area" and leaving the boundary line out of the sentence [1].

## By the numbers

- **0.4 acres — Derated capacity:** A 1-acre rating at the floor of the ±20% band, times the irregular-mowing column, is 0.40 acres of usable capacity on a cluttered lot [1].
- **15% — Boundary slope:** The 420 iQ's maximum slope at the boundary, against 45% inside the mowing area, in the same row of the table [1].
- **$8,249.97 — Cost per usable acre:** $3,299.99 divided by 0.40 usable acres, against $3,299.99 per systematic acre [1].
- **$11,199.92 to $2,242.85 — Price per rated acre:** Kress's quarter-acre tier against its seven-acre tier, a five-fold spread across one brand's ladder [4].

## What I'd watch:

My eye went to the place where a vendor's own answer contradicts its own spec line. The 435 iQ's table says 1.3 acres with a ±20% tolerance and 0.9 acres irregular. The help answer on the same page says the machine "is designed to manage complex lawns and rough terrain up to 0.9 acres in size" [2]. Both numbers belong to the vendor. That gap is where sizing rows start with a dealer, and it is worth having both figures on one page before a quote is signed.

- **Where the consumable clock runs:** Blade sets are rated at 500 to 1,000 mowing hours per set, not in months [1]. A machine that cuts 60% of the day burns through that interval faster than its owner expects, because the hour meter runs while the calendar does not.
- **What a dated sale hides:** Kress's discounted rows carry a printed cutoff of December 31, 2026, or while supplies last [4]. A durable buying call should ride on the list price, since the sale row will be gone before this article is old.
- **Where the slope number comes from:** On the Navimow range the same terrace moves from 45% to 55% to 84% as the model tier rises [3]. When a number behaves like an upgrade, my read is that it is a product-tier feature rather than a property of the category.

## The catch

The honest limit is that every figure here is a vendor claim. No page I could reach publishes a measured coverage test, a timed run over a real lot, or an independent slope trial. The 0.4-acre figure is arithmetic on two columns the vendor chose to print, not a result from a machine on grass.

On a clean rectangular lawn the systematic column is the fair one, and the 1-acre rating holds up well enough that the derating looks unkind. The ±20% band cuts both ways too: at the top of it the same machine is a 1.2-acre machine on a tidy lot. The buyer's problem is that the band never says which end their lot lands on.

The boundary slope figure needs its own caveat. The table prints it without saying which task it governs, whether it limits where the boundary can be drawn or how the machine crosses the line. I am not going to invent a mechanism the page does not state. What I can say is that it is a third of the headline slope on the 420 iQ and 20 points lower on the 435 iQ, and that a lot whose boundary crosses a bank is where the gap bites.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Robotic mowers are sold on an acreage rating that the same spec table qualifies with a ±20% tolerance and an irregular-mowing figure at half the headline, so the number on the box is a best case rather than a capacity.
- **Why It Matters:** The derating changes the money. A $3,299.99 machine rated at 1 acre is $8,249.97 per usable acre on a cluttered lot, and the small rungs of a price ladder can run five times dearer per rated acre than the large ones.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The vendor's own help answer:** Where a page quotes a smaller area than its spec line, as the 435 iQ does at 0.9 against 1.3 acres.
  - **The boundary column:** The lower of the two printed slope figures, which sits at a third of the headline on the 420 iQ.
  - **The duty cycle:** Mowing time against charging time, which decides how much of a day a full cut really takes.
- **The Catch:** Every figure is a maker's claim with no independent coverage test behind it, and the tolerance band means a tidy, simple lawn can still get close to the headline acreage.
