<!-- lead -->
A contractor who offers to take the permit fee off a bid is usually offering more: a form signed as owner-builder, which puts the builder's job in your name. The swap looks small because the fee is small, and the catch stays hidden until it costs someone a house deal. California ties one version of the exemption to a home the owner has lived in for the 12 months before the work ended [2]. Florida lets an owner build a one-family or two-family home for their own use, and a commercial building only where the cost stays at or below $75,000 [5]. What moves across the counter is the watching, the hiring and the insurance.

<!-- tension -->

## What the signature transfers

The exemption is not a licence. It is a hole cut in a licensing law, and each state cuts it differently.

California's simplest route covers only work the owner does in person, or work done by the owner's own staff with wages as their only pay [3]. A framing crew paid by the square foot is neither, so the exemption ends as soon as one shows up. That is often the real shape of the deal: the contractor stays off the paperwork and sends his trades anyway.

Florida wants the owner to watch every part of the job not done by a licensed contractor [6], and to sign the form in person [7]. Its own disclosure then says how the pattern looks from the state's side. Unlicensed people often have the owner get the permit, it reads, which implies the owner is supplying the labour and the supplies [8].

That page is blunter about the cost. A worker without a licence has to be on the owner's payroll, which brings tax withholding and injury insurance [8], and the owner's home insurance may not pay for injuries to those workers [8].

I keep circling that last line. It is the state saying the gap is real, and the bid never mentions it.

## By the numbers

- **Four structures a year — California's resale cap:** An owner who hires licensed trades directly is exempt only up to four single-family structures offered for sale in a calendar year, and the route closes once a general contractor is hired [1].
- **Three structures — Oregon's limit:** Oregon exempts an owner who hires licensed contractors on not more than three existing homes in the same year, but not for work that needs a permit unless a residential general contractor runs it [10][11].
- **Five hundred dollars — Washington's floor:** Washington calls work casual and minor only where the whole job lands under five hundred dollars, and the same rule drops any job that is part of a bigger one [13].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## Where the resale catches up

The resale rules decide whether the exemption lives past the project. California treats a sale, or even a listing, within a year of completion as an assumption that the house was built to sell [4]. The owner can argue with that one, and the burden is his.

Five or more homes sold inside the same year is a hard assumption in California, one the owner cannot argue with [16]. Florida writes its version into the form the owner signs: a home sold or leased inside a year is presumed built for sale or lease, which breaks the exemption [9].

Oregon comes at the same fact from the other end. An owner who does not move into the finished home has given evidence, on its face, of a plan to sell [12]. Washington sets a clock instead: the exemption does not reach anyone doing builder work to lease or sell land owned for under twelve months [14], and it is withheld outright when the work is aimed at selling, tearing down or leasing the property [15].

What I am watching next is how those clauses meet a normal market. An owner who adds a wing and stays for ten years never meets one. The one who takes a job transfer two summers later does, and the form signed at the counter decides which story the state believes.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

None of this makes the form wrong to sign. An owner who does the work, lives in the house and stays is the person these rules were written for, and the statutes read as kind to that owner.

The harm sits in the middle case. An owner signs, hires the trades the contractor picked, and never learns if those trades were licensed or on payroll. The statutes do not chase that deal. They just stop covering it.

I could be wrong, but I read the disclosure pages as the honest paper in the pile, because they are the only ones that describe the role instead of the fee. The other risk, work done with no permit at all, runs the other way, and this site's [unpermitted work calculator](/calculators/unpermitted-work/) works the permit-now, fix-later and sell-as-is math.

## At a glance

**The shift:** An owner-builder permit moves the builder's role onto the owner, with watching, hiring and resale conditions attached.
**The number:** Four structures a year in California, three in Oregon, five hundred dollars of casual work in Washington.
**The risk:** A resale inside the window turns the exemption into evidence of unlicensed building.
