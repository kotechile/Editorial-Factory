From January 1, a California city must approve your second backyard home: two separate cottages on one lot, checked against fixed rules and passed without a hearing. Three weeks after the governor signed that bill, the state's contractor board charged two brothers behind a failed backyard builder with 35 felonies. More than 200 owners in 18 counties had complained about that firm. The risk the board's release leaves in place is the payment rule it repeated: a deposit of no more than $1,000, then progress payments only for work already done or goods already delivered.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Assembly Bill (AB) 956 does not add a fifth home to a California lot. The promised total stays four: a house with two detached cottages and a converted unit, or a house with one cottage and the in-law suite inside it. What the law changes is which units a city must approve [4]. From January 1 the state promises two detached, new builds, each with four-foot side and rear setbacks. Once both are up, a city no longer has to allow a junior accessory dwelling unit (JADU) on that lot.

On paper that swap looks like a footnote. On the ground it is not. A JADU is a room in the house with a kitchenette. The new version is two separate buildings, each with its own slab, panel, water line and set of checks. California already issues these units at a pace that surprises people: 30,354 permits in 2024 against 1,336 in 2016, and they were over 26.6% of all homes permitted in the state that year [3].

What strikes me is that none of this touches the money. The act amends three code sections, all about zoning, deed limits and local approval, and none about payment [4]. The newest by-right job in the state still runs on a payment rule written for a kitchen remodel, and the board used its October release to remind builders what that rule says [1].

## By the numbers

- **$1,000 — Deposit cap:** The board repeated the rule as a lesser-of pair, no more than $1,000 or 10% of the job's total cost, with later draws capped at the value of work done or goods delivered [1].
- **35 felony counts — Alleged theft:** Colton and Austin Paulhus each face 33 counts of taking job funds, one of conspiracy and one of wage theft, after a board probe that led to a criminal case [1].
- **200+ complaints — 18 counties:** The board's file lists abandoned jobs, unpaid subs, and cash paid ahead of work that was done, or often not done at all [1].
- **26.6% — Share of permits:** These units ran from 1,336 permits in 2016 to 30,354 in 2024, and were over a quarter of homes permitted in the state in 2024 [3].

## What I'd watch:

The gap this case shows is not a missing law. It is a law that shows up after the money is gone. The board pulled the firm's license in November 2024. The charges surfaced on October 9, nearly two years later, and the next court date is January 12 [1][5]. An owner who pays a deposit in March is not waiting on that clock.

- **The first city rules:** A city may cap each cottage at 800 square feet of living space, and many will, because the cap is the cheapest way to stop a two-unit lot from turning into a small apartment block [4].
- **Fire walls, not state law:** The statute sets four-foot setbacks and says nothing about the gap between two buildings. Fire-rated walls and fire-truck access still decide whether a plan fits a 50-by-120-foot lot.
- **What a builder asks for at signing:** The board's own sample schedule starts at 5% for start-up and ends at 2% on key handover, a useful yardstick for any request that lands before the first site check [2].
- **Unpaid subs:** In the Anchored complaints, suppliers went unpaid while owners had already paid, the pattern that puts a lien on a house whose owner did nothing wrong. [The lien clock behind remodel payments](/mechanics-lien-clock-payment-release/) is the part of that failure that outlives the builder.

## The catch

Two things cut against the scary reading. The first is that this is one firm, and the charges are just charges: no plea had been entered at the October 14 hearing, and the brothers are presumed innocent while the case runs [6]. The second is that a deposit cap is not a test of whether a builder has money. A firm that cannot carry two cottages to the first draw is exactly the firm that wants a big deposit, and no statute turns a thin balance sheet into a thick one.

My read is that the useful control here is about order, not law. A draw schedule tied to site checks, a licensed general contractor with enough cash to reach them, and a written deal that names each payment step do more than a complaint filed after the fact. The state has spent a decade making the permit easier and has not made the money safer. AB 956 pushes more backyard work into the version of the job with the most cash out before the first check.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** From January 1, 2027, California cities must approve two detached backyard homes on a single-family lot, trading the promised in-house junior unit for a second cottage [4].
- **Why It Matters:** The payment rule did not change, so a two-building job still runs on a deposit capped at $1,000 or 10% and draws tied to finished work [1].
- **What I'd Watch:** How the first city rules use the 800-square-foot cap, what builders ask for at signing, and the January 12 court date in the state's largest backyard-home fraud case.
  - **Ministerial approval:** A decision made against fixed rules, with no hearing and no room to weigh a neighbor's objection.
  - **Draw schedule:** The list of payments tied to finished stages, the mechanism that keeps money behind work.
  - **Construction fund charge:** The count filed when money paid for one job is used for something else.
- **The Catch:** The case is one firm and the charges are unproven, a deposit cap cannot make a weak builder solvent, and a two-unit lot adds fire-wall and utility costs that no state statute estimates.
