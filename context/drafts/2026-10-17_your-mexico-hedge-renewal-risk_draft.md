<!-- lead -->
On October 2, USTR (the Office of the U.S. Trade Representative) opened a public docket on whether the North American trade rules behind your Mexico sourcing hedge should keep working. Written comments are due January 12, 2027. That deadline is the first renewal risk in a rule that now gets reviewed every year.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

I keep coming back to one thing: both escape routes from China exposure changed their legal form this month, in opposite directions.

The Mexico case never rested on a duty rate. It rested on the belief that the trade deal underneath it would stay put. The Federal Register notice published on October 5 records what happened at the July 1 meeting of the deal's Free Trade Commission. "The United States did not agree to extend the Agreement for a new 16-year term," it says. Without an extension, the text ends on July 1, 2036. Until then, the Commission meets to review it every year [1].

That changes the math of a sourcing decision. A preference reviewed each year can be reopened each year. The review also carries live enforcement. On October 8, USTR invoked the deal's Rapid Response Labor Mechanism at an auto-parts plant in Puebla, Mexico. It also told Treasury to hold that plant's imports open instead of closing them out [3]. That is a cash freeze at one plant. No new duty rate is needed to hurt.

The onshore option moved too. It did not move toward independence. On October 14, GlobalFoundries said it will run two new fabs at its Malta, New York site under a U.S.-Japan plan. Japan pays, up to $21 billion. GlobalFoundries brings the process technology and runs the plants. In a separate deal, the Commerce Department takes about four percent of the company [2].

**By the numbers:**

- **Up to $21 billion — Japan's fab funding:** about $10 billion would speed a first 300mm fab and about $10 billion would fund a second. The sum is a ceiling, not a promise [2].
- **900,000 wafers — Yearly capacity per fab:** each fab should add about 900,000 300mm wafers a year at full output. The first is due online in 2029 [2].
- **Four percent — U.S. equity stake:** the Commerce Department stake sits in a separate deal, not in the fab funding [2].
- **January 12, 2027 — Review comment deadline:** written comments and hearing requests for the 2027 review are due at 11:59 p.m. Eastern that day [1].

## What I'd watch:

The people closest to this are not arguing about rates. My read is that they are arguing about how long each option lasts. Three signals answer that next.

- **Who files in the review docket:** the notice asks for comment on the investment climate in North America and on compliance. Filings from importers will show if the yearly review becomes routine or a real fight.
- **Whether the freeze spreads:** the Puebla action covers one plant. One plant is enough to test whether buyers start pricing Mexico risk at the plant instead of the border.
- **The 2029 gap:** the first wafer from Malta is three years out, by the company's own timeline. Until then, onshore capacity is a promise. A promise does not cover a line that ships in 2027.
- **The memory squeeze below it:** Supply Chain Dive, citing Omdia research, reports memory prices up to 400% higher over two years [4]. That cost lands on the same buyers who pick the next line.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

The honest limit is that a yearly review is not an exit. The deal stays in force. Its duty-free terms stay in force. The 2036 date is a decade away. What changed is the cost of assuming the ground will not move, not the rate paid today. I could be wrong, but I do not think a comment docket reopens a sourcing plan this quarter.

The same restraint applies on the other side. Four percent is not control. The $21 billion is a cap for two plants that make nothing yet. The state does not set GlobalFoundries' prices.

Where I've landed is narrow. The two options now run on different clocks. Mexico's preference is reviewed each year. The onshore line shows up in 2029, and its money has a government at both ends. A landed-cost model that holds both fixed is comparing a licence to a building site.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The U.S. declined in July to extend the North American trade deal for another 16 years. The deal is now reviewed each year, and the first comment docket is open until January 12, 2027. In the same fortnight, the largest onshore fab news of the month came with Japanese money and a U.S. stake in the operator.
- **Why It Matters:** Both hedges against China exposure now carry political terms. Nearshoring rests on a preference that renews each year. Reshoring rests on capacity that arrives in 2029 and is paid for by governments.
- **What I'd Watch:** I'm watching the review docket, the plant-level freezes in Mexico, and the gap between the 2029 fab date and schedules that will not wait.
  - **Review filings:** comments under the 2027 review show if importers treat the yearly cycle as routine or as a fight.
  - **Held entries:** an import held open is not final, so what is owed on it stays open while a labor case runs.
  - **Fab dates:** a first wafer in 2029 is the earliest onshore output from this deal, so it cannot cover near-term volume.
- **The Catch:** A yearly review is not an exit and four percent is not control. The deal and its rates stand today, and the onshore capacity is still a plan.
