<!-- lead -->
Your air rule — air freight is said to earn its keep above $15 to $20 of cargo value a kilo — is about $195 short of the break-even, and that gap is the whole decision. The flight pays for itself across a month of sea transit once the cargo is worth near $195 a kilo. Take one 2026 rate table's own two ends, $4.50 a kilo by air against $0.50 by sea [2], hold that cargo the 30 extra days the sea leg costs [1], and use a 25% annual carrying rate [3][4]: a $4.00 premium buys back 29 days of cover on a $200-a-kilo part, 117 days on a $50 one and 12 days on a $500 module.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Two tests go by one name, and they measure different things. The first is a freight-to-value ratio: air is called cheap when the freight bill stays under 10% to 15% of the cargo's worth [2], which is where a $15 to $20 a kilo rule lands once air costs two or three dollars a kilo. The second is a test of time, and one freight guide prints it in full — carrying cost equals cargo value times the annual carrying rate times the extra days, over 365, with 20% to 30% for the rate [3]. If the rate is the input you trust least, [the case for loading the all-in rate rather than bank prime](/inventory-carrying-cost-rate/) settles it; the math below uses the 25% midpoint.

Then the same page puts its $15 to $20 a kilo rule under that formula [3], and the two do not meet. My read is that the page is describing two different purchases, one measuring freight against value and the other pricing a day of stock. Hold $17.50 a kilo of cargo for 30 days at 25% and the carrying cost comes to 36 cents a kilo. The premium in the same publishers' own rate tables is $2.70 to $7.30 a kilo [1][2]. For a $15 to $20 rule to hold, the carrying rate would have to run near 278% a year, eleven times the top of the band the page tells you to load [3][4].

A rate that high is less an error than an admission. Below about $195 a kilo, what the flight buys is margin: stock that sells sooner, a promo that ships on time, a line that does not stop. That is a real buy and it deserves its own budget line, but it is not a 20% to 30% charge on the balance sheet. Treating it as one is how teams end up flying goods that should float, or holding four months of cover to dodge a $4.00 premium.

The 30-day gap is a midpoint too, not a law. Air runs 5 to 8 days door to door and sea runs 22 to 45 [1], so a 14-day gap pushes the break-even to $417 a kilo while a 40-day gap pulls it down to $146. The fewer days a flight saves you, the more the cargo has to be worth before the trip pays.

## By the numbers

- **$194.67 — Break-even cargo value:** 365 times the $4.00 premium, over 30 days at 25%, gives $194.67 a kilo; run the same sum on July's $3.12 air spot against a $0.80 sea rate and it falls to $113 [1][2][3][5].
- **117 days — Cover bought on a $50 part:** 365 times $4.00 over $50 times 0.25 is 116.8 days of holding; the same premium buys 29.2 days on a $200-a-kilo part and 11.7 days on a $500 module [2][3][4].
- **278% — Rate behind the $15–20 rule:** the yearly carrying rate that makes a $17.50-a-kilo rule break even against a $4.00 premium over 30 days is 365 times $4.00 over $17.50 times 30, or 2.78 [3][4][5].
- **$3.12 a kilo — July air spot rate:** world air cargo spot rates averaged $3.12 a kilo in July 2026, up 28% on the year and down 6% on the month, which is the dated market read behind the $113 break-even [5].

## What I'd watch:

- **The premium is many line items, not one rate:** a 2026 guide breaks out the add-ons under the base air rate — screening at $0.10 to $0.25 a kilo, airport handling at $0.15 to $0.35 and a congestion fee at $0.25 to $1.00 [1]. Each one widens the premium the math rests on, and most quotes bury them.
- **Which sea rate you divide against:** full container rates run $0.20 to $0.50 a kilo against $0.30 to $0.80 for part loads [2], so a part-load lane flatters air by shrinking the premium it is measured against.
- **The days the service really saves:** express air quotes 2 to 3 days against 5 to 7 for standard air [2], and paying up for those last days rarely clears two days of holding on goods below $500 a kilo.
- **The credit nobody books:** a shorter, steadier lead time also shrinks the safety stock a node must hold, so the flight's case improves past the transit days it saves. My read is that this credit is real, hard to price, and left out of the math above on purpose, which makes the break-even a cautious one.

## The catch

The limits deserve stating plainly. These are published guide bands, not signed rates: nothing here prices a named shipper's premium, and a negotiated air deal can sit well under the $4.50 a table shows [2]. The spot figure is a monthly average that rose 28% in a year [5], so the $113 break-even it implies moves with the market and should never be written into a policy.

The bigger caveat is the one that decides the argument. Holding cost is not the only thing a flight buys, and the guides' rule is less a mistake than an answer to a different question. When a missed shipment costs a promo, a contract penalty or an idle line, the carrying rate is the wrong base, and the rule can be right for reasons this math cannot see.

What the numbers do settle is where to look, and the part I keep circling is that the cheaper figure is the one that gets repeated. Below about $195 a kilo, a 30-day gap will not pay for itself out of holding cost, so a flight defended with the 20% to 30% rate is defended with a figure that does not reach. Above it the same math turns and the buffer becomes the costly side. The part I keep coming back to is that cargo value per kilo, a number already on the invoice, decides a supply chain question that usually gets argued as a service-level debate.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The choice between air expedites and regional buffer is one division — the air premium per kilo over the daily cost of holding a kilo — and at a $4.00 premium and a 25% carrying rate it puts the break-even near $195 of cargo value a kilo for a 30-day sea gap.
- **Why It Matters:** The $15 to $20 a kilo rule in wide use is a freight-to-value ratio priced at a 278% carrying rate, so teams citing it are buying margin while believing they are saving balance-sheet cost, and the same math tells them which items should never fly.
- **What I'd Watch:** Three inputs swing the answer, and each is checkable before the next shipment books.
  - **The premium's parts:** the add-on fees under the base air rate, from screening to congestion, which widen the premium the math runs on.
  - **The sea comparator:** full container rates against part-load rates, because the lower sea figure makes the premium look bigger and air look worse.
  - **The second credit:** the safety stock a shorter lead time removes, which the math leaves out and which therefore makes the break-even a cautious one.
- **The Catch:** The rate bands are published guides rather than signed prices, the spot average moves monthly, and the math prices holding cost only — so a flight bought for margin, penalties or a stopped line still needs its own case.
