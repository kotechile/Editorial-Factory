<!-- lead -->
Heating oil sold for $6.19 a gallon in the week of October 19, up from $3.53 a year earlier [1]. The federal answer to that price is 315,000 barrels lent from the Northeast emergency reserve, about one day of what oil-heated homes burn in a winter [3]. The trap is on the other side: the way out of the fuel, a heat pump and the work around it, also got more expensive this month [4].

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

- **The shift:** Heating oil reached $6.19 a gallon as Washington lent fuel companies 315,000 reserve barrels.
- **The number:** $2,488, the season's cost at that price on the government's own 402-gallon estimate.
- **The risk:** The reserve barrels go to suppliers, and install materials rose 3% to 15%.

<!-- tension -->
## Why the cheap answer got dearer

The Energy Information Administration (EIA), the federal energy statistics agency, published its winter forecast on October 6. It put heating oil at $5.26 a gallon for the season, or $2,115 for a household burning 402 gallons [2]. The market did not wait for it, and I have been watching this gap widen all month. Weekly readings ran $6.05, $6.14 and $6.19 through October, and the last one sits above even the $5.79 the agency expected for November [1].

The reason is the fuel itself. Heating oil is the same refined product as diesel, and distillate has been the tightest part of the crude barrel all year [2]. Refining has slowed, imports cost more, and stockpiles are thin going into winter [2]. The Northeast also has little pipeline capacity, so cheap natural gas cannot simply replace dear oil there [2]. That is a world market arriving through one narrow pipe.

Federal help came in the third week of October. The Department of Energy offered fuel companies up to 315,000 barrels, drawn from Maine, Massachusetts and Connecticut [3]. Deliveries must finish by November 13, and each borrower returns the same volume plus a fee before the winter of 2027–28 [3].

The other option is to stop buying the fuel. That means a heat pump, and the parts an install consumes, including duct, gas piping, dampers, filters and a tankless water heater, rose 3% to 15% at twelve manufacturers this month [4]. Most disclosed lines sit between 3% and 8%, and two more take effect later in November [4]. The break-even between the two systems has drifted in both directions for a year, and [that arithmetic is what a homeowner is really being asked to sign](/heat-pumps-cheaper-to-run-pricier-to-buy/).

## By the numbers

- **$6.19 a gallon:** The national residential price in mid-October, up from $3.53 a year earlier [1].
- **39.6 million barrels:** What oil-heated homes burn over five winter months, against 315,000 lent from the reserve [2].
- **3,052 oil furnaces:** August shipments of the appliance that burns it, up 27.1% on a small base [5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## What I'd watch

The delivery deadline is the first thing to check. A release of 315,000 barrels against 262,000 barrels burned each day is a small test of a large price, and I'd expect the weekly survey to settle it within a month [1]. If the price holds near $6, the release changed the mood rather than the supply [1].

Two more things sit behind that date. Borrowers owe the fuel back with a fee before the next winter, which leaves the reserve thinner for 2027 [3]. And the agency publishes a new outlook on November 10, which puts a fresh set of prices next to the October ones [2].

What I keep circling is how little the replacement market has moved. Oil warm-air furnace shipments rose 27.1% in August to 3,052 units, a six-month streak, while gas furnaces fell 4.3% [5]. The trade press cautions that a base that small says little about the country, and I read it the same way: it looks like households replacing the box they already own, not a national turn back to oil [5].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch

The price can fall as fast as it rose. Heating oil tracks diesel. The agency expects a wholesale move to reach the retail price within four to six weeks, in either direction [2]. It also warns that switching to a cheaper fuel "would not necessarily result in lower expenditures for their household", because houses and equipment differ from any average [2]. The reserve carries a return obligation, not a price cap. What I'd want before comparing anything is the number no agency prints: what a heat pump would cost to install in a given house.
