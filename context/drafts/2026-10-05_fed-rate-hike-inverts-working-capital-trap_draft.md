<!-- lead -->
On September 16 the Federal Reserve raised its benchmark rate by a quarter point to 3-3/4 to 4 percent — the first hike in more than three years — on a unanimous 12-0 vote [1]. Two weeks later, on October 1, the Institute for Supply Management (ISM) reported that manufacturers were contracting their own raw-material inventories while their customers' inventories read "too low" [3]. The two releases, read together, describe a squeeze the standard working-capital playbook can no longer solve.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

For two decades, the rule of thumb was mechanical: when the cost of money rises, cut inventory. Carrying cost — the interest, storage, insurance, and obsolescence baked into every dollar sitting in a warehouse — is the thing multi-echelon inventory optimization (MEIO) exists to shrink. A rate hike is supposed to make that math easier, not harder.

What strikes me here is that this hike landed on a system with no slack. The ISM report shows the Inventories Index fell 2.0 points to 48.6, back into contraction, while the Customers' Inventories Index dropped to 41.6 — a level ISM itself labels "too low," and a 24-month trend [3]. Manufacturers are not sitting on a pile of expensive safety stock they can trim. They have already trimmed. The thing left to cut is the stock that keeps production running.

The tension is that the same rate hike that punishes holding also punishes replenishing. Supplier deliveries slowed for a tenth straight month, and the Prices Index jumped 6.8 points to 77.9 — back to its level at the start of the Iran war [3]. One Machinery respondent in the ISM survey said it plainly: "Higher interest rates slow down the growth of new construction projects; we also have to face up to the higher cost of components" [3].

## By the numbers

- **3-3/4 to 4 percent — Federal funds rate:** The FOMC raised its target range by a quarter point on a 12-0 vote, its first hike since 2023 [1][2].
- **48.6 — Inventories Index:** ISM's read on raw-materials inventories fell 2.0 points in September, back into contraction [3].
- **41.6 — Customers' Inventories Index:** ISM calls this "too low"; it has trended in that direction for 24 months [3].
- **77.9 — Prices Index:** Up 6.8 points in one month, returning to the level hit at the start of the Iran war [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The people closest to the story are already moving in two directions, and the split is the story. My read: the ones with scale and cheap capital are buying inventory depth; the ones without are being squeezed out of the buffer entirely.

- **AutoZone is doubling down on just-in-case depth.** It opened 16 mega hubs last quarter and 39 year-to-date, now operating 172, and put roughly $1.5 billion of capital expenditure toward store growth and hubs this year [4]. Each hub carries over 100,000 SKUs, and the company targets 300 hubs over three years. That is a big, deliberate bet that holding more inventory close to the customer is worth the carrying cost — the opposite of the "cut inventory" reflex.
- **SMBs are living the other side of the trade.** Supply Chain Dive reports small and mid-size supply chains are fighting a "storm of pressures" — demand, costs, lead times, and policy moving at once — that overwhelms their planning cycles. Without AutoZone's balance sheet, they cannot afford the buffer, and they cannot afford to run without it.
- **The next data point that answers the open question:** the November 2 ISM report and the Fed's year-end meeting. The Fed's own projections point to more tightening ahead, which would keep raising the cost of every dollar of safety stock operators try to rebuild.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

I could be wrong that this is a trap rather than a healthy rebalancing. The ISM "too low" customers' inventories reading is, by ISM's own convention, usually a positive for future production — it means downstream demand has room to pull more output forward. And a rate hike that cools inflation would eventually lower the input costs that are currently spiking alongside it.

But that relief, if it comes, arrives on the far side of a winter in which operators are running lean into rising prices and slowing deliveries. The honest caveat is that the data describes the last month, not the next one — and the system-wide stockout risk is exactly the kind of thing that only shows up in hindsight.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The Federal Reserve raised rates for the first time in more than three years (to 3-3/4 to 4 percent), and the ISM's September report two weeks later showed manufacturers contracting their own inventories while customers' inventories sat "too low" [1][3].
- **Why It Matters:** A rate hike makes holding inventory more expensive — but there is no inventory left to cut, so the usual working-capital lever is gone, and replenishing stock now costs more to finance and more to buy as prices spike.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **AutoZone's mega-hub bet:** 16 hubs opened last quarter, 172 total, ~$1.5B in capex — a large retailer paying to hold inventory depth close to customers while rates rise [4].
  - **The November ISM report:** whether the "too low" customers' inventories reading flips, and whether the Inventories Index stays in contraction.
  - **The Fed's next move:** the central bank's own projections point to more tightening, which would keep lifting the cost of every rebuilt buffer.
- **The Catch:** ISM calls "too low" customer inventories a positive for future production, and a rate hike that cools inflation could eventually lower input costs. The risk is the winter in between — running lean into rising prices and slowing deliveries.
