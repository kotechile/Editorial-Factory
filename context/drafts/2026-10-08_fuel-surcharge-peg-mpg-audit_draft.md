<!-- lead -->
A full-truckload fuel surcharge is not set by the price of diesel. It is set by three numbers a carrier picks: the base price it subtracts from the index (the peg), the miles per gallon it assumes, and the shape of its rate table [2][3]. Move any one of them and the same diesel price buys a different bill.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The diesel index is the one input nobody controls. The Energy Information Administration (EIA) prints the on-highway diesel retail price every week, and carriers peg their tables to that series [1][2].

Everything else is a choice. A per-mile schedule is (current diesel − base diesel) ÷ miles per gallon (MPG) [2]. At $4.25 diesel, a $2.50 peg and 6.5 MPG, the surcharge is $0.269 a mile — $322.80 on a 1,200-mile load [2]. Change the peg or the MPG and the number moves with it [3].

What strikes me here is there is no referee. The surcharge is not set by any government formula; each carrier sets its own baseline, efficiency figure and table [4]. Nothing is hidden, exactly — the schedule is usually printed on the carrier's own site — but it is written in arithmetic most buyers read once, at signature, and never again.

The firms that feel this first are the ones with the most freight on the road. A heavy lane mix multiplies whatever the formula says, so a schedule that looks like a rounding error on one load is a budget line across a million miles.

## By the numbers

- **$5.60/gallon — EIA diesel price:** The federal on-highway diesel retail price in the agency's most recent published breakdown (May 2026), the series carriers peg to [1].
- **$0.269 per mile — Worked surcharge:** What one per-mile table pays at $4.25 diesel, a $2.50 peg and 6.5 MPG; the same load runs $322.80 over 1,200 miles [2].
- **47 cents per mile — Peg sensitivity:** The identical formula at a $4.00 index, a $1.20 peg and 6 MPG — the peg and the MPG, not the market price, set the slope [3].
- **22% to 23% — Banded table:** How a less-than-truckload (LTL) carrier prices the same fuel: $3.00-$3.09 a gallon maps to a 22% surcharge on the base rate, $3.10-$3.19 to 23% [4].

## What I'd watch:

The operators closest to this are not forecasting diesel. They are re-reading the schedule they signed.

- **The peg:** A lower peg raises the surcharge at every diesel price [3]. Two carriers can quote the same base rate and settle a very different bill because one subtracts $1.20 and the other $2.50 [2][3].
- **The MPG assumption:** A lower MPG figure makes the surcharge climb faster than the fuel it is meant to cover [3]. The gap between a carrier's assumed MPG and its real fuel economy is where I'd want the fleet's own numbers.
- **The banding:** Banded tables step up when diesel crosses a threshold [4]. I'd want to know where those edges sit against today's index before a renewal, not after the invoice lands.
- **The audit line:** Fuel runs 20% to 30% of a carrier's operating expenses [2], so the surcharge line is worth reconciling against the weekly EIA print rather than accepting as billed [1][2]. Operators who map twelve months of invoices to the EIA price on each invoice date are the ones who find the drift.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

I could be wrong to treat the schedule as the whole story. Fuel is a real cost, and a table that underprices it does not stop the truck.

A schedule that lags the market can also leave a carrier losing money on a lane. A carrier losing money on your freight is a capacity risk at the next peak, not a saving [2]. The number worth arguing over is the formula, not whether fuel gets priced at all.

The part I keep circling: my read is that the peg and the MPG assumption move the bill more than the headline surcharge percentage does. That holds only if both are visible in the contract — and plenty of shippers have never seen either.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The fuel surcharge on a freight bill comes from a carrier's own formula — the peg, the MPG assumption and the rate table — rather than from any regulated diesel price [2][3][4].
- **Why It Matters:** The same diesel index yields very different surcharges across carriers, so the peg and the MPG assumption can matter more at renewal than the base rate [2][3].
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether shippers read the schedule as closely as the rate.
  - **The peg:** The base diesel price a carrier subtracts before pricing fuel; a lower peg means a higher surcharge at every price [3].
  - **The MPG assumption:** The fuel economy written into the formula; a low figure makes the surcharge rise faster than the real fuel cost [3].
  - **The banding:** The diesel ranges a carrier maps to set surcharge steps; the edges are where a small price move triggers a jump [4].
- **The Catch:** Fuel is a genuine cost, and a schedule that under-prices it can leave a carrier losing money on a lane — a capacity risk, not just a saving [2].
