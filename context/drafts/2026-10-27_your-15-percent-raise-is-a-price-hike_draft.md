<!-- lead -->
Memory has become the line that sets your budget. Gartner expects global tech spending to reach $7.35 trillion in 2027, 15% more than this year, and the fastest-growing part is data center systems at $1.37 trillion, up 55.5% [1]. The same forecast says why: over the past twelve months, demand from data centers drove memory prices to more than double, an effect Gartner calls "memflation" [1]. So your 15% raise is, in large part, a memory price hike, and it buys less computing than last year's budget did.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The day before that forecast, Gartner published a survey of 203 CEOs, and the two documents describe the same year from opposite ends [2]. Forty-four percent of them said they struggle to show a financial return on AI to their boards, while 75% still plan to spend more on tech [2]. The bit that stuck with me is that both can be true at once: a firm can raise the budget and still lose the argument, as long as most of the raise is the vendor's price moving.

Memory is where that price is set. TrendForce has memory-chip contract prices up another 10–15% in the fourth quarter and flash storage up 15–20%, with server memory in short supply [4]. Gartner expects its "memflation" effect to last through 2027. It also says the pressure on buyers' ability to pay will hold back demand across the whole forecast [1]. Read that again: the forecaster is telling you that the growth is not appetite.

Put the two releases side by side and something neither says becomes clear. A spending number built from input prices cannot tell a board whether the firm is doing more work, and a return figure that 44% of CEOs cannot produce cannot tell it whether the money worked. Once growth is priced rather than built, a budget review stops being a check on progress and turns into an argument about unit cost.

## By the numbers

- **$7.35T — 2027 spend:** $7.35 trillion against $6.39 trillion in 2026, with data center systems the fastest-growing line at $1.37 trillion, up 55.5% [1].
- **2x — Memory prices:** Memory prices more than doubled in twelve months, and Gartner expects that "memflation" to last through 2027 before hardware prices settle [1].
- **44% — No return to show:** Of 203 CEOs surveyed in April and May, 44% cannot show a financial return on AI, while 75% plan to spend more [2].
- **+50% — Agent cost miss:** Companies running AI agents on uncapped per-token pricing are expected to beat their own cost plans by more than 50% through 2027 [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The three lines below move on their own rules, and they are the ones a team can still price.

- **Long-term contract terms:** TrendForce says some suppliers' increases will trail the market because of clauses in long-term contracts [4]. Those clauses are the gap between market price and last year's price, and they are readable before signature.
- **The line you can cap:** Gartner's own advice is a strict "no telemetry, no deployment" rule, which is about metering rather than a spend ceiling [3]. A team that cannot measure per agent cannot say which workload ate the increase.
- **Rented against bought capacity:** Data center systems are set to grow 55.5% while cloud services grow 25.7%, to $367 billion [1]. A firm moving steady work onto rented capacity meets a different curve from one buying its own hardware.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The honest limit is that a forecast is not a reading, and this one can move: if buyers really cannot pay, Gartner's next edition can come down [1]. The survey is 203 bosses answering in April and May, which describes a mood rather than a market [2]. My read: the growth is price, not volume, and that is my reading rather than Gartner's, since a firm that mostly buys software meets a milder version of it at 14.6% [1]. What survives all three caveats is more useful, because the one figure worth defending next year is what a unit of work costs you, and no supplier can inflate that for you.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Gartner's 2027 forecast puts global tech spending at $7.35 trillion, up 15%, with data center systems rising 55.5% while memory prices more than doubled. The survey published the day before found 44% of CEOs cannot show a financial return on AI [1][2].
- **Why It Matters:** If budgets grow because inputs got dearer, a bigger number no longer proves a firm is running more or better tech. The return question and the renewal arrive together, and only unit cost answers both.
- **What I'd Watch:** The clauses and meters that decide whether a team pays market price or its own.
  - **Long-term agreement pricing:** Contract clauses that let some buyers' increases trail the market, readable before signature.
  - **Per-agent metering:** Tracking that shows which workload used the budget, the first step to any cap.
  - **Rented versus bought capacity:** Cloud services growing 25.7% against data center systems at 55.5%, two curves for the same work.
- **The Catch:** A forecast can be revised down, 203 people describe a mood rather than a market, and the price-not-volume reading is the desk's own. Buyers heavy in software meet a milder version at 14.6%.
