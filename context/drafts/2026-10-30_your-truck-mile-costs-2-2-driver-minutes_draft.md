<!-- lead -->
The diesel burned by a delivery truck's mile costs about $0.79 at this week's national price, and that number is where a routing business case usually goes soft [1][3][4]. A light van on the same fuel costs about $0.31 a mile, because the highway tables put the two vehicles at 7.1928 and 18.5 miles per gasoline gallon equivalent [1][2]. That $0.79 is also what 2.2 minutes of a driver's wage buys at the federal median of $21.57 an hour, which ties the two biggest lines of a route to one rate [5]. A route change that adds three minutes to save a mile pays more in wages than the mile's diesel was worth [3][4][5].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

My read starts with where the two numbers come from, because neither is a vendor's estimate of what a route is worth.

The highway statistics series, which the U.S. Department of Transportation publishes each year, puts a delivery truck at 7.1928 miles per gasoline gallon equivalent (GGE) against 18.5 for a light truck or van [1][2]. Those are energy-equivalent miles, not gallons at the pump, since one gallon of low sulfur diesel holds 1.12 GGE, so the truck's real figure is 8.06 miles on a gallon of diesel [2][3].

The second number is the stop. FuelEconomy.gov reports that only 12% to 30% of the energy in a gallon reaches the road, and its own diagram is drawn for stop-and-go city driving [7]. Each stop restarts that loss, which is why the class that halts two hundred times a day posts less than half the economy of the van beside it in the same table.

The delivery truck also sits closer to a highway tractor's 6.3048 than to the van's 18.5 [1]. A vehicle that spends its day pulling away from kerbs burns fuel at nearly the rate of the one hauling forty tons between cities, and no route plan written on the van's number sees that.

At this week's prices the two lines convert cleanly. The U.S. Energy Information Administration put on-highway diesel at $6.352 a gallon on October 26, and the Labor Department's wage tables put a light truck driver's median at $21.57 an hour [4][6]. Divide one by the other and a mile of diesel is worth about 2.2 minutes of the person driving it.

## By the numbers

- **7.1928 — Delivery-truck economy:** miles per GGE in the 2024 highway statistics, against 18.5 for a light truck or van, a spread of 2.6 times per mile [1][2].
- **1.12 — Diesel's energy factor:** one gallon of low sulfur diesel equals 1.12 GGE, which turns that figure into 8.06 miles per gallon of diesel [3].
- **$6.352 — Diesel print:** U.S. on-highway diesel per gallon the week of October 26, 2026, against $2.634 a year earlier [4].
- **$21.57 — Driver's hour:** median wage for light truck drivers in the 2025 survey, or $44,860 a year [6].

## What I'd watch:

My read is that three things decide whether a miles-saved pitch survives contact with these tables.

- **The currency of the claim:** a ten-mile cut on a hundred-mile day is about $7.9 of diesel, the same money as 22 driver-minutes, so a planner has to know which line a vendor is quoting [3][4][6].
- **The stop rate:** at 22 stops in a paid hour the median wage spreads $0.98 of pay across each stop, and at 18 stops the same hour puts $1.20 on each one, the one line an optimizer can shorten without buying fuel [6].
- **The plan's own basis:** a case built on the van's 18.5 figure instead of the truck's 7.1928 understates fuel by 2.6 times, the direction that makes a weak project look funded [1][2].
- **The work the router cannot see:** the stop set sits upstream of the sequence, which is where [the district's stop density](/your-routes-5x-stop-gap/) shapes more of the day than the ordering does [1].

## The catch

The part I keep circling is that both numbers are averages, not readings of a reader's own route.

The highway figure covers a class of delivery trucks in the 2024 edition, so a fleet whose vans run longer legs between stops will beat it and one working denser districts will not [1][2]. The fuel is a choice too, because a truck burning gasoline at the week's $4.269 puts its mile at about $0.59 and the rate near 1.7 driver-minutes [5].

The rate moves with the pump, which makes it a rule rather than a fact. A year ago, at the diesel print then, the same mile was worth nine-tenths of a driver-minute, so a case written last autumn priced a mile at about 41% of today's level [4][6]. The wage is a median as well, and benefits, overtime and route pay sit on top of it, which lifts the driver-minute and helps the case for time over miles [6].

The rate also weighs only two lines of a route's cost. It says a mile's diesel is worth 2.2 minutes; it does not say the minutes freed are spent well, and a plan that saves miles without shortening the paid day saves nothing but the pump [1][4].

## At a glance

**The shift:** A route's mile can be priced against a driver's minute, because both come from dated federal tables.
**The number:** 2.2 driver-minutes buys the diesel in one truck-mile.
**The risk:** Both are averages, and on gasoline the rate falls to about 1.7.
