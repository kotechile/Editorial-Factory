<!-- lead -->
7 days' notice now rewrites which Canadian goods carry a 50 percent duty, and Friday's order moved the line both ways at once. It put cocoa powder, carpets and window air conditioners under the duty, lifted it off plywood, corrugated cartons and packing machinery, and made all of it effective on 30 October. That is a seven-day fuse on a number that decides whether a Canadian supplier still works in your landed-cost model.

Three days earlier, on 20 October, a New Jersey food importer filed the first court challenge to this tariff in the Court of International Trade. Its case is narrow and costly to ignore: the law lets the government collect the duty only 30 days after the order that names the goods, and when the September round added Canadian cheese, collection began seven days later. The importer, Gellert Global Group, has paid more than $381,000 in duty on the entries inside that gap.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The rate stopped being the interesting number. The date a product was first listed is the number that matters now, because that date decides whether the money you paid is a cost or a claim.

Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930 lets the president impose duties to answer trade discrimination against U.S. commerce, and it caps them at 50 percent. It also builds in a wait. The law says the new duty is collected "thirty days after the date of such proclamation" on "the articles enumerated in such proclamation" [4]. That wait is the only shield a foreign supplier gets from this law, and it is a rule about timing, not about the rate.

The July orders cleared the wait. They were signed on 20 July, took effect on 19 August, thirty days later, and slipped three more days under a pause before collection began on 22 August [1]. The September round did not clear it: issued on 8 September, it ordered collection from 15 September, seven days later, which is the fourth count of the complaint [1]. The October order has now done the same to a fresh set of goods. Newly listed articles "shall be subject to the additional ad valorem duty of 50 percent," from 30 October, seven days after the signature [2].

My read is that this is a pattern, not a slip. A list that can be rewritten in a week, twice in six weeks, is not a rate you can put in a five-year sourcing case. It is a queue, and a product's place in that queue is the only fixed fact you have.

## By the numbers

- **50% — Duty rate:** the legal ceiling, and the October text says it applies "in addition to duties imposed pursuant to section 232," the separate steel and aluminium tariffs [2][4].
- **7 days — Notice on newly listed goods:** 8 September to 15 September in the September round, then 23 October to 30 October in the October round [1][2].
- **$381,000 — One importer's bill:** paid on entries made between 15 September and 8 October, the window the complaint calls the 30-Day Subclass [1].
- **30 days — Statutory wait:** 8 September plus 30 days lands on 8 October, which is where that subclass closes; 23 October plus 30 lands on 22 November [1][4].

## What I'd watch:

The firms closest to this are not re-sourcing. Gellert says it is absorbing the tariff through the end of 2026 while delaying hiring and investment, and the cheese it brings in had entered free of duty under the U.S.-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) [1]. Paying and keeping the paperwork is the move that keeps a refund open, and it is the opposite of what the same news would push a sourcing team to do.

- **The answer date.** The government must reply by 21 December, and its view is that the clock ran from the July finding and never restarted [5]. A ruling on that one point would reach every importer who paid since 22 August.
- **The motions already filed.** The complaint arrived with a bid to stop collection while the case runs and a bid for judgment in the importer's favour, so this can move without a trial [6].
- **The class.** The filing asks to stand for all importers who paid the duty, plus a subclass of those who paid on goods first listed in September, which would turn any refund into a process rather than a lawsuit [1].
- **The next round.** 22 November is the first date the complaint's reading would treat as lawful for the October additions, and a further order before then would open a third group on the same short fuse.

The part I keep circling is the shrink. Plywood, corrugated cartons and packing machinery came off the list on 30 October, so a packaging change scoped against the September list is already answering a question the list took back. I would want to know how many sourcing calls in the last six weeks were built on a list that has since moved twice.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The government's view is not frivolous. Its case would be that the finding came in July, the 30-day clock ran once against that finding, and a later order that changes which goods are covered does not restart a clock that has already run out. If that view holds, the September and October dates are lawful and the refund claim is worth nothing.

The second limit is structural. A refund only helps an importer who kept importing, because the claim follows the entry records. A firm that moved the volume home or to Mexico to dodge the 50 percent keeps the moving cost, the re-qualifying work and the lost volume discount, and gets none of the money back. That gap does not show up in a landed-cost sheet, because the duty line looks easy to undo and the tooling line does not.

The third limit is the document. Both annexes leave out civil aircraft and aircraft parts, customs writes the rules that settle borderline codes, and neither list is final for longer than the next order. My read is that the safest plan assumes a list that keeps moving, and prices it as an option rather than a rate.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** On 23 October the White House rewrote which Canadian goods pay a 50 percent duty, effective 30 October, adding cocoa powder, carpets and air conditioners and removing plywood and packing machinery. Three days earlier an importer sued over the same seven-day gap in September.
- **Why It Matters:** The date a product was first listed, not the rate, decides whether duty already paid is a cost or a refund claim, and that date now moves faster than a re-sourcing programme can.
- **What I'd watch:** How the December reply lands, whether the class is certified, and whether the next order repeats the short fuse.
  - **The 30-day wait:** the law collects the duty a month after the order that names the goods, so newly listed articles get a month of cover that the September and October rounds did not give them.
  - **The 30-Day Subclass:** the importers who paid on goods first listed in September, whose refund claim is the strongest in the filing because their entry dates sit inside the contested window.
  - **The listing date:** the day a tariff code first appears on the list, which is the fact that decides refund eligibility rather than the day the goods arrived.
- **The Catch:** The refund theory is untested and the government's view is plausible; a firm that moved production to escape the duty keeps the cost and loses the claim.
