<!-- lead -->

Your console rose instead of falling after memory and storage costs climbed, and the three current machines now cost $500 more together than they did at release [4]. Sony, Microsoft and Nintendo each passed that bill along rather than absorbing it. What decides the repair-or-replace sum is no longer the machine's age. It is whether the part still exists and whether you can fit it.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Console replacement prices rose after launch for the first time.
- **The number:** $500 more for the three current consoles together.
- **The risk:** Cheap parts are vendor-controlled, and supply ends before the hardware does.

<!-- tension -->

## The old price curve broke

Consoles used to get cheaper as they got older. Not this time. This generation moved the other way in three steps.

| Vendor | Date | Price move | What the company said |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sony | 2 April 2026 | The disc PlayStation 5 rose to $649.99 in the United States | "Continued pressures" in the global economy [1] |
| Microsoft | 1 August 2026 | 512-gigabyte consoles up $100; 1-terabyte models up $150 | Console storage and memory costs rose more than 2.5 times over, with another doubling expected by the fall of 2027 [2] |
| Nintendo | 1 September 2026 | Switch 2 rose from $449.99 to $499.99, fifteen months after launch | A revision to "market conditions" expected to last [3] |

iFixit put the three side by side this week and priced the total [4]. What strikes me is that the increase covers a cost, not a margin. Microsoft says consoles are "typically not sold at a profit" and that it sells them for less than they cost to make [2]. The same announcement retired its 2-terabyte model and added instalment financing and a certified refurbished line, which is how a vendor says the price has outrun the buyer [2]. I read that as the industry admitting the old price curve had flipped before it repriced the box.

The other side of the repair-or-replace sum has not moved. A drifting joystick is still a cheap part [5]. A boxed New Nintendo 3DS XL now sells above its original list price, eleven years after it launched [4]. The two ends sit far apart, and both are still about the part.

## By the numbers

- **$14.99:** iFixit's price for the DualSense joystick, the part that drifts first [5].
- **$199.99:** the launch price iFixit cites for the New Nintendo 3DS XL [4].
- **$450.00:** PriceCharting's current price for the boxed New Nintendo 3DS XL [6].

## What fails, and what it costs

The usual failure list is unglamorous: drifting sticks, dust, loud fans and consoles that run hot [4]. Most improve once the machine is opened and cleaned [4]. Dust is the cheap half of the maintenance bill, and the thermal paste under a six-year-old heatsink is a consumable in all but name. On paper, the joystick is a rounding error next to the console it sits in. The part is cheap. The work to fit it is where the cost sits.

iFixit rates the joystick replacement difficult, and puts the fix at one to four hours, because the module is soldered to the controller's board [5]. That means a soldering iron, a desoldering pump and a steady hand, or a repair shop's hourly rate on top of the part. I keep coming back to that labour, because it is the one cost no vendor sets.

iFixit ran repair workshops across Europe this autumn and found people with the motivation and the knowledge who still could not afford the part [7]. Microsoft's answer to its own price rise is a certified refurbished console at up to $100 off list [2]. Sony's Black Friday promotion this month discounts games rather than hardware [8]. The quiet move is that nobody is making the parts cheaper.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

None of this makes a console an investment. Microsoft's increase recovers a cost on hardware it already sells below cost, and a company that loses money on the box has no reason to make repairs cheap [2]. I don't read the resale premium as a general rule either. The sealed New Nintendo 3DS XL reaches $838.78, and much of that premium is the box, with no loose-unit figure in this record [6].

Nintendo stopped repairing that handheld line in March 2025, when it ran out of components [4]. The console you already own is worth keeping because its replacement got more expensive. That is also why its parts will get more expensive and scarcer, on a clock set by the people who made it rather than by the market. The choice is whether to repair what you own or pay the new replacement price, and the [repair-or-replace calculator](/calculators/tech-debt-repair/) frames the two numbers that decide it: the quote and the replacement cost.
