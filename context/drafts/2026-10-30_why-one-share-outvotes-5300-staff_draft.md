<!-- lead -->
Anthropic set aside a whole share class for its 5,300 employees in the filing it lodged on 26 October, and gave that class no claim on the money and a vote on only "certain limited matters" [1]. The same filing hands one share of Class F stock to a company owned by the seven co-founders, and that one share carries "50.1% of the total voting power" with no claim on the money either [1]. Two weeks earlier, after its own board put a price on the stock, Anduril let staff sell at $94.80 a share between 13 October and 9 November, in an offer capped at $1.3 billion [2]. One company printed a price for its staff. The other filed a document explaining who gets to decide whether it ever does.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Owning a business and running it used to be the same thing. The Anthropic filing puts the two on one page. The money sits in Class A, the stock the public will buy and the stock staff get through their stock grants, and each of those shares carries one vote [1][4]. The power sits elsewhere. A trust holds Class T, which "does not otherwise have voting rights with respect to matters submitted to stockholders generally and has no economic rights", and the directors that class picks "will constitute a majority of the authorized members of our board of directors" [1]. The company said so itself, in its own words: Class A holders "generally will not be able to affect the outcome of any matter submitted to a vote of our stockholders or the election of our directors, regardless of how they vote" [1].

The class built for employees is the part I keep coming back to. Class E is the only class made for people who work there, it has no claim on the money, and it votes on two narrow questions [1]. Staff get "one share of Class E common stock for each six months of employment completed", which works out at two shares a year — slow to earn, quick to lose. After a departure that is not for cause, "one-half of such employee's then-outstanding shares of Class E common stock will be automatically redeemed by us annually", and the rest go on death, incapacity, a for-cause firing or a move to a rival [1]. The filing is blunt about the terms: "Any redemption of Class E common stock will be made for no consideration" [1]. Class F, by contrast, cannot be watered down by new stock, as Fortune read the filing [3].

This is not an Anthropic quirk. In 2025, 15 of the 31 tech firms that listed in the United States had more than one class of stock, and 41% of all initial public offerings (IPOs) did, against about 10% across the previous four and a half decades, on data from the University of Florida's Jay Ritter that Fortune cites [3]. The structure has become normal at the exact firms most likely to pay an engineer in stock.

## By the numbers

- **50.1% — Class F voting power:** one share, held by a company owned by the seven co-founders, carries a majority vote and no claim on the money [1].
- **2 shares a year — Class E accrual:** the staff vote is earned at one share per six months, then halved each year after a non-cause exit and bought back for nothing [1].
- **$23.7 billion — Unbooked stock pay:** none of it was booked as of 30 June 2026, because the condition that vests the grants "was not probable"; it hits the accounts over about 2.1 years once the offering closes [1].
- **$94.80 — Anduril tender price:** what staff could accept from 13 October to 9 November, in a company-run offer capped at $1.3 billion [2].

## What I'd watch:

Candidates and staff are reading offer letters differently than they did a year ago, and the homework has moved from the headline valuation to the share classes.

- **The class behind the votes:** it is fair to ask which class carries the vote, who holds it and what ends it. Fortune reports the Class F share goes away once "fewer than three of the seven founders remain in the LLC for 90 days", which makes the whole setup a function of who stays [3].
- **The grant date against the listing date:** Anthropic's 2021 plan stops issuing awards when a new plan takes effect "the day immediately prior to the day the registration statement ... is effective" [1]. The board has already approved grants after 30 June that take effect the day before the listing, at a value "equal to the price per share of the shares sold in this offering" [1]. A grant priced at the offering captures none of the jump from private to public. The older grants carry that.
- **The tender record:** a private shareholder's only seen price is one a board chooses to publish, inside a set window and a cap. Anduril's offer is a good example, and [a tender-versus-IPO calculator](/calculators/secondary-tender-calculator/) is the kind of tool that makes the trade concrete: sell into a capped offer now, or wait for an exit that the filing says is less likely to arrive uninvited [1][2].
- **The new payroll lever:** the same filing switches on a first staff stock purchase plan, live the day before the listing, with a $25,000 yearly cap and payroll deductions of up to 15% [1].

The filing's own words on liquidity are worth quoting rather than paraphrasing. The structure "is likely to have the effect of limiting the likelihood of an unsolicited merger proposal, unsolicited tender offer, or proxy contest for the removal of directors", and it "may have the effect of depriving our stockholders of an opportunity to sell their shares at a premium over prevailing market prices" [1]. That is the company describing, in its own words, the trade it is asking staff to make.

## The catch

The structure is not a swindle, and it is worth saying so plainly. Anthropic is a public benefit corporation whose charter names a mission, the trust exists to guard that mission, and the co-founders have promised to give away 80% of their stock, per Fortune [3]. Dual-class tech firms that listed since 1980 beat similar stocks by about 19 points over their first three years, while single-class ones trailed by 2.4 points, on Ritter's numbers [3]. The staff vote is real as far as it goes. It simply does not reach the calls this piece is about.

Two limits on what I have written. The filing is a draft: the price range, the number of shares for sale and the size of the new plan pool are all blank in the version filed on 26 October [1], so none of this turns into a per-share value for an Anthropic grant. And the filing says the structure makes an *uninvited* approach less likely. A company-run tender is a different event, and Anthropic has held one before. Anduril's price is proof that a board can make a market any time it chooses to.

Where I've landed is that the offer question has changed shape. It used to be what the stock is worth. At these firms it is who else gets to decide what it is worth, and how often they have decided.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** Anthropic's filing gives staff shares that carry money but no control, plus a voting share that carries no money.
**The number:** 50.1% of the vote sits in one Class F share held by the co-founders' company.
**The risk:** the price of that stock, and any tender that would set one, is a board call.
