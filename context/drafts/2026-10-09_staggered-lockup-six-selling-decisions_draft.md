SpaceX employees get their next window to sell today, when up to 328.4 million shares — about 7% of the still-locked block — become eligible to trade [4]. It is the fourth rung of a ladder, not a finish line, and that distinction is the whole story.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The old lockup rule after an initial public offering (IPO) was one wall at 180 days. Everyone waited, everyone was freed on the same date, and every employee made a single sell-or-hold decision. SpaceX built a ladder instead.

When the company listed on June 12, fewer than 5% of its shares were available to trade [2]. The rest unlock in stages, tied to earnings reports and to fixed days after pricing [3][4]. There is no single "lockup expiration" left to plan around.

I have been watching this calendar all year, and the part that keeps my attention is not the share counts. It is that a staged release moves the hard work from one big choice to a sequence of small ones.

The first window was August 6, when the day after a weak earnings report freed 911.5 million shares [2]. Releases of about 7% then fall on days 70, 90, 105, 120 and 135 — August 20, September 9, September 24, October 9 and October 24 [3].

The schedule is public, and it is unusual. A company this large normally debuts with a wider float. This one started below 5% and will climb toward 40% by December 8 [2].

## By the numbers

- **328.4 million — Shares freed today:** The Oct 9 tranche is roughly 7% of the still-restricted block, and an equal tranche follows on Oct 24 [4].
- **$135 — IPO price:** SpaceX priced there on June 11 and began trading on Nasdaq the next day, in the largest U.S. listing on record [1][2].
- **Under 5% — Float at the debut:** Fewer than one share in twenty was tradeable on day one, which is why each release moves the float so much [2].
- **40% — Float by December 8:** The share of the company that will be tradeable once the staged releases finish; the other 60%, including Musk's stake, stays locked into 2027 [2].

## What I'd watch:

- **The earnings trigger:** After the third-quarter report, a release of about 1.3 billion shares — some 28% of the block — becomes eligible, and it is the largest single tranche of 2026 [4][5]. The date depends on when SpaceX reports, so the calendar matters more than the percentages [3].
- **A release is not a trading window:** A lockup date lifts the IPO restriction on a slice of your shares. It does not open your employer's trading window, which usually shuts in the weeks before earnings [3]. Eligibility and the ability to sell are two different gates.
- **The 10b5-1 clock:** An employee who is not a director or officer needs a trading plan adopted at least 30 days before its first trade; directors and officers face a 90- to 120-day cooling-off [3]. A plan meant to trade in December has to exist in early November — if the company allows plans during the lockup at all [3].
- **The tax-year seam:** The final 180-day window ends December 8, and a sale lands in the 2027 tax year if it slips past January 1 [3][5]. Splitting a plan across December and January spreads the gain over two years [5].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

Staggering the release is not the same as solving the decision. If anything, it makes the drift easier. A single cliff forces one call; six windows invite six small ones, each swayed by the last headline and each easy to postpone.

My read on the trap: an employee who never writes the plan down can still "decide" six times and sell nothing, ending the year as concentrated as they started. The schedule only pays off for the people who set the target before the first window and then execute it, window after window [5].

Most of the tax was also settled before anyone sells. Restricted stock unit (RSU) shares were taxed as wages the day they were delivered, and the withholding was likely the 22% supplemental rate — short of a senior earner's real rate, with the difference due the following April [3]. The bigger the grant, the larger that gap.

The numbers are also only as good as the price. The one release that flexed with the market — a bonus tranche worth 30% above the $135 offer — never fired, because the stock closed no higher than $125.33 in the measuring window [3]. The ladder is fixed; the value on it is not.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** SpaceX split its 180-day lockup into a ladder of staged releases instead of one cliff, so employees now face six selling windows — the next today, Oct 9 — rather than a single date [3][4][5].
- **Why It Matters:** Equity is where most of a senior engineer's wealth sits, and a lockup decides when any of it becomes cash. Holding fewer than 5% of shares tradeable at the debut means each release can move the float — and the price — hard [2].
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The Q3 release:** An estimated 1.3 billion shares, about 28% of the block, become eligible after third-quarter earnings [4][5].
  - **The window, not the date:** Whether the company's trading window is open in the same week a tranche unlocks — the two are not the same gate [3].
  - **The 10b5-1 plan:** Whether enough time is on the clock to adopt a plan before the December window, since staff need 30 days and officers need 90 to 120 [3].
- **The Catch:** A staged release rewards a plan written before the first window and punishes drift; the withholding gap on RSUs and the fixed bonus trigger mean the headline tranche size is not the number that ends up in your account [3].
