<!-- lead -->
A Maine judge ordered a builder to pay a homeowner $630,500 back. The checks come at $125 a week, which pays the debt off in 97 years, before any interest [1]. The family won the case outright and still watched the payments stop for more than six months; the first check since then arrived this week [1]. If you hire a builder, this case reads to me as one blunt truth: a court order is not money in your hand. The only cash you can hold is the cash you have not paid yet.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

This article follows a Maine family that won a $630,500 judgment against their builder and collects it at $125 a week. It concerns any homeowner hiring a contractor, and the finding is that a verdict against a broke builder is not a recovery.

<!-- tension -->

## The order was real. The money was not.

The record is short, and I read it twice before the arithmetic sank in [1]. In 2022, Adam Goode hired a builder a friend had praised on a fixed-price deal of about $281,000 to rebuild his family's camp in Raymond, Maine down to the studs. A year went by and the camp was still unfit to live in. A text asked for "$20,000 more to continue your project." The family stopped paying. A second builder then found a roof and ceiling letting water into the house, with no vapor barrier under it. In court, records showed the company the builder claimed as his — Timberline Construction of Windham — was not active in the state's registry. It had been struck off years before the deal was signed. A judge found the builder broke the state's home-contract law again and again, and awarded more than $630,500 plus interest [1]. The court set the pay-back at $125 a week, and warned that missed checks could mean a contempt charge and jail [1].

The mechanism here is structural, not bad luck. Maine does not license or regulate home builders at all, so nothing screens the person behind the company name [2]. A company struck from the registry can still take deposits, and a judgment can only collect from someone who still has assets. The state's own words say how often that fails: even after wins in civil suits and criminal theft cases, Maine has been unable to collect a large share of the money its courts ordered paid, because the builders were bankrupt, judgment-proof, or gone [2]. The attorney general's office receives more complaints about contractors than about any other category of business [2]. The verdict ends the argument. It does not end the problem.

## By the numbers

- **$630,500 plus interest:** The pay-back a Maine judge ordered against the camp's builder [1].
- **$125 a week:** The agreed payment, which retires the award in 97 years without interest [1].
- **$93,666:** The deposit ceiling Maine law puts on a $281,000 home-build deal [2].

## Where the leverage actually sits

The state's own offices point at the same answer, and my reading is that they point at it quietly. The attorney general's office will tell anyone whether a given builder has drawn complaints — a call or an online form away — and urges you to check a builder's record before work starts [2]. State law already builds the escrow shape into every deal over $3,000: the deal must be written, the deposit capped at a third, start and end dates set down, and every change order signed by both sides on paper [2]. Those rules only bite while money is still in the homeowner's hands, which is why the unpaid balance is where the leverage lives. The Goode family held theirs too late, when the leverage was already gone. The bond and insurance lines on a bid are a smaller backstop than most assume; a bond's ceiling — $25,000 flat in California — and its claim clocks are [fixed no matter what the job costs](/your-contractors-bond-dies-at-25000/) [3]. The complaint check is free. The registry search for the signing company is public record.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

Every one of these controls buys leverage, not certainty, and I would not oversell any of them. A deposit capped at a third still leaves two-thirds of a job exposed while the work runs. A written deal is only as strong as the live company behind it. The state's own page lists theft convictions where collection still failed in large part [2]. What survives is modest: know who you are paying before the deposit moves, keep the last payment large, and treat a win in court as the start of a collection fight, not the end of one. The Goode family's first check in six months arrived this week. At this pace, the last one lands around the year 2123.
