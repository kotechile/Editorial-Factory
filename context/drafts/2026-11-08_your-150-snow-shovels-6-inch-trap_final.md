<!-- lead -->
A two-car driveway in the second week of November comes down to what the hardware store has on the shelf, and this year that shelf holds a 12-inch machine and a 22-inch one. The trap in your $150 snow shovel is the intake buried under its driveway claim, because depth of cut—not clearing width—decides how many passes your driveway takes. Toro prices the Power Shovel 38361 at $149.99 and calls it suited to small driveways that hold up to four cars [1]. Snow Joe's SJ627E (the 22-inch corded unit) costs $249.99, takes 13 inches in one pass, and is the one to buy for a driveway you clear from an outlet [2].

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

- **The shift:** Depth of cut sets pass count; clearing width does not.
- **The number:** 13 inches of intake against 6, so one pass or two.
- **The risk:** The wider machine draws the whole circuit, and Snow Joe's cord limit stops at 100 feet.

<!-- tension -->
## Depth sets the walk

Both pages make the same shape of claim, but they answer different questions. Toro says the shovel suits small driveways that hold up to four cars, then adds "and up to 6-in of snow at a time" [1]. Snow Joe describes a path 22 inches wide by 13 inches deep in a single pass [2]. My read: only the second answers what a February morning asks.

Depth sets pass count because a single-stage rotor—the spinning auger that scoops snow—can move only the snow that fits under its housing. A fall deeper than the intake forces a second pass. A 10-inch storm does exactly that to the Toro, so the second pass doubles the walking on every strip [1]. The Snow Joe clears that storm in one pass [2]. That pass count multiplies the walking by about 3.4 times [1]. Neither page prints the pass count math; both sell the job in pounds a minute instead.

A 24-foot driveway is 288 inches across, which is 24 passes at a 12-inch cut and 14 at a 22-inch one. Doubling the narrow machine for depth gives 48, and the wider machine clears more snow per amp of current: 56 pounds a minute against 40 [2]. The real surprise sits two rows down in the Toro spec table, where the page lists one current draw under the motor row and a different one under the motor type [1]. Popular Mechanics notes that corded machines are "limited to smaller, 15-amp motors that run on the power an outlet can provide" [3]. That line is often read as a battery argument, but it is more useful as a reason to check what else shares the circuit.

## By the numbers

- **48 passes:** a 24-foot driveway in a 10-inch storm, because the 6-inch intake needs two passes, against 14 for the 22-inch cut [1].
- **$0.298 per rated pound:** Snow Joe's $249.99 price divided by 840 pounds a minute, against $0.500 for the Toro at 300 [2].
- **1,800 watts:** 120 volts times the 15 amps Snow Joe prints, against 900 watts for the Toro's 7.5-amp row [2].

## Where each machine wins

The shovel is not a worse machine; it is a machine for a different shape of job, and the shape is narrow. A 12-inch cut fits between deck railings and along a front walk. Its 20-foot throw is longer than most of those spaces, and the low current draw leaves half the outlet for lights on the same circuit [1]. What I keep circling is how much of that sits under the same driveway claim the wider machine makes.

The thrower wins wherever the snow lands on the apron between the car and the street. Its deeper intake clears a plow pile in one pass, its 25-foot throw clears the driveway, and its high capacity is the class figure Popular Mechanics says the outlet caps [2]. The cost of that is the current draw: the Snow Joe wants an outlet with nothing else running and wire thick enough to carry the full load to the far end of the drive.

The cord is the part buyers budget for last and makers budget for first. Snow Joe publishes the requirement on the 18-inch sister model: a maximum extension cord length of 100 feet, a minimum of 12-gauge wire at that length, and 14-gauge at 25 feet [4]. That model is sold out and not a purchase option here, but the row belongs to a machine with the same current draw and reads as the maker's own answer. Popular Mechanics makes the same point from the other side: a power cord "substantially reduces their range" [3].

| The situation | The machine that fits |
|---|---|
| A deck, front steps, or a walk narrower than four feet | The Toro Power Shovel 38361: a 12-inch cut fits between railings, and the throw range clears the space |
| A two-car driveway within 100 feet of an outdoor outlet | The Snow Joe SJ627E: one pass at full depth, and the wider cut on every walk |
| A driveway more than 100 feet from the nearest outlet | Neither machine: the maker's cord limit rules out both sizes |

The decision left after the intake math is the cord question, and a driveway beyond the maker's limit takes neither machine. ## The catch

Toro's own spec table disagrees with itself, and the disagreement is instructive. One row reads "Motor Amps 7.5 Amps"; two rows below, the motor type reads "15 Amp Series Wound Electric" [1]. The lower row is the one the math uses, and I could not find a page on either host that resolves the two.

The harder problem is how little measured evidence sits behind either machine. Both makers publish pounds a minute and throw distance, and neither publishes the test behind them. Those capacities are vendor claims of the same class as the driveway claims. The pass count math also assumes straight, full-width passes on a flat driveway surface, which is the best case for both machines and the case no driveway quite is.

## What we did not test

The figures here come from two manufacturer product pages, one sister-model spec table and one publisher's roundup. Neither machine was handled, nothing was run, and no capacity, throw distance or current draw was measured. The watts and pass counts are math done on printed maker numbers, and the only outside voice is Popular Mechanics, quoted as a publisher rather than as a test of either machine. Toro's page may route buyers to a dealer, so local price can differ.
