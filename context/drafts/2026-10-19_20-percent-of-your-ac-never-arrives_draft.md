<!-- lead -->
In a Sacramento attic, a duct system lost 12% of its cooling on the way to the register closest to the furnace. The same system lost more than 40% on the way to the bedroom at the end of the run [1]. That gap is the part of an air conditioning (AC) decision nobody quotes. When the system finally fails, the choice on the table is a coil repair or a full replacement. The replacement is sold on a factory rating. The ducts are what deliver it. Field research cited in the InterNACHI inspection standard puts duct leakage at 15-20% of space-conditioning energy use on average, even in new construction [1]. Put plainly: about a fifth of your AC never arrives.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The load calculation the trade sizes equipment to assumes the ducts are perfect. Manual J makes two assumptions when it estimates duct loads. It assumes there is no duct leakage, and that conduction loss does not depend on how long the run is [1]. The standard does not hide this. It states it.

The field measurements point the other way on both counts. Leakage ran 15-20% on average across that body of California research, even in new homes [1]. Conduction loss scaled with run length: 12% at the near register, more than 40% at the far one, in the same attic [1]. The standard's own reading of that result is that the far bedroom's duct is most likely undersized [1].

What strikes me is how cleanly this splits the money. Heating and cooling is 52% of a household's annual energy consumption, on the 2020 end-use survey from the U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) [2]. Apply the leakage finding to that slice. Roughly 8% to 10% of the whole household energy bill then moves through a gap the quote never mentions. That is the desk's arithmetic on two cited figures, not a measured saving.

The federal floor has moved too. Central air conditioners and heat pumps made on or after January 1, 2023 must meet a Seasonal Energy Efficiency Ratio 2 and a Heating Seasonal Performance Factor 2, set per product class. The minimum is 13.4 SEER2 for a split-system air conditioner under 45,000 Btu/hr. It is 14.3 SEER2 with 7.5 HSPF2 for a split-system heat pump [4]. Any new box beats a 15-year-old one. The question is how much of that gain reaches the room.

## By the numbers

- **20% — Duct leakage penalty:** Leakage raises space-conditioning energy use by 15-20% on average, and the research behind it covered houses with ducts in attics and crawlspaces, new builds included [1].
- **More than 40% — End-of-run loss:** The same Sacramento attic lost 12% of duct energy to conduction at the register nearest the furnace and more than 40% at the far bedroom, a Modera and Jump measurement from 1995 [1].
- **52% — Heating-and-cooling share:** Space heating and air conditioning take 52% of a household's annual energy use, on 2020 data [2]. The average residential retail price of electricity was 17.30 cents per kilowatthour in 2025 [3].
- **350 cfm/ton — Airflow floor:** The acceptance check calls for indoor coil airflow above 350 cfm per ton, and equipment sized no more than 15% above the calculated sensible load [1].

## What I'd watch:

The people closest to this problem treat the duct system as part of the equipment. It is not the walls it hides behind. Three things show up in how they write the scope.

- **The load calculation comes before the model number.** Sizing is meant to follow a room-by-room load calculation. The duct design uses a matching method, and the equipment is picked from the result [1]. A quote that starts at the box is working backwards.
- **Airflow gets measured, not assumed.** The acceptance criteria include a charge check and an airflow check, with indoor coil airflow above 350 cfm/ton [1]. That is a reading taken at the unit. It is the first thing a homeowner can ask to see on the invoice.
- **Leakage is tested before and after.** The standard directs that leakage be measured as part of acceptance, using a pressurization method [1]. A before-and-after number is the only evidence that a duct repair did anything.

I keep coming back to the comparison problem. Two contractors can quote the same box at different prices. Neither quote can be weighed, because the thing that decides the outcome is missing from both. The leakage number is the one line that makes them comparable.

## The catch

The leakage finding is not a savings figure. The research measures what leakage costs. It does not measure what a retrofit recovers. The standard reports that a duct retrofit cut run times in the same field work [1], and that is as far as the evidence goes. Anyone promising a percentage saving is guessing past the source.

The measurement is also old and local. The 12% and 40% readings come from one 1995 study in one Sacramento attic, on an R-4 duct system [1]. A house with ducts inside the conditioned space is a different case. A tight, well-designed system makes the box the right answer. My read is that the finding is a reason to test, not a verdict on your house.

Testing carries its own cost. A leakage test and a room-by-room load calculation are work. They land on a homeowner at the worst time, when the system is already down. The honest trade is that the test buys information before the cheque, and it is the one part of the decision that still changes after the box is picked.

The floor does not close the gap either. The 13.4 SEER2 air conditioner minimum and the 14.3 SEER2 heat pump minimum tell you what a new unit must be. They do not tell you what it delivers [4]. A rated box on a leaky, unbalanced system is still a rated box losing a fifth of its output in the attic.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The replacement is sold as an efficiency rating. The field research behind the inspection standard puts duct leakage at 15-20% of space-conditioning energy use, even in new construction. The load method the trade sizes to assumes zero leakage [1].
- **Why It Matters:** Heating and cooling is 52% of household energy, so the penalty sits on the largest line in the bill [2]. The loss is uneven too: 12% at the near register against more than 40% at the end of the run [1].
- **What I'd Watch:** I am watching whether the scope carries a measurement rather than a promise.
  - **Load calculation:** A room-by-room estimate of what each room needs, done before a model number is picked, so the equipment is sized to the house [1].
  - **Airflow check:** A reading of indoor coil airflow above 350 cfm/ton at acceptance, the cheapest evidence that the system moves the air it was sold as moving [1].
  - **Leakage test:** A before-and-after duct leakage measurement, because the penalty is real and a repair without a number is a claim [1].
- **The Catch:** The 15-20% figure measures what leakage costs, not what a retrofit recovers. The sharpest readings come from a single 1995 study in one attic. The SEER2 floors set a manufacturing minimum, not a delivered result [1][4].
