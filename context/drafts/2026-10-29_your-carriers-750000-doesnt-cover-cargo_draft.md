<!-- lead -->
The number a shipper assumes is standing behind a load is $750,000. That is the minimum cover a for-hire trucking firm must file before it can register [1]. It also sits at the top of a table headed "Schedule of Limits—Public Liability" [1].

Your carrier's $750,000 doesn't cover cargo. The only cargo figure in the same rulebook is $5,000 a truckload, and it belongs to household-goods movers [2]. A broker's $75,000 is narrower still. The law scopes it to freight charges a broker failed to pass on, not to goods that arrived short [3].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Three documents get confused in a carrier packet, and they answer three different questions. The federal filing says what the truck owes the public after a crash. A broker's bond says what happens when the go-between keeps the money. Cargo cover says what pays for the load itself, and it is the only one of the three the law treats as optional [3][4].

My read is that the confusion is structural, not careless. Public liability is written for what a truck does to other people and their property on the road [1]. That is why the schedule is priced by hazard class rather than by the value of the freight [1]. The goods inside the trailer get their own paragraph in the same statute. That paragraph says the regulator "may require" security to pay a shipper for damage to property in the carrier's hands [3]. A permission is not a mandate.

Part 387 does mandate cargo cover in exactly one place, and it is not general freight. Household-goods carriers must file security that repays individual shippers for all property belonging to them [6]. The amounts are $5,000 for a loss on one vehicle and $10,000 for an aggregate loss at one time and place [2]. That is the whole of the cargo floor in the federal rulebook. It exists because a moving company holds a family's entire home in one van.

The weight threshold matters as much as the amounts. The whole subpart excepts a vehicle under a gross vehicle weight rating of 10,001 pounds [5]. That is exactly where an expedited van or a final-mile sprinter sits [5]. A shipper routing urgent freight through those vans sits outside the federal floor. It is a blind spot with no paper trail. Nothing in the paperwork says so.

## By the numbers

- **150 times — Liability floor against cargo figure:** The public-liability minimum divided by the only cargo amount the rules state is 750,000 ÷ 5,000, and that is how far apart the two instruments sit [1][2].
- **2.5% — Mandated cover on a $200,000 load:** A household-goods shipper who loses a $200,000 load against a $5,000 per-vehicle floor is covered for a fortieth of the loss [2]. That is the most generous cargo rule in the part.
- **60 days — Claim advertisement on a broker failure:** When a broker fails financially, its surety must advertise for claims for 60 days from the date the cancellation notice runs, and pay those it validates [3][4].
- **30 days — Notice and response clocks:** A broker bond cannot be cancelled without notice 30 days before the effective date, and a surety must answer a claim by the 30th day after it arrives [3][4].

## What I'd watch:

- **The certificate, not the registration.** What a risk team reads is a certificate of insurance carrying a cargo limit and a policy number [1][6]. The federal filing proves liability cover and says nothing about the goods. Both pages arrive in the same packet and look equally official.
- **The broker-security section, re-read each year.** The property-broker surety section carries its own currency note, "effective January 16, 2026" [5]. That is the clearest sign this corner of the rulebook moves fast. A shipper holding a 2024 copy is reading a different regime.
- **Unpaid freight as the bond's real job.** The claims a broker bond was built to pay come from carriers that hauled a load and never got paid [3][4]. A double-brokering dispute is where the instrument earns its keep. A shipper's damaged-goods claim is a different kind of claim.
- **Small-vehicle capacity.** Expedited and final-mile van networks sit below the weight rating that triggers the federal floor [5]. The freight they carry is often the highest-value, most time-critical load in the network.

## The catch

Cargo cover exists as a product. Plenty of carriers buy it. What makes a policy apply to a specific load is a schedule and a contract term, not a federal filing [1][3]. That is the part I keep circling. The rules tell a shipper what the counterparty must file, and the answer is a document about the public rather than about the freight.

Two limits apply. The household-goods amounts are a floor under a regulated moving market, not a market rate [2]. So the gap they show is a gap in mandate, not a benchmark of what cover costs. And this piece carries no premium, because none was sourced. What it can say is where the duty lives: the contract and the policy schedule. Not the registration file.

The practical shape is less dramatic than the scare version. Most freight moves without a loss, and most losses that do happen settle between insurers. The exposure is narrower and nastier. It is the load that fails on a broker's lane, in the month after the carrier's policy lapsed, with nothing behind it but a bond written for somebody else's unpaid invoice [4].

The filing clock still governs the claim itself. A shipper with the right counterparty and the wrong paperwork loses on procedure rather than on cover [see the freight claim filing window](/freight-claim-dies-at-9-months/).

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The federal numbers shippers quote at each other measure public liability, broker honesty and household-goods cargo, in that order. None of them measures cover for general freight.
- **Why It Matters:** A shipper who reads a carrier's registration as proof the load is insured is relying on a document written for the public on the highway. The loss then lands on a contract clause and an insurance schedule instead.
- **What I'd watch:** Whether cargo limits start appearing on the certificate rather than being assumed from the registration, and how the broker-security section reads after its next amendment.
  - **Public liability:** The schedule that pays people and property harmed by a truck on the road, priced by hazard class.
  - **Broker security:** The surety bond that pays carriers and shippers when a go-between keeps freight money it collected.
  - **Cargo liability:** The separate cover that pays for the goods themselves, required by federal rule only for household-goods moves.
- **The Catch:** Nothing here prices a cargo policy or measures how often carriers carry one. The household-goods amounts are a regulated floor, not a market benchmark, so the reader finishes the arithmetic with their own contract.
