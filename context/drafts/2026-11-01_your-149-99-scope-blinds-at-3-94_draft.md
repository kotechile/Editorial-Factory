Inside your finished wall the camera has about three and a half inches of cavity before the far face, and past 3.94 inches it blinds you to everything deeper in the bay. That limit is published only by the $149.99 Teslong TD450S, the one scope in this comparison whose maker states a working distance at all. The Klein ET16 costs $40.58 and the Klein ET19 costs $169.99, and neither page carries a focus range anywhere in its specification table [1][2][4][5].

The cheap one needs an Android phone as its screen. The expensive one has the smallest head and the biggest claim. Only the middle one can be aimed after it is inside the wall, which is the feature that decides whether you learn anything from the hole you cut.

## The big picture:

My read starts with the shape of the problem rather than with the shopping list, because the three cameras fail at different distances and for different reasons.

A head thin enough for the hole is useless if the lens cannot focus on what sits beyond it. Focus range is the number a maker publishes when the lens is fixed, and its absence carries information too [3][4][5]. The TD450S holds focus from 0.79 to 3.94 inches straight ahead and from 0.4 to 2.76 inches on its side lens, measured from the face of the probe [3]. A bay between two-by-four studs at sixteen inches on centre is about fourteen and a half inches wide, so most of what is in there sits past the point where the image keeps its detail.

Then the hole. The heads measure 0.22 in on the ET19, 0.33 in on the TD450S and 0.35 in on the ET16 [3][4][5]. A quarter-inch bit is 6.35 mm, which passes the ET19's head and nothing else in this table, while a three-eighths bit at 9.53 mm passes all three.

And steering. Only the TD450S moves its head, through 210 degrees on a lever beside the grip [3]. The other two bend at the cable and look wherever the bend happens to point them.

## By the numbers

- **$149.99 — The middle price, the only stated focus range:** The TD450S costs $20.00 less than the Klein ET19 and $109.41 more than the ET16, and it is the one camera here whose table says where its lens focuses [1][2][3].
- **3.94 inches — The far edge of the view:** Straight ahead the TD450S holds focus from 0.79 to 3.94 inches; on the side lens the range runs 0.4 to 2.76 inches, so the sideways look is the shorter of the two [3].
- **0.22 in — The smallest head:** The ET19's 5.5 mm head is the only one of the three that passes a quarter-inch hole, which is the hole a homeowner can patch from a tub of filler [4].
- **$75 — The patch underneath the tool:** A professional charges at least $75 to repair small holes up to 4 inches across, and $200 to $550 for several holes in one room, so the camera is the cheap half of this decision [6].

## What I'd watch:

What strikes me is that none of these three failures is about price.

- **Focus against the bay:** The TD450S looks past the near face of the cavity and loses the far stud at 3.94 inches, while neither Klein table lists a working distance at all [3][4][5]. That silence leaves a buyer guessing whether a $169.99 camera focuses at one inch or at ten.
- **A display claim that contradicts itself:** The ET19 description promises a 1920 x1080 monitor, and the same page's specification table prints 480p for that display and 720p for the camera [4]. The TD450S prints 854x480 for its 4.5-inch screen while capturing 1920 x1080 stills from a 1.0 megapixel sensor, so the pixel number measures the chip rather than the view [3].
- **A phone doing the work of a screen:** The ET16 has no display and no battery of its own; it draws power from an Android phone's port and shows 640 x 480 there [5]. That is the whole $109.41 gap to the TD450S, and it is also the reason to buy one for a job you will do once.

I keep coming back to the hole, because it is the part the reader controls. A camera that needs a three-eighths opening can go through a baseboard gap, a coaxial plate or the ceiling of the room below. One that needs a quarter-inch opening can go almost anywhere and still cannot be pointed once it arrives.

## The catch

My view is that the winner is narrower than the price table makes it look.

The TD450S wins because it is the only camera here that can be aimed after it is inside the wall, and because its published focus range is the one number a buyer can check against the depth of an actual cavity. It still cannot see the far end of a bay, and it still asks for a three-eighths hole.

| The situation | What I would buy |
|---|---|
| A stain on a ceiling with the wall intact | The TD450S, aimed through a three-eighths hole at the joist bay above |
| A draft you cannot trace in a finished exterior wall | The TD450S, because the side view reaches the top plate without a second hole |
| Lath and plaster, or a wall you will not repaint | The ET19, whose 0.22 in head passes a quarter-inch hole, accepting a fixed view |
| An already open cavity, or one straight look | The ET16, phone on one end and probe on the other, for $40.58 |
| A duct run longer than five feet | None of these three; the TD450S probe is 5.08 ft and the ET19's 9.8 ft cannot be aimed |

The shared trap is the patch, not the camera. A professional charges at least $75 for small holes up to four inches, so the difference between a quarter-inch hole and a three-eighths hole is not a different repair bill, since both land in the same band [6]. What changes is whether you were allowed to make the hole in the first place, and no focus range or articulation angle answers that for you.

This comparison is built from three manufacturers' specification tables, three price pages and one published repair-cost guide. The cameras were not handled for this article, no wall was opened for it, and no independent measured test of any of the three is cited here. For the spend that sits behind the choice, the desk's [ownership cost model](/calculators/true-cost-of-ownership/) prices the repair and the tool together, and the [home diagnostics hub](/categories/home-diagnostics-tools/) carries the rest of the beat.

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Focus range, not pixel count, is the number that decides whether a wall camera shows you anything.
- **The number:** 3.94 inches, the published far focus of the $149.99 Teslong TD450S.
- **The risk:** None of these three sees the far side of a bay, and every one of them needs a hole in the wall.
