<!-- lead -->

Two-thirds of American homeowners lost power in 2026, three times on average, with the last outage running five and a half hours. In the same month, the storage industry's own report said the home battery market will shrink 4% this year and that five states take 88% of what gets installed. The fear is everywhere. The money is not. [1] [2]

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The home battery has stopped being a bill-savings product. Its two halves are now priced in different places.

Demand is national. A survey run by the polling firm Talker Research covered 2,000 homeowners from August 18 to 24. It found that 47% name backup power as the single upgrade that would make them feel most prepared. That was the top answer, ahead of the next choice by 18 points. [1]

Nearly half of the homes that lost power had already made a change. On readiness, owners graded themselves 6.6 out of 10. [1]

The dark hours cost real things. Of the homes that lost power, 57% lost heating or cooling, 48% threw out food, and 44% lost internet or cell service. Nearly a third slept somewhere else. About 28% of homes run medical gear on the grid: 39% of those keep medication cold, 36% use a CPAP (a breathing machine for sleep apnea), and 30% run oxygen equipment. [1]

The money is not national. American Clean Power and Wood Mackenzie counted 676 megawatts of home storage in the second quarter. That was the fourth-biggest quarter on record, down 15% from the first quarter and up 3% from a year earlier. Five markets took 88% of it: California, Texas, Puerto Rico, Arizona and Illinois. All five fell from the prior quarter. [2]

The industry's own answer is that the utility becomes the buyer of last resort. Its report says the sector is "increasingly positioning virtual power plants (VPPs) as a key enabler of load growth." A VPP is a fleet of home batteries that a utility pays to draw on. [2]

What I keep coming back to is the clock. The federal tax credit that made the hardware cheaper expired on December 31, 2025. It covered 30% of a project, capped at $2,000. The 4% drop forecast for 2026 is built on exactly that. [2] [4]

## By the numbers

- **66% — Homeowners who lost power:** two-thirds of U.S. homeowners reported an outage in 2026, three on average, with the last one lasting 5.5 hours [1].
- **88% — Five states' share:** California, Texas, Puerto Rico, Arizona and Illinois took 88% of home storage installed in Q2 2026, and every one of them fell from the prior quarter [2].
- **46% — Solar-plus-battery attach rate:** the share of new solar paired with storage, up from 41% a year earlier, even as the market's own forecast is a 4% drop for 2026 [2].
- **$500 to $300 — SMUD's battery buy-down:** SMUD (the Sacramento Municipal Utility District) cut its per-kilowatt-hour incentive on September 23, dropping a 13.5 kilowatt-hour Powerwall's check from $5,400 to $3,240 [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

I've been watching three numbers that decide whether a battery is insurance or a decoration.

- **The buy-down floor.** SMUD cut its enrollment incentive by 40% at the margin. It came with a deadline: projects had to be filed for interconnection by September 22 and enrolled by December 31, 2026 to hold the higher tier. Other utilities are re-pricing their programs too. [3]
- **The utility's own bid.** VPP payments are the part of the case that barely existed a few years ago, and the report calls them the growth channel. The catch: a VPP pays for cycles, not for the outage you actually care about. SMUD keeps a 20% reserve in the battery for that. [2] [3]
- **The attachment rate.** 46% of new solar systems now ship with a battery, up from 41%. Owners are already voting for storage where the resilience case gets paid for first. [2]

## The catch

Disclosure first: the outage survey was paid for by Sunrun, which sells batteries and solar. The polling house, the sample and the dates are on the record, so I'd read the direction rather than the decimals.

The honest limit is that this is a national story with a local answer. A home in one of the five big markets is buying a product with a working subsidy and a utility that wants its cycles. A home 500 miles away buys the same box at the same price, with no program and no one to sell the discharge to. Two identical systems, two different paybacks.

The report does not call the drop permanent. It expects the home market to rebound between 2027 and 2031 at about 9% a year. Utility-scale battery prices also fell 2%, to $916 per kilowatt. So the hardware keeps getting cheaper even as the subsidy thins. What I can't answer yet: does cheap hardware close a gap that a state program used to close? [2]

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Two-thirds of U.S. homeowners lost power in 2026, but the home battery market is set to shrink 4% this year and five states already take 88% of installations. The demand for backup and the money that pays for it now sit in different places.
- **Why It Matters:** The federal credit expired at the end of 2025 and utilities are trimming their own buy-downs. A battery's payback now turns on the grid where you live and the program your utility runs, not on your monthly bill.
- **What I'd Watch:** the incentive floor, the utility programs, and how much solar is sold with storage attached.
  - **VPP payments:** money a utility pays a household for letting it draw on the battery during peak events, which is the newest part of the payback.
  - **Enrollment incentive:** a one-time utility payment against the hardware, like SMUD's, which just fell from $500 to $300 per kilowatt-hour.
  - **Attachment rate:** the share of new solar systems sold with a battery, which shows where owners are already choosing backup.
- **The Catch:** the outage figures come from a survey paid for by a battery vendor, five markets dominate the install base, and step-downs are already under way — so the same system can pay back on one utility's turf and not on the next one's.
