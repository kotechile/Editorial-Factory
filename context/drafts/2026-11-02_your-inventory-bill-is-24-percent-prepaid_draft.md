<!-- lead -->
Caterpillar put $4.1 billion of cash into stock in the first nine months of 2026, twice the drain of a year earlier, and its cash from operations still rose 30%, because buyers had already handed it $4.0 billion in advances for machines that had not shipped [1]. That is 24% of the stock on its books paid for before it was built. Tractor Supply, in the same stretch, added 11.2% to its stock with no such line behind it, and its cash from operations fell by a third [2]. The same act of putting more goods on the ground produced opposite cash statements, and the gap is the part of your inventory bill that is already paid.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Both firms had a reason to carry more. Caterpillar's stock rose 22.5% while its nine-month sales rose 21%, so the goods grew roughly in step with the order book, and the size of the build is not the interesting part [1]. The funding under it is. Customer advances, the money a buyer pays before a machine ships and the company books as a contract liability rather than revenue, climbed to $5,329 million from $3,314 million [1][4]. The cash-flow line for those advances put in $4,002 million this year against $1,649 million a year earlier [1].

Tractor Supply's build had no buyer behind it. Stock rose 11.2% while sales rose 3.6%, average stock per store climbed to $1,290.8 thousand from $1,231.9 thousand, and turns slipped to 3.08 from 3.24 over nine months [2]. Same-store sales rose 0.4% against 3.9% a year earlier, and that is the demand the extra goods were ordered to meet [2]. The company borrowed instead: long-term debt rose to $2,230.3 million from $1,744.2 million [2].

I keep coming back to the fact that neither filing calls any of this a financing choice. Caterpillar's report shows the stock as a use of cash and the advances as a source, which is what the statement requires, and Tractor Supply's shows a build and a debt increase. Read side by side, they are one choice priced two ways.

## By the numbers

- **162 days vs 129 — stock on hand:** Caterpillar's $22,214 million of stock against nine-month cost of sales is 162 days of goods, and Tractor Supply's $3,618.8 million against its own cost line is 129, both worked out here from the closing balance [1][2].
- **$5,329 million — customer advances:** Caterpillar's money paid before shipment, up from $3,314 million at the start of the year, with $4,002 million coming in on the cash-flow line against $1,649 million [1].
- **30% up, 33% down — cash from operations:** Caterpillar's nine-month figure rose to $10,593 million from $8,148 million while Tractor Supply's fell to $882.6 million from $1,310.4 million [1][2].
- **3.75% to 4% — policy rate:** the range the Federal Reserve held on Oct. 28, on a 10–2 vote, with two members preferring a quarter-point rise [3].

## What I'd watch:

Three lines answer the question next quarter. The first is Caterpillar's advances balance: if it stops growing while stock keeps rising, the funding has quietly moved to the balance sheet and the cash gain reverses [1]. The second is dealer stock. Caterpillar's mining and heavy-construction sales rose "primarily due to the impact from changes in dealer inventories", so part of that volume was dealers stocking rather than end users buying, and a dealer drawdown would reach the sales line first [1].

The third is payables, the other zero-cost source in the chain. Tractor Supply's accounts payable rose to $1,659.9 million from $1,549.2 million, which covered $110.7 million of a $529.8 million build; the rest came from cash and borrowed money [2]. That is not a scandal, it is what a retailer's terms allow, and the borrowing shows up in the long-term debt line. What I am watching next is whether the December meeting lifts the price of that borrowed part, since two members already voted for a quarter-point rise [3]. The rate is also the smallest piece of [the carrying cost of stock](/inventory-carrying-cost-rate/), which is why a build funded at any rate costs more than the interest line suggests.

## The catch

The comparison is a demonstration, not a market study. An equipment maker can book advances because it sells years before it builds, while a retailer sells at the counter and cannot take a deposit on a bag of feed. So 24% describes Caterpillar's business model rather than a target another firm can copy. Where I have landed is that the share is still the right thing to measure, because the other option is your own money, and the price of your own money is published every six weeks.

The advance is not free in the way it looks, either. It is a debt with a ship date, since the buyer has paid for a machine that must be delivered, which makes the cash behind the build a promise to finish it. If demand softens, the firm holding the deposits is the one that has to ship into the weaker market. Caterpillar's quarter shows the other side of the same margin: $128 million of tariff refunds lifted operating profit while material costs including tariffs still ran through the factory [1].

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** Stock builds now arrive funded two ways, by customer deposits or by the balance sheet.
**The number:** Customers paid for 24% of Caterpillar's stock before it shipped.
**The risk:** A deposit is a promise to ship, and a self-funded build pays the policy rate.
