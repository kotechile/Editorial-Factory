<!-- lead -->
A planning module reaches the budget as one line: a price per user, per month. The cycle it has to run inside is a different order of money. The Institute of Business Forecasting (IBF) puts what it costs to run a sales and operations planning (S&OP) cycle at $250,000 to $750,000 a year. That is the pay of 2.3 to 7 supply chain managers at the 2025 median wage [1][3]. The software sits inside that yearly bill; it does not replace it. So a tool that cannot shorten the cycle is a purchase on top of a program nobody priced.

<!-- tension -->

## The cheapest line to sign

IBF splits an S&OP build into four parts, and only two of them happen once [1]. Acquisition covers the system work, the data pulls, the dashboards and the baseline, at $50,000 to $300,000. The build itself — people's time, a full-time coordinator or project manager, outside advice and training — runs $250,000 to $400,000. Together those come to $300,000 to $700,000 before the first meeting.

Then there is the part that never stops. The yearly operating line pays for the monthly cycle and its reviews, an S&OP champion or lead, and a share of systems spend, and IBF puts it at $250,000 to $750,000 [1]. The same page allows improvement work at about 15 per cent of the one-off. That last line is the one most business cases leave out.

Priced in people, the yearly figure is easier to judge. The Labor Department's 2025 wage data puts the median supply chain manager at $51.55 an hour, or $107,230 a year [3]. The low end of the range works out at 2.33 managers; the high end at 6.99. The comparison is wages only, with no benefits load, so the real headcount worth sits lower rather than higher.

The timing makes the math harder. IBF allows four to six months of design and pilot work before the first review, and only 5 per cent to 10 per cent of the benefit lands at go-live [1]. Break-even arrives about 15.4 months after kickoff, with the rest of the gain spread to year three. What I keep circling is that the yearly bill starts in month one, while the payback starts somewhere in year two.

## By the numbers

- **15.4 months — Break-even from kickoff:** the point at which the program has repaid its build, with 5% to 10% of the benefit banked at go-live [1].
- **8% to 25% — Licence share of the bill:** 25 planning seats at $210.00 a month is $63,000 a year, against a cycle that already counts its systems spend [4][1].
- **2.4% — Mature-process gain:** IBF's average for a process at the third of four maturity levels, split 1.8% operational and 0.6% revenue, with a 9% to 14% stock cut beside it [1].

## What buyers are asking now

The buyers who treat the cycle as the cost ask a different question in the demo. Their question is which recurring hours the tool removes: the coordinator's year, the hours each review takes, or the work of matching the plan to the ledger. The ones who treat the licence as the cost ask for a discount. My read is that only the first question still matters a year later, and it is the one a sales team is least able to answer.

Before a module is asked to cut those hours, [forecast value added](/forecast-value-added-lose-to-last-period/) is the test of whether the layer it feeds earns anything at all. A layer that loses to last period's actuals is already a cost, and no licence fixes that.

The case for running the cycle is strong, and IBF makes it in its own numbers: a 63 per cent annual return after three years for an average $500 million company [1]. That is an argument for pricing the cycle well, not for skipping the price.

The features that close a planning deal are review steps by another name. A scenario round, a sign-off session and an exception queue — the list of forecasts that broke a rule — each book named people for a fixed hour. At $51.55 an hour [3], one extra hour a week for one attendee is $2,681 a year. What the onboarding plan shows is whether the tool adds meetings, and whether the business case counts the hours they take.

## The catch

These are a trade body's estimates from 2021, with no sample size and no method note attached. One line of the breakdown does not even reconcile with its own percentage rule. I read them as a shape rather than a market price: the yearly line runs from about a third of the one-off to two and a half times it, and it repeats.

The benefit side is thinner than the cost side. The page that says a 15 per cent gain in accuracy brings a 3 per cent pre-tax gain never says what the 3 per cent is measured against [2]. The one checkable figure is $1.52 million per point of under-forecast — planning below what customers then order — at a $1 billion business [2].

Three years of the program's own cost, $1,095,000 to $3,055,000 on IBF's ranges [1], is the hurdle that figure has to clear. On those numbers the program has to buy back 0.7 to 2.0 points to cover itself, which is the test a finance partner can actually run.

None of this says S&OP is not worth running. It says the line that decides the budget is the one that repeats every year, and a licence that adds a review step is a cost dressed as a skill.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** Planning approvals still price the licence, not the annual cycle that runs the plan.
**The number:** 2.3 to 7 supply chain managers a year, the cycle against the 2025 median wage.
**The risk:** Every review feature that justifies the module adds standing attendee hours.
