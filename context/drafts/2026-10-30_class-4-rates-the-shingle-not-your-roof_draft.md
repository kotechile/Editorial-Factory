<!-- lead -->
After two seasons of small hail, an aged roof is up to ten times more likely to break under the next big stone than a new one, and no Class 4 shingle label was ever built to tell you that [2]. Class 4 is the top rung of a lab impact test — Underwriters Laboratories (UL) standard 2218 — run on a new sample, and the class records how that sample held up [1]. That is the whole of what the label says. Texas, where the credit rule gets quoted most, asks for the same test and a class from 1 to 4, with Class 4 earning the top credit a carrier will give [1]. The roof the shingle lands on is a different object, and it is not new.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Asphalt covers about 80% of US home roofs, so the shingle is where a hail decision lands [3]. A roof gets picked once and then spends fifteen years being aged by weather nobody writes down. The test and the roof run on different clocks: UL 2218 looks at a new sample, and the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety (IBHS) says flatly that today's standards do not test how a product holds up over time, or across the sizes and counts of hail a roof meets [2]. What I keep coming back to is the size of that gap.

The damage adds up quietly. Small stones, under an inch across, fall far more often than the ones that make the news, and each pass strips the granules that shield the asphalt from sun and water [2]. Two lab events on top of normal weather moved the damage response by a factor of ten [2]. So the class on a wrapper and the state of a roof are two different questions, and only one of them is printed on the label.

The credit splits the same way, and not in the direction the ads suggest. Texas requires a discount, then lets each company set the size of it, the test it will accept, the label it wants and the form a contractor must file [1]. The class is standard and the money is not — the same split the desk found on [the discount side of a roof file](/your-49-discount-isnt-all-roof/).

## By the numbers

- **2218 — the test number:** UL standard 2218 is the impact test a covering must pass before any class applies, and it is what a Texas credit is tied to rather than a brand or a price [1].
- **2.8% — wind-and-hail claim rate:** wind and hail is the top cause of home claims, with that share of insured homes filing one in 2018-2022, against an average $20,062 paid per claim in 2023 [6].
- **902 — Texas hail events:** hail of an inch or more was logged 902 times in Texas in 2025, more than any other state, against 244 in Colorado and 5,432 across the country, up from 5,373 in 2024 [5].

## What I'd watch:

- **The five label items:** since 1999 a maker has had to print the UL label, the class, its own name, the year made and the brand on the shingle, and a product that passes the test without those marks earns nothing under the Texas wind pool's rules [1].
- **The installer's form:** once the roof is on, the contractor files Texas Department of Insurance (TDI) form PC068, which is the step that turns a passing product into a credit [1].
- **The rating, not the class:** IBHS buys shingles at retail and rates them side by side so gaps between brands and product years can be seen [3]. A roofer who can name a product's rating is offering more than one who quotes a class.
- **The aged-panel question:** the 2026 field season ran new shingles and one-year-aged modified shingles through repeated hail, in a year the team never met a baseball-size stone [4].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The ten-times figure is a lab comparison between weathered, hit panels and new shingles. That is not a failure rate on a real roof, and IBHS's own wording carries the "up to" that a pitch drops [2]. What I find hard to square is that the honest version is still awkward: no one publishes a durability rating, so a buyer weighing two Class 4 products has no measured basis beyond the retail-bought test IBHS runs.

There is a second limit that cuts against the upgrade case. The credit is priced by the carrier, so the payback hangs on a rate filing and not on the shingle, and where the credit is small or missing the label protects the house and not the premium.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

**The shift:** Impact-rated roofing is sold by class, and the class measures a new sample rather than the aged roof the hail hits.
**The number:** Up to ten times — how much more fragile IBHS's weathered, twice-hit panels were than new shingles.
**The risk:** Nothing certifies that a given Class 4 product holds up as it ages, and the credit is priced per carrier.
