<!-- lead -->
The tax-free swap died behind a 25% line on September 28, when the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) ruled that moving low-basis stock into a new fund, and letting that fund hand the stock to a broker, is a taxable sale [1]. The same paperwork named the pooled version as the next idea under review, with comments due October 28 [2]. The trade press read it as the end of exchange funds [9]. The text says less than that, and the gap decides who is really exposed.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The ruling walks through three steps. A holder puts stock with a big built-in gain into a brand new fund. The fund sells shares to a broker, taking cash or stock that fits the fund's own theme. The fund then buys that broker out by handing over the holder's original stock [1].

Each step had a rule behind it. The first leaned on a code section that lets property move into a company for stock without tax. The second leaned on a rule that lets a fund hand out shares in kind without booking a gain [1]. Put together, the holder ended up with a spread-out basket and owed nothing.

The IRS said the mix fails. It called the fund a pipe, and treated the holder as having sold the stock to the broker at market price [1]. That is old reasoning, not new law, aimed at a structure built in the past few years [1][8].

What strikes me is how few people the wrapper ever fit. The first step only worked if the stock was already spread out: no single company above 25% of the pile, and five or fewer companies holding no more than half [2]. Anyone with 40% of their money in one employer's stock failed that test before this ruling existed.

So the ruling did not shut the door on the holder with one big position. It shut the door on people who were already spread out and wanted a different mix without paying for it.

## By the numbers

- **25% — Single-stock ceiling:** The limit a portfolio had to clear, on the way to a second test capping five or fewer names at half its value [2]. One large holding fails both by its own arithmetic.
- **7 years — Lock on the stock:** Stock given to a partnership that is then handed to another partner inside seven years makes the giver book the gain, and a payout back to the giver is measured on the same clock [3][4]. That is the timer behind the pooled fund.
- **37% — Top rate on the gain:** The 2026 top bracket starts above $640,600 for a single filer and $768,700 for a couple filing jointly [5], plus a 3.8% net investment income tax [6]. That bill is what the wrapper was built to push back.
- **$72,000 — Retirement cap:** The 2026 limit on what can go into a workplace plan from both sides combined [7]. It is the other sheltered place a sale can land, and it is small next to a seven-figure holding.

## What I'd watch:

The notice asks for arguments; it is not a rule. Treasury asked which similar trades have different facts and deserve different treatment, and set October 28 for written comments [2]. I have been reading the law-firm notes on this all week, and the sharp ones argue about scope rather than whether the ruling was right [8].

- **The comment file:** What pooled funds file by October 28 tells you whether the partnership version gets carved out or written into the next guidance [2].
- **The label risk:** The notice says future guidance could name a trade as a transaction of interest or a listed transaction [2]. That adds reporting duties, and it can reach back to funds already running.
- **The look-back clause:** Any guidance "could apply prospectively only or retroactively to transactions that already have taken place" [2]. A fund joined in 2024 is not safe just because it predates the paper.
- **The 20% slice:** The version Treasury describes needs at least 20% of the pool's assets in something other than stock or bonds, which is how it argues the pool is not a fund [2]. If that slice stops working, the pooling math stops working.
- **The exam route:** An examiner can already challenge one of these trades under current law, without waiting for new guidance [2]. Enforcement runs beside the rule-making, not behind it.

## The catch

My read is that the obituary named the wrong patient. The notice says plainly that it does not address other trades involving exchange funds, and that it takes no view on them [2]. It also steps back from a transfer that seeds a fund with stock matching that fund's own theme, meant to stay there [2]. A plain pooled fund, run to hold for years, is not the trade this paper describes.

The real limits sit elsewhere. The seven-year clock is written into the law, not into a fund contract, so the tax break unwinds if the stock comes back inside it [3][4]. The swap also keeps the original cost basis, so the bill is pushed forward rather than erased, and an heir who inherits the position lands somewhere a seller never can.

I could be wrong about the timing. Treasury may decide the partnership version is different enough to leave alone, and then this is a story about a wrapper almost nobody could use.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** On September 28 the IRS ruled that putting low-basis stock into a new fund, then having the fund swap that stock to a broker, is a taxable sale rather than a free transfer [1]. The same notice named the pooled version of the trade as a strategy under study [2].
- **Why It Matters:** The wrapper was sold as a free way to spread a big position, and it only ever worked below a 25% single-stock ceiling [2]. The ruling lands on the holder who was already spread out, while the holder with one big name still faces the pooled fund and its seven-year lock [3].
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The comment deadline:** Written comments close October 28, and what funds argue about scope will shape whatever comes next [2].
  - **A listed-transaction label:** The notice holds open the option of naming one of these trades, which would add reporting duties after the fact [2].
  - **Reach-back:** Guidance could cover trades already done, which is the gap between an old plan and an open bill [2].
- **The Catch:** The notice does not address plain exchange funds and says so outright, so the "exchange funds are dead" read goes too far [2]. The break is also not free: it carries a seven-year clock and keeps the old cost basis [3][4].
