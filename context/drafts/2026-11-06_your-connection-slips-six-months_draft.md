<!-- lead -->
In October, Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E) warned about 25,000 hookups that they could be stuck as long as six months past the date they were given [1]. The cause was not a shortage of crews, copper or transformers. It was a vote: after Senate Bill 492, a wildfire-liability measure, died on the last night of the session, the utility put off about $2 billion of its 2027 work [4]. That reorders a home project. A heat pump, a battery, a new panel and an extra dwelling all end at the same application, and the application is now the slowest step.

## At a glance
- **The shift:** A failed liability bill pushed utility work into a queue that reaches front doors.
- **The number:** 25,000 people told a hookup could arrive six months late [1].
- **The risk:** The meter, not the machine, sets the date your hardening works.

<!-- tension -->
## A liability bill hits your panel

The order of events matters, because each step was a choice. Lawmakers would not vote on Senate Bill 492 on the final night. The next day PG&E cut its 2027 building plan from $13.4 billion to $11.4 billion [4]. The company gives one reason: California makes a utility pay for fire damage caused by its gear, even when it followed the safety rules, and that risk lifts the interest it pays [3]. My read is that the finance side is not really in dispute. The remedy is.

Then came the letters. In October, about 25,000 people who were waiting for power were told the wait could run six months past their date [1]. The list included data centres, larger housing projects and clean-energy jobs. At a hearing on 28 October, a committee heard that more than 60 clean-power and storage projects due by 2030, at least 15 gigawatts, now have no firm date [2]. Housing and office hookups are being quoted at one to six months. City and county works got a vaguer answer: 2027 and beyond [2].

That is where a home plan loses its footing. A family that wants to cut its own risk needs the utility to turn the work on. A heat pump, a battery, a panel that can carry both: each one ends at the meter. The same line holds the data centres and the solar farms, so the household is not racing a crew. It is waiting on a budget set by an argument it cannot join.

## By the numbers
- **25,000 hookups:** The people PG&E warned could wait six months past their date [1].
- **15 gigawatts:** Power in the 60-plus clean and storage projects now facing no firm date [2].
- **$11.4 billion:** PG&E's 2027 building budget, down from $13.4 billion after the bill failed [4].

## Where this bites a renovation

The people closest to the fight are arguing about who pays. Consumer Watchdog's Jamie Court told NBC Bay Area (NBC) that the utility is using the hookups as leverage, and he called it "pure, pure blackmail" [1]. The Utility Reform Network's Mark Toney gave KTVU (a Bay Area television station) a narrower case. Burying line costs about $4 million a mile, he said, and insulated overhead line runs near one-fifth of that, so "ratepayers do pay the interest" either way [4]. PG&E answers that the delayed projects are not yet in rates, which is its reply to the claim that customers have already paid [3].

What I keep coming back to is the order of work inside one house. A service upgrade and the gear it feeds are usually bought as one job. The gear can then sit in a garage for months while the meter waits. A homeowner can check the panel's spare room before assuming it needs more amps; the sums behind the 80% test are set out in [Your Panel Doesn't Need More Amps: The 80% Test](/panel-upgrade-80-percent-test/). The point is narrow but useful. A job that runs on the service already in place does not join the queue.

There is a second timing question, and the hearing made it plain. A project that misses the 2030 tax-credit deadline faces "at least a 30% increase in the cost of those goods", said Jan Smutny-Jones of the Independent Energy Producers Association [2]. He also said the state may have to keep more than 3 gigawatts of old coastal gas plants running. That is a maybe, not a decision. It still belongs beside the household's own clock, because a delay that slows new plants and keeps old ones lit is paid in a second currency.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch

The letters warn of a risk. They do not show that 25,000 hookups have already slipped, and no public list says which jobs moved. The part I keep circling is how little of this a family can check. PG&E also says its safety work is untouched, and it points to four years without a major fire started by its gear. The honest limit is narrower than the headline. A family cannot look up its place in the line, cannot read the building plan, and cannot tell from a letter whether its own date will hold. What it can do is get the date in writing before it signs for equipment.

That date is the one part of the job it does not control.
