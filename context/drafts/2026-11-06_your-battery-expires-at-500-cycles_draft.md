Your phone's battery carries a printed expiry, and it is not a date. Apple rates the battery in an iPhone 14, and every model before it, for 500 full charge cycles at 80 percent of original capacity [1]. iFixit, which sells the replacement kits, reads that same rating as roughly 18 to 24 months of ordinary use, after which the phone asks to be charged far more often and may start to run slower [4]. That is the quiet reason a two-year-old handset feels finished while its screen and camera still work. The part that resets the counter lists at $47 to $99, so the third year of a phone is a parts decision, not a handset decision [6].

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** A battery's life is published as a cycle count, not a warranty period.
- **The number:** 500 full cycles, Apple's rating for an iPhone 14 and earlier.
- **The risk:** Normal wear is excluded from warranty, so the swap is the owner's bill.

<!-- tension -->

## Why the counter beats the calendar

A charge cycle is not a plug-in. Apple defines one as using all of a battery's power, which can take several days of partial charges rather than a single run to empty [3]. Capacity fades with each of those cycles, and the rating simply says where 80 percent sits. Charge twice a day and the same battery reaches its printed end in half the time it takes on an every-other-day habit, which is why two owners of one model can be years apart.

Apple's own page splits the generations, and the split is the interesting part. The 500-cycle line covers the iPhone 14 and everything older; from the iPhone 15 the rating doubles to 1000 cycles at the same 80 percent threshold [2]. The iFixit guide for that phone repeats the doubled number [5]. The page does not say what changed, and I found no source that separates a better cell from a different way of counting. What a buyer sees is two numbers on one page, a whole generation apart.

The European Union has since put a floor under both. Its ecodesign rule requires batteries in phones and slate tablets placed on the market from 20 June 2025 to withstand at least 800 charge and discharge cycles while keeping 80 percent of their initial capacity [8]. That minimum is 1.6 times Apple's older rating and four fifths of its newer one, which makes the regulation a useful yardstick for reading a spec sheet written by the seller.

The warranty is where the timeline turns. Apple's one-year cover excludes batteries that wear down from normal use, and its AppleCare plan replaces a battery at no extra charge only once capacity drops below 80 percent [2]. Put beside iFixit's estimate, the rated end of life lands after the free-repair window has closed.

## By the numbers

- **500 full cycles:** Apple's rating for the battery in an iPhone 14 and earlier, at 80 percent of original capacity [1].
- **18 to 24 months:** iFixit's estimate of how long that rating lasts for most users [4].
- **800 cycles:** The European Union's minimum for phones placed on its market from 20 June 2025 [8].

## What the swap actually buys

Priced per rated cycle, the part is cheap. An iPhone 12 kit at $46.99 against 500 cycles works out to about 9 cents a cycle, while Apple's $89 service on the same model is about 18 cents [6]. The newer generation prices the same way: a 1000-cycle kit at $49.99 works out to about 5 cents a cycle [7]. That is the whole economics of keeping a handset: the wear item is priced like a consumable, not like a repair.

In months it reads the same way. The kit comes to roughly $2 to $2.60 a month of extra life on the older generation, and Apple's service to $3.70 to $4.95 [6]. A first-timer can do the job in 30 minutes to a couple of hours with the tools in the box, according to the same page [6]. My read is that the cheapest line on the bill is the one that decides whether the device is worth keeping at all.

A quote still has to be compared against something. The desk's own [repair-or-replace calculator](/calculators/tech-debt-repair/) takes a repair estimate and a replacement price and returns the ratio the decision turns on, which is the number to look at before a slow phone becomes a shopping trip.

## The catch

The duration iFixit prints beside the rating is a repair vendor's gloss, not a measurement. The guide states no sample and no method behind it, so it describes the range its own customers report rather than a laboratory result [4]. Apple's cycle count is the number to hold on to, and the duration is only what that count converts to.

The doubling is unexplained as well. Two ratings sit on one page with no note on what changed between them, and a reader who quotes the newer number as proof that batteries got twice as durable is quoting a spec revision [2]. It may be a better cell. It may be a different test. The published record does not say.

What I keep coming back to is how ordinary the wear is. Nothing fails, nothing is recalled, and the phone simply asks for the charger earlier each month until its owner calls it old. A published cycle count turns that drift into a date you can plan around, which is more than most of the spec sheet offers. It still will not tell you whether the screen survives the next drop, or whether every slow phone is a tired battery.
