On Sept 30, 2026, Gov. Gavin Newsom signed SB 868, making California the biggest market in America for "balcony solar" — a panel you hang off a rail or fence and plug straight into a standard wall outlet, no permit and no utility permission [1][2]. The law lets any renter or homeowner run up to 1,200 watts of certified panels per home — enough to power a refrigerator and a few small appliances — and takes effect Jan. 1, 2027 [2][4].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

California has the most expensive residential electricity in the lower 48 — roughly double the U.S. average across its three big investor-owned utilities [5] — and a rooftop-solar industry that cratered after the state gutted its net-metering rules. I've been watching this for months, and what strikes me is how much the fix has narrowed: instead of another rooftop subsidy, the state just legalized the cheapest possible entry point, a device you buy off a shelf and install yourself.

The people this helps are the ones rooftop solar never reached: renters, condo owners, and anyone without the five-figure outlay a full rooftop system demands. Panels run $300 to $2,200 [3]. The people it worries are the utilities — Pacific Gas & Electric and San Diego Gas & Electric opposed the bill, arguing it would push grid costs onto households without panels [3].

## By the numbers

- **1,200 watts — Home limit:** the most a single dwelling can run, and the devices must be certified by UL or an equivalent safety lab [2].
- **$300–$2,200 — Panel cost:** the range of plug-in kits already on sale, installed by the customer rather than a contractor [3].
- **35 states — Plug-in wave:** how many statehouses weighed plug-in solar laws in 2026, after Utah passed the first one in 2025 [2].
- **2030 — Sunset year:** when the no-permit exemption expires unless lawmakers extend it — a last-minute utility-backed addition to the bill [2].

## What I'd watch:

- **Certification race:** the products on shelves today don't yet meet California's safety standard, so buyers still have to register them like rooftop solar until certified models land — advocates expect them around spring 2027 [3].
- **The sunset fight:** Bright Saver's Kevin Chou calls the 2030 repeal a "shadow" over the bill; his bet is that so many panels will be on balconies by then that no legislator will vote to take the law away [2].
- **Who certifies first:** EWG says at least two companies expect UL-type approval by the end of the year, which would put compliant products on shelves in 2027 [4].
- **Utility pushback:** PG&E and SDG&E fought the bill on cost-shift grounds; I'd want to see whether they challenge the 2030 renewal or the certification rules next [3].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The honest limitation is the one the headlines skip: a plug-in panel won't power your house in a blackout. The law requires an anti-islanding feature that shuts the device off when the grid goes down, so it can't hurt a lineworker — which means no outage backup, just cheaper daytime power [3]. And even the savings are modest: proponents estimate up to 20% of a home's electricity and roughly $500 a year for a small apartment [3]. My read is this is an affordability bill first and a resilience bill a distant second — the real payoff is shaving a record-high bill, not riding out a blackout.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** California legalized plug-in solar — a $300–$2,200 panel you plug into a wall outlet with no permit or utility permission, up to 1,200 watts per home [1][2].
- **Why It Matters:** It opens home solar to renters and condo owners for the first time, in a state with the nation's most expensive electricity [5].
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **Certified models:** whether UL-certified systems actually hit shelves by spring 2027, since today's products don't meet the new standard [3].
  - **The 2030 sunset:** whether lawmakers renew the no-permit exemption or let it lapse [2].
  - **Utility pushback:** whether PG&E and SDG&E keep fighting the cost-shift they raised during the debate [3].
- **The Catch:** plug-in panels shut off in a blackout (anti-islanding), so this cuts your bill, not your outage risk — and it saves only up to ~$500 a year on a small apartment [3].
