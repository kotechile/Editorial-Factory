<!-- lead -->
The 61-day window behind your loss harvest costs the position, not just the trade. It reaches back 30 days before the sale, so a purchase you made last month can void the deduction before the trade settles, and the loss then moves into the replacement shares instead of onto your return. That one rule is what turns "harvest the loss" into a real decision for anyone who holds a large block of employer stock.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Loss harvesting is sold as a way to bank a loss without giving up the position: sell, buy something similar, stay invested. That works when the replacement is another fund tracking the same index. It fails on a single stock.

The test is whether the new shares are "substantially identical" to the old ones, and the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) says stock of one company is not, as a rule, the same as stock of another [5]. Buy a different company and you have changed the bet, which is not a harvest at all. Buy the same company inside the band and the loss is disallowed [1].

What strikes me here is how hard that cuts against the usual story about concentration. The deduction is worth most to the reader who means to keep the position, and that is the reader the rule will not help. The holder who had already decided to sell down collects it for free, because the band costs nothing that was not already being given up.

## By the numbers

- **61 days — Window length:** the band opens 30 days before the sale and closes 30 days after it; any purchase of the same stock inside it disallows the loss [1][2].
- **$3,000 — Slice against salary:** losses come off realised gains with no cap at all, and only the rest can lower other income, by up to $3,000, or $1,500 on a separate return [3][4].
- **20 years — $60,000 at $3,000 a year:** a loss that size, with no gains to soak it up, becomes a two-decade deduction; that sum is mine, not the code's [3][4].
- **Day 31 — First clean re-buy:** the period ends 30 days after the sale, so the first buy outside the band falls on the day after [1].

## What I'd watch:

Nearly all of the risk lives in the calendar rather than the tax form, because the band runs backwards as well as forwards.

- **The purchase before the sale:** a buy through an employee stock purchase plan (ESPP) or a broker's automatic buy inside the previous 30 days can void a sale that looked clean [1][2].
- **Where a voided loss goes:** it is put off rather than lost. The new shares take the old shares' basis, adjusted for the gap in price, and the loss is counted when that lot is sold [6].
- **The half that pays properly:** against realised gains the offset has no dollar cap, which is why a holder with other positions to close gets far more from a harvest than $3,000 suggests [3].
- **The open question:** the rule reaches a buy "by purchase or by an exchange upon which the entire amount of gain or loss was recognized", and it does not say where a share handed over at vesting falls [1].

I would want to read how a plan manager describes vesting before treating the work as done.

## The catch

My read is that the honest version of this rule is narrower than the way it gets sold. No source here gives a tax rate, so nothing in this piece turns the deduction into dollars of tax saved, and any figure that did would rest on a guessed bracket.

Two more limits are worth naming. The ban has carve-outs for a sale made in the seller's own trade or business, and for a stock dealer acting in the ordinary course [7], and neither reaches a salaried engineer. Whether two holdings are "substantially identical" is also settled on the facts of a case, not by a set definition [5], so a plan holding shares of a subsidiary or a successor needs its own read before anyone leans on the sums.

What I keep circling is the lopsided part. The rule is cheap to satisfy for someone who was selling anyway and costly for someone who was not, and the twenty-year figure above is the worst case, where no gain ever arrives to soak up the loss.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** a loss harvested on employer stock is disallowed when you buy the same shares inside a 61-day band that opens 30 days before the sale, not after it.
- **Why It Matters:** the deduction's value depends on whether you were keeping the position or leaving it, and only the departing holder collects the loss without also paying for the band.
- **What I'd Watch:** where the calendar and the basis land when a fall at vest turns into a harvest.
  - **The look-back:** buys made in the 30 days before a sale count, so an automatic plan buy can void a harvest that looked clean.
  - **The basis rule:** a voided loss moves into the new lot and is counted when that lot is sold, so the deduction is put off rather than forfeited.
  - **The gains offset:** losses come off realised gains in full, and only the rest is capped at $3,000 against other income.
- **The Catch:** no rate is given here, the rule's text does not settle how a vesting date counts, and the twenty-year figure assumes no future gain soaks up the loss.
