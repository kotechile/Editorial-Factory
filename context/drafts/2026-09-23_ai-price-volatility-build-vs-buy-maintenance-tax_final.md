Three Artificial Intelligence (AI) vendors changed their prices five times in just eight weeks this summer [1]. OpenAI cut one model by 80% and another by 20% on the same day, then trimmed its top model weeks later. Each price drop creates a new work ticket for your tech team.

<!-- tension -->
**The big picture:** Artificial Intelligence (AI) dropped the upfront cost to build custom tools. What once took months now ships in days, but this speed turns your final tool into a moving target [2]. Models, prices, and vendors now change monthly. 

**Why it matters:** The engineers fixing your custom tool are the exact people you need focused on growth. Every hour they spend chasing a vendor's price change is an hour lost on your main product [1].

The build-vs-buy choice used to rely on the sticker price. Now it depends entirely on the long-term upkeep costs [1][2].

**By the numbers:** The hidden costs to build your own software add up fast.
- **$375,000:** The payroll cost for two engineers to build a custom tool for nine months, before it even works [1].
- **5 price events:** Three vendors changed costs in eight weeks, including cuts from OpenAI and Google [1].
- **$125k–$250k/year:** The permanent upkeep cost—half to one full engineer forever—for a tool that seemed free to build [1].
- **76.6%:** The share of Software as a Service (SaaS) buyers who face surprise costs after they sign a deal [4].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## Decide on the long tail

**The playbook:** Tech leaders must make four specific moves when choosing to build or buy this quarter.

- **Price both paths in engineer-years:** Compare the upfront build and the permanent upkeep using your exact salary numbers [1]. If you do not track Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) in time and payroll, you lack a real answer.
- **Treat provider churn as a cost:** Every price change, model launch, and new tool creates a work ticket [1]. Ask the vendor if managing those changes is their job or yours.
- **Build narrow or strategic only:** A short script works well for one vendor and one team. The costly mistake lives in the middle: tools too complex for a script but not vital enough to staff forever [1][2].
- **Run the reversibility test:** Buying is easy to reverse when a contract ends. A failed custom build wastes sunk payroll and leaves a team stuck with a tool no one wants [1].

One finance firm built a custom dashboard to dodge a $20,000 yearly fee. They spent $240,000 in salaries over two years to keep it running before they finally scrapped it. The free tool became the most costly item on their plan.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## Do not buy everything

**The catch:** The main source here—CloudZero—openly sells the buy side. But even they admit building wins for narrow problems, custom billing models, strict data rules, or massive scale [1].

Buy common tools, but build your core features. You should buy basic AI tools and cost trackers. Keep your main business logic and custom data flows in-house. A vendor's price change is a headache, but you must never rent your true edge.

- **The Winning Moves:** Decide based on total long-term cost, not the sticker price.
  - **Calculate engineer-years:** Price both options over their full life using your exact salary numbers.
  - **Count provider churn:** Treat every vendor price change and model launch as a new work ticket.
  - **Build narrow or strategic:** Script simple tasks, buy common tools, and save custom builds for your true edge.
  - **Test reversibility:** Remember that buying is easy to reverse, but a failed custom build wastes permanent payroll.
- **The Catch:** Do not overcorrect and buy everything. Building still wins for core business logic, custom data flows, and narrow technical problems.
