<!-- lead -->
Slow movers are not the fringe of a catalogue. In one warehouse's speed study, the two slowest classes of stock-keeping unit (SKU) — the C and D movers — were 67.76% of the items on file. They held 60.7% of the units on hand and produced 49.8% of the picking work [1]. Your stock report shows them at a healthy gross margin (the sale price minus what the item cost to buy). That is the trap: the screen the pruning decision runs on cannot see what the tail costs.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

Every yearly review runs the same scorecard. It sorts SKUs by revenue, margin and turns. It cuts the bottom. The bottom keeps surviving, and the shelves keep filling.

Gross margin answers one question. Does this item sell for more than it cost to buy? It says nothing about what the item cost to keep. What strikes me here is that three costs sit outside that answer, and the tail pays all three.

The first is capital-days: the units on hand times how long they sit. The second is floor space. A slow mover holds its slot for a year while a fast mover turns a whole season in the same space. The third is the write-off. The average maker carries 20-30% of its stock as dead or obsolete goods, which on a $3 million inventory is $600,000 to $900,000 written off later [3].

Put those three against a margin figure and the ranking changes. In one consumer-goods case, 68 of 156 SKUs (44 percent) earned just 8 percent of revenue. The bottom 100 items lost money once fully loaded costs were counted — after they had read positive on margin [2].

## By the numbers

- **67.76% — Share of the catalogue in the tail:** The C and D speed classes were two-thirds of the SKUs on file, against 16.54% for the fast A and B movers [1].
- **60.7% — Share of units on hand:** The tail held most of the stock for 49.8% of the picking work, and the slowest class held 10.64% of the units at 0% of the work [1].
- **$600,000 to $900,000 — Write-off on a $3 million inventory:** What the average maker's 20-30% share of dead stock eventually costs [3].
- **50% reserve at 181-365 days:** One published reserve policy books 0% at 0-90 days, 25% at 91-180 days, 50% at 181-365 days and 100% past a year [5]. The health target most firms set is dead stock below 3% of stock value [4].

## What I'd watch:

I've been reading the pruning case studies this week. The operators who fixed this did not cut harder. They changed what they measured.

- **Stock held per unit of work:** The number that separates the tail is units on hand against units shipped, not margin. In the speed study the tail carried 60.7% of the units for 49.8% of the work, while the fast movers carried 28.66% of the units for 50.2% [1]. My read: a stock-to-work ratio above 1 marks the cut list with no margin input at all.
- **The reserve, booked first:** The consumer-goods case shows the lag. Write-offs had risen 340 percent over five years, and forecast accuracy outside the top 50 SKUs averaged only 42 percent [2]. The tail was also the least predictable stock in the building. The reserve policy that answers it is an aging ladder: 25 percent at 91-180 days, 50 percent at 181-365, 100 percent past a year [5]. Book it before the review and cheap items stop looking cheap.
- **The floor, priced in feet:** One storage vendor says the long tail takes about 80 percent of warehouse space for 20 percent of revenue [6]. That is a vendor claim, but it points the same way as the speed table.
- **The write-off line, watched quarterly:** The health target most firms set for dead stock is below 3 percent of value [4]. A trend line against that number is the cheapest early warning the tail gives.

The field note I keep coming back to: a multi-brand apparel retailer whose bottom 30 percent of slow movers read positive on margin and about −18 percent net once pick travel, pallet fees and markdowns were counted. Cutting the bottom 2,400 items lifted warehouse throughput 14 percent and freed 38,000 square feet.

## The catch

A slow mover is sometimes cheap insurance, and this math will not tell you which ones. A spare part that keeps a line running is worth far more than its holding cost. An item that anchors a customer's whole order is a different asset from one that anchors nothing.

The evidence is also thinner than the claim sounds. The speed shares come from one vendor's own client study, not a survey [1]. The 44 percent and the loss-making line come from a single case written up secondhand [2]. The 20-30% dead-stock share is an industry reference, not a government measure [3].

What survives is the direction, not the precision. Margin does not price capital-days, floor space or the write-off. I could be wrong that a stock-to-work ratio alone is enough to cut on. I am fairly confident it beats gross margin as a first cut.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The slow tail of a catalogue is most of the stock and most of the floor — in one warehouse's speed study the C and D movers were 67.76% of SKUs and held 60.7% of on-hand units for 49.8% of the picking work [1]. Margin cannot see that.
- **Why It Matters:** A cull scored on margin keeps the items that eat capital-days, floor space and the write-off reserve, and the average maker holds 20-30% of stock as dead goods [3].
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether cull lists start being ranked on stock per unit of work and on booked reserve instead of margin.
  - **Stock-to-work ratio:** Units on hand divided by units shipped for the same item. It flags the tail with no margin input [1].
  - **Aging reserve ladder:** The reserve booked by age — 25% at 91-180 days, 50% at 181-365, 100% past a year [5].
  - **The 3% ceiling:** The dead-stock target most firms set as a share of stock value [4].
- **The Catch:** One vendor's client study and one case carry the numbers, service-critical slow movers are not cut candidates, and the dead-stock share is an industry reference rather than a census.
