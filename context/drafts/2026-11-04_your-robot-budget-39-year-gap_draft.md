A warehouse automation budget is approved as one number. Robots, racking, a panel upgrade and the steel deck that holds it all arrive in one capital request. Federal tax law splits that number into schedules decades apart. New machines and interior work can be written off in full in the year they are ready for use [1][2]. Money spent on making the building bigger, or on its frame, is 39-year property and comes back a few percent at a time [1]. That is your robot budget's 39-year gap. A $700,000 building line is about $17,950 a year on that schedule, against $700,000 in one year on the other. Same project, same cases moved, and about $682,000 of difference in year one [1][3].

<!-- tension -->

## Two schedules inside one approval

The long schedule is not aimed at warehouses. A building is written off over 39 years, and its first year counts from the middle of the month it enters service [1]. The code also has a middle class for work done inside one. It is called qualified improvement property: work on the interior of a building, put in service after the building itself, written off over 15 years [1].

Three kinds of spend drop out of that class. They are an enlargement, an elevator or escalator, and the building's internal frame [1]. A second rule then decides whether a purchase goes at once or over years. The extra first-year allowance reaches property written off over 20 years or less [1][4]. That covers the robots and the 15-year interior work. It does not reach 39-year property [1].

So one construction weekend can produce two answers. A slab poured inside the existing footprint, to carry a goods-to-person grid, is interior work. An extension built to house the same grid is not [1].

My read is that operators have treated this as the accountant's problem. It is not. The accountant sees the invoice and the closing papers, not the drawings. The drawings are where the class is decided. A deck that sits inside the shell, on the existing floor, is described differently from one that adds floor area or braces the frame. On a $700,000 line, that description is worth about thirty-nine times the first-year write-off [1][3].

## Where the split gets decided

The buyers who come out cleanly do one thing before the purchase order. They break the estimate into robots, interior work and shell work, and keep the three lines apart through approval. A turnkey price does not merge them in law. It does hide which line is which, until someone reads the drawings [1].

What I keep coming back to is how much of this is a calendar question. Property is in service when it is ready and available for its specific use [2]. The tax service's own example is a machine that arrived last year but was not working until this year. It counts as placed in service this year [2]. A robot that lands in December and is running in February belongs to February. A group that books a December delivery as a December write-off is counting on something the rules do not give.

There is a second clock, and it only matters where the first-year allowance does not apply. If more than 40 percent of a year's asset value enters service in the last three months, the whole year moves to a rule that assumes each asset arrived mid-quarter [1]. That trims the first-year number on the robots too. It is also the rule that catches a used fleet, because the allowance needs original use to begin with the buyer [1].

What is left for those machines is the election to expense. It is capped at $2,500,000, starts to shrink above $4,000,000 of cost, and cannot exceed the taxable income the business earns that year [3].

## By the numbers

- **100 percent — The first-year allowance:** property written off over 20 years or less, whose original use begins with the buyer, can go in full in the year it is ready for use [1][2].
- **$2,500,000 — The expensing ceiling:** the election to expense is capped there, starts to shrink above $4,000,000 of cost, and is limited to the business's own taxable income [3].
- **40 percent — The fourth-quarter switch:** when more than 40 percent of a year's asset value enters service in the last three months, the mid-quarter rule applies to all of that year's personal property [1].

## The catch

No article can classify your building from a desk, and I would not trust one that tried. The code gives a test: is the spend for an enlargement, an elevator or escalator, or the internal frame [1]? A deck can fall on either side, depending on what it is bolted to and whether it adds floor area. Whoever reads the drawings against the statute decides. What this piece fixes is the size of the question, so that it gets asked before the invoice rather than after the return.

The allowance also carries a date. Public Law 119-21 restored it at 100 percent for property acquired and put in service after January 19, 2025, and the expense ceiling is indexed for later years [2][3]. Check the current version in two years, not this number. All of it is federal. A state that departs from the federal allowance can change the answer without touching the code.

One more limit is worth carrying. A write-off cuts taxable income, so its value depends on income that exists. A loss year defers the gain rather than creating it, and the election to expense is limited to what the business earns [3]. The cash a write-off returns is real, but it is a share of a cost already paid. Whether these machines should be owned at all is a separate argument, and [the own-versus-rent arithmetic](/owned-shuttles-or-rented-robots/) is where that one starts.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** an automation budget is being read as two assets, not one purchase — machines on a one-year schedule, building work on a 39-year one.
- **The number:** about $682,000 of first-year write-off on a $700,000 building line.
- **The risk:** what the spend is for decides the class, and that is read off the drawings.
