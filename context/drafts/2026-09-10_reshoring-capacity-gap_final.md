<!-- lead -->
US factory owners tripled their spending on new plants over the last four years. That cash bought them just a 1.5% bump in production space [1]. At the same time, imports hit a four-year high despite heavy border taxes [1]. The story about bringing production home has a huge hole. The money moved, but the factories mostly stayed put. A fresh survey shows the rising anger behind these numbers. The Reshoring Initiative asked 249 goods makers about their plans in 2026. They found 36% of Original Equipment Manufacturers (OEMs—the companies that assemble final products) had moved production to the US or were trying to. That share is up from 29% a year earlier [3]. Yet only 65% of those companies felt happy with the results. That rate crashed from 96% in 2025. Over the same year, the share of fully unhappy brands jumped from 4% to 25% [2].

<!-- tension -->
The plan to bring production home relied on three steps. First, border taxes and global threats push companies to move. Second, the money follows. Third, factory output and jobs appear. The first two steps happened on time. The third step stalled. The same surveys show exactly why. Finding workers and paying overhead rank as the top challenge for 35% of factory owners. Missing local parts tie for the top spot at 35% [3]. Money and policy no longer block the path. The real hurdles are people and building things. Policy fears have also shifted. A full 57% of factory owners say their main problem is doubt over changing rules, rather than the actual tax rate [4]. Companies can plan around a known cost. They cannot plan around a moving target. This reality favors operations leaders who look at the Total Cost of Ownership (TCO), the full lifetime price of a part. Only 40% of product makers use this total-cost approach today [4]. The current market punishes anyone still planning factory moves based on the final delivery price alone. Those companies spend heavily on new buildings. Then they find missing suppliers, high wages, and inflation long after the checks clear [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
Stop pricing factory moves based on the first invoice. The data shows a ten-point swing toward tracking the full lifetime cost in a single year [4]. Build your sourcing case on the complete price tag. Include tax risks, global threats, delivery speed, and the steep price of local vendor shortages. Do not rely on the final delivery cost. That metric hides the exact risks you are trying to avoid. Treat your hiring pipeline as a major physical project. People are now your main limit. Companies already lean on trade schools, local colleges, and internal training to fill hard roles [4]. Operations leaders must make those pipelines official right away. Lock in training deals for the next three to five years instead of scrambling to hire right before a plant opens. Plan around a range of policy outcomes rather than a specific tax rate. Build backup choices into your sourcing. Recheck the business case every three months. A full 57% of your peers already confirm that sudden rule changes break plans much faster than high taxes do [4].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
Moving production is not failing entirely. Daily results still show the positive impacts beating the negatives two to one. At the same time, most product types rely less on imports today [2][1]. Two massive groups drag down the grim numbers of 1.5% space growth and record imports. Computers and electronics, along with clothing and accessories, make up 44% of all imports from low-cost Asian countries [6]. They are also the hardest supply chains to move. Patrick Van den Bossche, the lead author at Kearney, notes these two groups "skew the picture from an aggregate perspective" [6]. The honest reality is much narrower than the scary numbers. Moving production works for the middle of the catalog. In this space, speed and physical closeness justify the expense. The strategy stalls exactly where the biggest import dollars live. In those high-volume groups, a tiny edge in final delivery cost still decides who wins the order [1][6]. Operations leaders must avoid promising the board a complete return home. But they should not abandon the strategy either. Grade every individual Stock Keeping Unit (SKU), a specific product item against its actual full cost and local labor reality. Only move production for the items where the math works.

<!-- tldr -->
- US factory spending tripled over four years, but production space grew only 1.5% while imports hit a four-year high [1].
- Happiness with moving production crashed from 96% to 65%, driven by worker shortages (35%) and shifting rules (57%) rather than a lack of money [2][4].
- Operations leaders must calculate complete lifetime costs, treat hiring as a physical project, and move production item-by-item [4][6].
