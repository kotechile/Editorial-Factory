<!-- lead -->
US manufacturers roughly tripled their investment in new plants over the past four years — and added about 1.5% to manufacturing capacity for it [1]. In the same window, imports hit a four-year high despite all that spending and a thicket of tariffs [1]. The reshoring story everyone has been telling has a gap in the middle: the money moved, the factories didn't. A second fresh dataset puts a face on the frustration. The Reshoring Initiative's 2026 survey of 249 manufacturers found 36% of original equipment manufacturers (OEMs) had reshored or were actively engaged in it, up from 29% a year earlier [3] — yet only 65% of them were satisfied with the results, down from 96% in 2025, with outright dissatisfaction jumping from 4% to 25% [2].

<!-- tension -->
The reshoring thesis was always a three-step story: tariffs and geopolitical risk push production home, capital follows, capacity and jobs follow. The first two steps happened on schedule. The third stalled. The reason is visible in the same surveys: 35% of manufacturers name labor availability and overhead costs as their top challenge, and domestic component gaps tie it at 35% [3]. The binding constraint is no longer money or policy — it's people and physical execution. Just as telling is what manufacturers are actually worried about on policy: 57% say their primary challenge is *uncertainty* — tariffs changing with little notice — not the tariff rate itself [4]. A known cost can be planned around; a moving target cannot. Who this helps: ops leaders who re-cost sourcing on Total Cost of Ownership (TCO), a method only 40% of OEMs use today, up from 30% last year [4]. Who it hurts: everyone still underwriting reshoring on free-on-board or landed-cost math alone, who commit capex and then discover vendor gaps, labor costs, and inflation after the checks clear [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
Stop pricing reshoring on the invoice. The single clearest shift in the data is a ten-point swing toward TCO in one year [4]. Build your sourcing business case on the complete cost — tariff exposure, geopolitical risk, delivery speed, and the cost of a vendor gap — not on landed cost, which hides the risk you are actually trying to hedge. Then treat the labor pipeline as a capital project, because it is now the constraint. Manufacturers already lean on trade schools, community colleges, and internal upskilling ahead of four-year degrees to fill the skilled-trades roles that are hardest to hire [4]. If you are an ops leader, formalize that pipeline now: lock apprenticeship and community-college partnerships to a 3–5 year horizon rather than hiring reactively against the next plant opening. And plan around a policy *range*, not a rate — build sourcing optionality and re-underwrite the business case quarterly, because 57% of your peers are already telling you the moving target, not the tariff number, is what breaks plans [4].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
Reshoring is not failing. The operational-impact data still shows positives outweighing negatives two to one, and most product categories are starting to lean less on imports [2][1]. The grim headline — 1.5% capacity growth, imports at a four-year high — is dragged down by two enormous categories, computer and electronics and apparel and accessories, which together are 44% of all Asian low-cost-country imports and are also the hardest to reshore [6]. Kearney's lead author says these two categories "skew the picture from an aggregate perspective" [6]. So the honest read is narrower than the scary headline: reshoring works for the middle of the catalog, where speed and proximity are worth real money, and it stalls exactly where the biggest import dollars live — the categories where a one-percent landed-cost edge still decides the order [1][6]. That is the real takeaway for an ops leader: don't promise the board a full reshore, and don't write off the strategy either. Grade each SKU against its actual TCO and its labor reality, and only reshore the ones where the math survives contact with both.

<!-- tldr -->
- US manufacturing investment roughly tripled in four years, but capacity grew only ~1.5% and imports hit a four-year high — the money moved, the factories didn't [1].
- OEM satisfaction with reshoring collapsed from 96% to 65%, and the new binding constraint is labor (35%) and policy uncertainty (57%), not capital or the tariff rate [2][4].
- Re-cost on Total Cost of Ownership (only 40% of OEMs do), lock the skilled-trades pipeline as a capital project, and reshore SKU-by-SKU where the math survives — not the whole catalog [4][6].
