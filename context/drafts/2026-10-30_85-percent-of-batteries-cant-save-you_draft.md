<!-- lead -->
On the morning of 14 October, Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E) added 25,000 homes to a wildfire shutoff list and took 7,300 off it. By that afternoon, 88,000 customers across 29 counties were in line for an outage set to run four days after forecasts of dry offshore wind, and nearly 5,500 of them depend on power for medical equipment [1]. The same state bills 29.75 cents a kilowatt-hour for household power, third-highest in the country [2]. That is the gap in its backup plan: about 85 percent of California's grid batteries sit on the utility's side of the meter, where they can't save your house [3].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

A wildfire shutoff is decided per line, not per house. Southern California Edison (SCE) tells customers that someone can stand in calm air and still lose power, because the line feeding the block "crosses a windy, high fire risk area before reaching their address" [4]. So "one block or neighborhood may have power while others don't" [4]. The utility then opens that line and leaves it open until crews walk it and check the poles.

What strikes me here is how little of the state's battery boom reaches that moment. The California Energy Commission (CEC) counts 21,112 megawatts of battery storage, and most of it is industrial plant [3]. Nearly 16,000 megawatts come from 310 big sites in the state, and another 2,000 are piped in from Nevada and Arizona [3]. Those systems shuffle power around the wide grid. They do not switch back on a line the utility has just opened on purpose.

The rest is small, and it is the only part that keeps a fridge cold. The commission counts about 3,000 megawatts spread over more than 300,000 systems at homes, schools, farms and shops, and says those "can provide backup power during outages" [3]. Do the division and roughly 14 percent of the fleet sits on your side of the meter, where a shutoff actually lands.

The price side explains why the split holds. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) puts the state's August household rate at 29.75 cents, against a U.S. average of 18.16, which is 64 percent higher [2]. Only Hawaii and Maine are dearer [2].

A grid battery earns capacity payments and a set return through the rate base. A battery on a garage wall earns only what its owner avoids paying, under a tariff the state's own regulator calls "usually lower than the retail rate" [5].

## By the numbers

- **88,000 — Homes put on notice:** PG&E's 14 October list covered 29 counties, about 5,500 Medical Baseline homes and six Tribal governments, with more than 40 help centres open across 23 counties [1].
- **10,410 — Cut three days earlier:** The utility actually cut 10,410 customers inside a 12,200-customer, 17-county zone, then warned that restored homes "could experience another outage" [6].
- **29.75 cents — The household rate:** The August figure is third-highest in the country and 64 percent above the 18.16-cent U.S. average, which the agency marks as an early estimate [2].
- **21,112 megawatts — The battery fleet:** Nearly 16,000 megawatts sit in 310 big in-state plants and 2,000 more come from out of state, against about 3,000 megawatts in 300,000 small systems [3].

## What I'd watch:

The gap between a notice and an outage is where the money hides. A list is not a cut, and only the cut empties a freezer, so I want the post-event reports that say how many of the 88,000 lost power and for how long.

- **The household share:** 3,000 of 21,112 megawatts is the number that decides whether a shutoff is survivable on a normal block.
- **The missing credit:** the federal clean energy credit stopped applying to spending after 31 December 2025, and the install date controls it, so a 2026 job gets nothing even if it was paid for earlier [7].
- **The export rate:** the regulator says sales back to the grid usually fetch less than retail, so a battery earns its keep only by running the house [5].
- **Medical Baseline homes:** about 5,500 were inside one week of notices, and those are the homes where four dark days is a health event [1].

The other half of the problem is arithmetic. [A bank sized against a year of bills instead of critical loads runs out first](/backup-battery-sizing-critical-loads/), which is why two houses on one line get different results from the same kit.

## The catch

The honest counterweight is that the state's price eased even as its reliability exposure did not. August's 29.75 cents is 3.8 percent below the 30.92 cents of a year earlier, while the U.S. average rose 3.4 percent [2]. My reading is that the cost story here is thinner than the reliability story, and I could be wrong about how much of that dip lasts the winter.

Shutoffs also work. A line that stays live through a wind event is a line that can start a fire, and the utility taking the heat is the one that dodged the spark. What the record does not show is a household plan that treats the small battery as basic kit rather than an optional extra.

The three levers are separate and should stay that way. A retail rate, an export credit and an expired tax break are read side by side, never added up, and none of them offsets another.

## At a glance

**The shift:** The state built 21,112 megawatts of batteries, but about 85 percent sits on the grid's side of the meter, where it cannot cover a shutoff.
**The number:** 88,000 customers were in line for a four-day outage in October.
**The risk:** The household share is the part that lost its federal credit in 2026, so the only fix that works now costs full price.
