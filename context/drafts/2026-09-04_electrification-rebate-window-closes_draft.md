August 31, 2026, at 5:30 p.m. Central time was the last moment a homeowner in Wisconsin could file a rebate for replacing a fossil-fuel appliance with an electric one [2]. The next morning, September 1, the federal High-Efficiency Electric Home Rebate program stopped paying for gas-to-electric swaps across every state that runs it [1][3]. If you had been planning to replace a gas furnace, water heater, or range with electric equipment and cover part of the bill with a rebate, that window is now shut — and it was not widely announced.

<!-- tension -->
This is the second quiet cut in less than a year. The 25C Energy Efficient Home Improvement tax credit — worth up to $2,000 toward a heat pump or a heat pump water heater, capped at $3,200 a year — expired on December 31, 2025 [4]. Then, on May 29, 2026, the Department of Energy issued Program Notice 26-2, which stripped the HEAR appliance rebate of the thing it was built to do: pay households to remove fossil-fuel equipment [1].

The new rule is blunt. Rebates now cover upgrades "only from existing electric equipment to more efficient electric equipment," plus new construction [1]. A gas furnace that dies and gets replaced by a heat pump no longer qualifies. A gas water heater swapped for a heat pump water heater no longer qualifies. A gas range pulled for an induction cooktop no longer qualifies. The program even changed its name — from "Home Electrification and Appliance Rebates" to "High-Efficiency Electric Home Rebates" — which tells you the direction the money is moving: away from conversion, toward efficiency of the homes that already made the switch [1].

Look at the dates stacked together and the story sharpens. The tax credit died at the end of 2025. The conversion rebate died at the start of September 2026. What's left is a thinner, more conditional set of incentives — and a homeowner population that, by and large, still believes the old deal is on the table. The people this hurts are the ones who planned their retrofit around rebate math: the middle-income household with a working-but-aging gas furnace, waiting for it to fail before going electric. The people it helps are the already-electrified homeowner doing efficiency upgrades, and the insulation and air-sealing trade, because the new rules put the envelope first [1][5].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
The money is not all gone. It has moved, and it comes with conditions. Here is what is actually left and how to work it.

First, the whole-home path is still open. The HOMES program — Home Owner Managing Energy Savings — is a separate, performance-based rebate where the payout scales with the energy you can prove or model that you save [5]. It was not hit by the fuel-switching removal. If your real project is a deep retrofit — air sealing, insulation, and a heat pump sized to a tighter envelope — the whole-house rebate is the route that still pays, and it rewards exactly the work that makes the heat pump cheap to run [5].

Second, electric-to-electric upgrades still qualify under HEAR. Replacing an old electric resistance furnace or an aging electric water heater with a heat pump or a heat pump water heater is still rebate-eligible, with up to $8,000 for an ENERGY STAR heat pump for space heating and up to $1,750 for a heat pump water heater, within a $14,000 per-project cap [2][6]. Below 80% of area median income, the rebate covers 100% of project cost; between 80% and 150%, it covers 50% [6].

Third, the envelope now comes first by rule. Notice 26-2 requires homes to use rebates for insulation and air sealing before heating-and-cooling upgrades — unless the home is already appropriately sealed [1]. So before you buy a heat pump, check your attic insulation and air sealing. If they are not done, the program wants that work first, and it is the work that actually cuts the bill.

Fourth, geothermal still has a federal lifeline. The 25D Residential Clean Energy Credit is untouched, covering 30% of a ground-source heat pump's cost through 2032 [4]. It is a bigger check than 25C ever was — but it is a ground loop, not an air-source swap, so it is a different job and a different budget.

The practical move this month: stop pricing your project on the old incentive sheet. Pull your state's current program page, check whether you are electric-to-electric or whole-home, and run the payback on the real rebate that survives — not the one you remember from 2024.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
The honest caveat: this does not make electrification a bad investment, and it is not a ban. The change is about who the federal money pays, not whether a heat pump saves you money on the gas bill. A heat pump still beats a gas furnace on running cost in most climates, and the equipment got better and cheaper on its own. What died is the subsidy for the *switch itself*. If your gas furnace is genuinely done and you were counting on a rebate to soften a like-for-like swap, that specific subsidy is gone — and you may be better served either waiting on the whole-home retrofit route, or checking state and utility incentives, which several states still run independent of the federal program [2]. The rebate landscape changed by a stroke of a pen in May and a calendar date in September; the payback math changed with it.

<!-- tldr -->
- As of Sept 1, 2026, the federal HEAR rebate no longer pays for gas→electric swaps — the 25C tax credit for that job died Dec 31, 2025, too.
- What survives: the HOMES whole-home rebate (modeled savings), electric→electric HEAR upgrades (up to $8,000 heat pump / $1,750 HPWH), and the 30% geothermal 25D credit.
- Before buying a heat pump, verify your state's current program and your insulation/air-sealing — the new rules put the envelope first.
