<!-- lead -->
A misclassified entry costs up to 4x the duty you underpaid, and your exposure does not stop at the duty. Federal law lets Customs and Border Protection (CBP) add a penalty of two times the unpaid duty for a careless error, four times for a reckless one, and the full value of the goods when the error was deliberate [1]. The one discount written into the statute is a prior disclosure [1]. It is lost by missing a date, not by getting the code wrong.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The duty is not in dispute in any of this. Under the same section of law, CBP must recover the duties, taxes and fees the government was deprived of whether or not it also assesses a penalty [1]. So the only live question is the multiplier stacked on top.

That multiplier is set by how the error is described, not by how much money is involved. A clerical slip that was not part of a pattern is not a violation at all [1].

A negligent mistake reaches two times the duty. A grossly negligent one reaches four times, and fraud reaches the value of the merchandise itself [1].

A prior disclosure cuts all three down, but only inside a narrow window. If the importer discloses before, or without knowledge of, a formal investigation, the goods are not seized and the penalty is capped [1].

For negligence and gross negligence the cap is interest on the unpaid duty, running from the date of liquidation. For fraud it is 100 percent of the duty, or 10 percent of the dutiable value where no duty was affected [1]. Every version of that cap depends on tendering the money.

The awkward part is the clock. CBP dates a formal investigation from its own written record. That record is the day CBP first had facts suggesting a violation [1].

The importer has to prove it did not know. That puts the burden of proof on the company that just found its own error [1].

My read: this is a filing problem wearing a classification costume. The importers who get hurt are rarely the ones with the most wrong codes. They are the ones who found out in March and were still assembling paperwork in June.

The scale is small. CBP's own paperwork estimate for the prior-disclosure collection covers 762 respondents and 762 responses a year, at an estimated three hours each [4]. Most of the entries that are wrong never take this path.

## By the numbers

- **4x — Reckless-error ceiling:** A grossly negligent violation carries a penalty of up to the lesser of the merchandise's domestic value or four times the unpaid duty, taxes and fees; a negligent one is the same test at two times [1].
- **100 percent — Fraud disclosure cap:** Disclose a fraudulent violation before a formal investigation and tender the duty, and the penalty is capped at 100 percent of the duty rather than the value of the goods [1].
- **10 days — Oral disclosure deadline:** An oral disclosure has to be confirmed in writing to the local fines and penalties officer within 10 days, and the officer can deny it if that record never arrives [2].
- **762 — Disclosures a year:** CBP's information-collection notice estimates 762 respondents and 762 total annual responses for prior disclosures, at three hours per response and 2,286 total burden hours [4].

## What I'd watch:

What I am watching next is the paperwork trail a company builds in the first two weeks after it learns an entry was filed wrong, because that trail is the whole case.

- **The written record:** An oral call to a CBP officer can start the process, but it only counts if a written record of what was said reaches the fines and penalties officer inside 10 days [2]. A conversation with no follow-up letter is close to nothing.
- **The four parts:** The disclosure has to name the merchandise, the entries by number or by port and rough dates, the false statement and how it happened, and the correct data — with anything still unknown promised inside 30 days [2]. I would want to know who in a company can produce those four things on a deadline.
- **The tender:** A disclosure is denied outright if the company never pays the loss CBP finally calculates [2]. Teams that file and then argue about the number without depositing it lose the benefit they filed for.
- **The five-year file:** Entry records have to be kept five years from the date of entry [3]. That is also the window an audit can reach back into, so an audit and a defense reach the same boxes.
- **The escalation price:** Above $100,000, and with more than a year left before the deadline for CBP to act runs out, a company can ask headquarters to review CBP's number — and gives up its right to contest that number afterwards [2].

## The catch

The cap is not zero. For negligence and gross negligence the reduced penalty is interest on the unpaid duty from the date of liquidation [1], so a company that discloses late still pays for the delay.

The disclosure is also not self-certifying. CBP decides whether it was prior, and the company carries the burden of proving it did not know an investigation had started [1]. That is a fact question about internal emails, not a form.

Some errors are not violations at all. Clerical mistakes and mistakes of fact fall outside the section unless they are part of a pattern [1], which means the panic response can be worse than the error.

The part I keep circling: the penalty is a multiple of the duty, so the cheapest disclosure is the one filed on a small error early. The same steps taken after a broker audit covers three years of entries cost a great deal more to execute.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A wrong tariff code exposes an importer to a penalty of two to four times the unpaid duty, and to the value of the goods where the error was deliberate, under 19 U.S.C. § 1592 [1].
- **Why It Matters:** The duty is owed either way [1]. A prior disclosure is the only mechanism that turns the multiplier into interest, and it works on dates rather than on argument [1][2].
- **What I'd Watch:** How fast a company converts the day it learns of an error into a written, dated filing.
  - **The 10-day record:** The written confirmation that keeps an oral disclosure alive [2].
  - **The four parts:** Merchandise, entries, the false statement, and the correct data — the checklist CBP reads [2].
  - **The tender:** The payment that decides whether the disclosure stands at all [2].
- **The Catch:** Disclosing does not erase the duty or the interest, the company carries the burden of proving it acted before an investigation began, and clerical errors are outside the section entirely [1].
