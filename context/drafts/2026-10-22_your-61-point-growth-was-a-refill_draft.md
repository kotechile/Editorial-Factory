<!-- lead -->
Constellation Brands shipped 123.9 million cases of beer in the quarter to 31 August. That is 5.5% more than a year earlier. The volume its distributors sold on to stores fell 0.6%. The 6.1-point gap between those two lines is not demand growth. It is a refill, and the company says so: distributors were rebuilding stock. A survey of 150 small and midsize businesses (SMBs), out two weeks earlier, found the same error at the other end of the chain.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

An order from a distributor has two parts. One is what the distributor's own buyers pull. The other is the change in what the distributor wants to hold. A plan that reads orders counts both as demand. It counts the second part twice: once in the supplier's shipments, and again in the buyer's forecast.

I keep coming back to one fact. Constellation prints both numbers. Shipments are what it sends to distributors. Depletions are what those distributors ship on to stores, measured with third-party data. So the gap is public each quarter, for anyone who wants to check a plan against it.

The gap is wide this year because of last year. Constellation and its distributors cut stock in fiscal 2026 as premium beer sales slowed. Chief executive Nicholas Fink told analysts on 7 October that the company went into fiscal 2027 "too light". It raised shipments to clear order backlogs and to keep shelves full. Distributor stock is now in a "great position", he said, though still below its usual days on hand.

Then comes the line a planner should keep. For the full year, Fink said, the company expects "ships and depletes to track within 99% of each other". That is a claim about the quarters still to come. My reading is this: if the year ends near parity after a quarter in which shipments led stores by 6.1 points, the rest of the year has to run the other way. The refill is not a free gift to the top line.

The second document shows the buyer's side of the same trade. Netstock sells stock planning software. It surveyed more than 150 SMB users and drew on platform data from 2,500 customers. It found that 53% of these firms are ordering earlier or in larger lots this year. Another 39% lean more on forecasting and planning tools.

The early-order group is not doing better. Among firms ordering earlier, only 44% report service above 90%. That compares with 62% of everyone else. The report's own words are careful: "early buying often reflects existing availability pressure more than it reflects a proven strategy". The group that moved its orders up was already the group under strain.

A third number ties the two documents together. Typical stock value sat at $3.91 million in the second quarter. It has held in a $3.8 million to $4.0 million band for three years. The report says early ordering has not raised what a typical firm holds. Moving an order up changes when the same goods arrive. It does not change how much the firm holds. It adds no news the forecast did not have.

## By the numbers

- **6.1 points — Shipments beat sell-through:** beer shipments rose 5.5% to 123.9 million case equivalents while depletions fell 0.6%. The spread is the desk's sum of two stated figures, not a number the company prints [1].
- **99% — Full-year parity guide:** the firm expects shipments and depletions to land within 99% of each other for fiscal 2027, after a quarter in which they split [3].
- **44% against 62% — Service above 90%:** SMBs that moved orders up report worse service than the rest, a gap the report ties to strain rather than to the tactic [2].
- **$3.91 million — Typical stock value:** flat in a $3.8 million to $4.0 million band for three years, which is the proof that early orders re-time goods instead of adding to them [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

- **The next gap:** Constellation reports each quarter, so the December release carries the first test of the parity guide. A shipment line that trails depletions would be the give-back the sums imply.
- **The same survey question:** a second edition, put to the same group, would show if the 44% is a fixed cost of hedging or a bad year.
- **Dead stock, not turns:** 24% of SMBs report more than 10% of stock as dead, up from 12% in 2024, while turns hit a three-year high. Faster movement and more waste can rise together.
- **Who can see both lines:** brewers and distributors report shipments and depletions as two metrics. A direct seller has one line and must rebuild the second from store data, which is where the check tends to stop.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

My read is that the refill was the right call. A channel that cut too deep and left shelves bare needs stock, and Constellation flagged the swing a year out. Its September 2025 outlook told investors to expect shipments to trail depletions by 6.0 to 7.0 points in the second quarter of fiscal 2026, then to align for the second half. This year's 6.1-point lead is the mirror of that guide. For anyone who reads the company closely, the move was disclosed, not found.

Two limits sit on the other leg. Netstock sells the software whose case its report makes, and 150 people answering one set of questions cannot prove that early orders cause poor service. The report itself says the link may run the other way, from tight supply to early orders. What survives that caveat is small and still of use: the hedge did not show up as cover.

A refill is also not free. Constellation's beer margin fell 160 basis points to 39.0% even as shipment volume rose 5.5%, because marketing and other selling costs more than offset lower tariff costs. Volume that comes from a channel righting itself carries the same cost base as volume that comes from a shopper.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Constellation shipped 5.5% more beer than a year earlier while its distributors sold 0.6% less on to stores, a 6.1-point gap the firm blames on distributors rebuilding stock. In the same month, a survey of small and midsize firms found that those ordering earlier to shield themselves report worse service, not better.
- **Why It Matters:** A distributor's order is shopper pull plus the change in its own stock. A plan that reads orders as demand counts a buffer twice, and books a one-quarter refill as a growth trend — the trend the parity guide says has to be given back.
- **What I'd Watch:** the next shipments-against-depletions gap, the survey's next read on its early-order group, and the dead-stock share that rose to 24% from 12% in 2024.
  - **Ships against depletes:** two volume lines a supplier reports — one to distributors, one from them to stores — with the gap between them equal to the channel's stock change.
  - **Parity guide:** a full-year target that puts the two lines within 99% of each other, which tells a planner the split is meant to close.
  - **Early-order group:** the share of buyers who moved orders up, tracked against their own service rate rather than against the firms that did not.
- **The Catch:** The refill was the right call after a real shortage, and Constellation flagged the swing a year early. The survey is a vendor's group comparison with 150 replies and cannot fix cause, and the volume came with a 160-basis-point fall in margin.
