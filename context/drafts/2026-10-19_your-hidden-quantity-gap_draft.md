<!-- lead -->
The share of purchase orders that arrived on time and in full (OTIF) fell to 63.6% in the third quarter, from 66.1% in the three months before, according to SourceDay's Fall 2026 Supplier Reliability Index [1]. The split inside that number is the part worth staring at. On-time delivery slipped only 1.9 points, to 66.5% [1]. The shortfall sits almost entirely in the quantity. The hidden gap is the quantity, the half of the score a buyer's buffer protects. Purchasing managers reported the same month that the buffer is gone [2].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

A service score has two gates on every order line. One is the delivery window the buyer set. The other is the full ordered quantity, meaning the cases or units the buyer actually needs.

A line counts only when both hold, so the two gates are protected by different things. Freight protects the date. Stock protects the quantity.

For three years both gates climbed together. On-time delivery went from 55.0% in early 2023 to 68.4% in the second quarter of this year, and the combined score from 53.2% to 66.1% [1].

Then they split. The combined score lost 2.5 points while on-time lost 1.9, and the gap between them widened [1].

The Institute for Supply Management (ISM) surveys purchasing managers every month. Its September report describes the other side of that split [2]. Customers' inventories registered 41.6, in "too low" territory for 24 straight months, and respondents' own inventories fell to 48.6, back into contraction.

The prices index jumped 6.8 points to 77.9, back to where it sat when the Iran war began. Twenty-four commodities were reported up in price. None were reported down.

What strikes me is that those two reports describe the same quarter from opposite ends of a purchase order. One measures what arrived. The other measures what the buyer had on the shelf, and what the supplier had to pay to put anything there.

My read is that the in-full gate works as a shock absorber. When prices jump and the shelf is thin, the supplier cannot hold the whole price, so it holds a smaller promise. The record 10.9% of purchase orders changed by the supplier looks like that decision being made, up from 8.8% in the prior quarter [1].

Average response time stretched to 52.2 hours from 48.1, the slowest since the third quarter of 2024 [1]. More than a third of closed lines, 36.4%, changed at least once [1].

**By the numbers:**

- **63.6% — Combined OTIF rate:** the share of purchase order lines arriving on time and complete in the third quarter, down from 66.1% [1]
- **41.6 — Customers' inventories index:** purchasing managers have called their customers' stock too low for 24 straight months [2]
- **77.9 — Prices index:** up 6.8 points in September and back to its level when the Iran war started; 24 commodities rose, none fell [2]
- **10.9% — Supplier-initiated order changes:** the highest reading in the index's four-year series, up from 8.8% [1]

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The people closest to this are not reading the combined score. They are reading the parts, and the parts disagree.

- **The gap between the two gates:** in-full lost 2.5 points and on-time lost 1.9, so quantity is where the damage sits [1]. A buyer watching the headline number cannot see which half moved.
- **The change log:** a record 10.9% of orders were changed by the supplier before delivery, and 36.4% of closed lines moved at least once [1]. That log runs ahead of the miss, which makes it the cheaper place to look.
- **Response time:** at 52.2 hours on average, a buyer confirming a date has lost two working days before anyone can re-plan [1].
- **The short-supply list:** ISM's respondents named memory, dynamic random access memory and electronic components as hard to get [2]. Where the component is short, the in-full gate is the one that fails first.
- **The price of speed:** the ground parcel rate per package sat 38.8% above its January 2018 baseline in the third quarter and is projected to reach an all-time high of 43.3% in the fourth [3]. Buying velocity costs more this quarter than last, and it does not add a single unit to a shelf.

The freight market has its own version of the same story. Diesel averaged $6.199 a gallon in the week to 5 October, $2.488 above a year earlier [4].

The fuel line is visible, and it gets managed. The quantity line is the one that quietly changes.

## The catch

SourceDay publishes this index from its own network's purchase order data and does not disclose how many suppliers or orders sit behind the number [5]. It is a vendor measuring the problem its product addresses. The weighting is a choice too: on-time delivery carries 40%, supplier responsiveness 30% and order stability 30% [5].

The ISM survey has the opposite limitation. It is a diffusion index, built from what purchasing managers report rather than from a count of shipments. A reading above 50 means more respondents saw an increase than a decrease.

Sentiment can move before a single pallet does.

One quarter is not a trend, and the SourceDay report says so itself. A mix shift would produce a similar reading. If buyers moved volume toward long-tail items or unproven suppliers in the quarter, in-full would fall ahead of on-time with no change in anyone's behaviour.

I could be wrong about the cause. What the data supports is narrower and still useful: quantity broke before timing, the change log broke before both, and the buffer behind the quantity has been thin for two years.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** the share of purchase order lines arriving on time and complete fell to 63.6% in the third quarter from 66.1%, and the loss landed on the quantity delivered rather than the delivery date [1].
- **Why It Matters:** the two halves of the score are protected by different things — freight guards the date, stock guards the quantity — and purchasing managers say the stock is gone, with customers' inventories too low for 24 straight months while prices returned to their war-start level [2]. An in-full miss now lands on a buyer with nothing behind it.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The two-gate gap:** whether in-full keeps falling faster than on-time, which keeps the problem in the goods rather than on the road [1].
  - **The change log:** supplier-initiated changes at a record 10.9%, running ahead of the delivery miss [1].
  - **The short-supply list:** which components ISM's respondents name, since a shortage there turns into a quantity miss first [2].
- **The Catch:** the index comes from one vendor's network with no disclosed sample size, covers a single quarter, and a change in product mix would produce a similar reading [5]. The ISM survey measures reported sentiment, not shipments.
