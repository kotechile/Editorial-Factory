<!-- lead -->
The thermal camera that a federal building-science guide says you can buy for as little as $250 does not see through a wall; it reads the heat of the wall's own surface, which is why your imager needs a house that is already misbehaving [2]. The spec that decides whether the picture means anything sits in no product listing. An infrared scan only earns its keep when the spread from inside to outside reaches 15 degrees Fahrenheit or more, and the heat has to be off for at least 15 minutes before the first frame so the surfaces can settle [1]. Below that gap, a dark patch on a ceiling is as likely to be a stud bay, a warm duct or a draft from an attic hatch as it is missing insulation, and the camera will not tell you which of those it is.

## The big picture:

My read starts with what the imager actually sells: an energy audit, when what it really makes is a pointer — a place to go and look, with no number attached to it [1]. The people who use one for a living treat it that way: the image finds a suspect patch of wall, then somebody opens the ceiling or pushes a probe into the wood to learn what is there.

That second step is where most home audits stop, because the picture looks like an answer. Three faults cool a surface the same way: fiberglass missing from a bay, cold air pushing through a crack, and water sitting in drywall. All three read as the same cool patch on the screen [1].

Air leaks are the hardest of the three to image, because they only show while you move air. The trade method is a blower door, which holds the whole house at a set pressure.

The federal guide's home version is to close the windows and the outside doors, turn the heat off at the thermostat, then run every exhaust fan and the clothes dryer with the inside doors open [2]. Under that pull, an air leak shows as a streak at a top plate or a sill, while missing insulation just sits there as a soft, even patch.

So the camera and the blower door are not rival buys. One says where, the other says how much, in a number you can write into a work scope. That split matters because 52% of a household's yearly energy use goes to heating and cooling alone, so the shell of the house is where the money sits [6].

## By the numbers

- **1,500 cubic feet per minute — a sealed house:** a blower door holds 50 pascals, about the push of a 20 mph wind, and a tight shell usually reads below this flow [3].
- **4,000 cubic feet per minute — a leaky one:** the same test above this rate is the association's working line for a leaky house, and the test itself runs about half an hour [3].
- **11% — modelled energy saving:** the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) model of air sealing plus attic and rim-joist insulation puts the saving at 11% of total energy costs, or 15% on heating and cooling alone [5].
- **$205 a year — power-bill floor:** the average household bought 10,791 kilowatt-hours in 2022 at a 2025 average of 17.30 cents, which is $1,866.84 a year, and 11% of that comes to about $205 before any gas is counted [5][7][8].

## What I'd watch:

The inspectors I have read on this do not scan on a whim. They pick the hour when the house sits furthest from the weather, and they treat the camera as the first of two steps rather than the whole job [1][2].

- **The hour:** winter early mornings and evenings, or summer mid-to-late afternoons, because that is when outside and inside are furthest apart [2].
- **The pressure step:** windows shut, thermostat off, exhaust fans and dryer running, inside doors open — which is the difference between a streak that means air and a patch that means nothing [2].
- **The confirmation:** the suspect spot gets opened or probed before anyone quotes a repair, since the image only says the surface runs cool [1].
- **The number:** when the question is whether the house leaks rather than where, a blower door answers it in about half an hour of test time [3].

## The catch

What I keep coming back to is how narrow the working window is. The camera needs a real gap in heat to see anything, which in most of the country means a handful of cold mornings a year and a homeowner willing to sit in a cooling house for a quarter of an hour first [1].

The savings have their own gap. The consumer page promises "up to a 10% savings" on the yearly energy bill, while the model behind it puts the national average a point above that, with a wide spread [4][5]. The same table runs from 5% of total costs in climate zone 1 up to 16% in zone 8, and it assumes a 25% cut in air leaks that a weekend of caulk may not deliver [5].

I would not read any of this as a reason to skip the camera. The tool is cheap, the heat it reads is real, and the habit it builds — look first, open second — is the one the trade uses.

What I would resist is treating the picture as the verdict, because the costly part usually starts past the wall: a cold patch in a ceiling still has not told you whether the loss is the fiberglass or the duct running through it. That arithmetic sits in [20% of Your AC Never Arrives](/20-percent-of-your-ac-never-arrives/), where the same model starts from a house with 23% duct leakage [5].

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** cheap thermal imagers put a tool that used to belong to auditors into a homeowner's hands, and the guidance that comes with it says the useful picture depends on weather and pressure, not on the sensor.
- **Why It Matters:** a scan that reads air, insulation and water as one dark patch sends a homeowner into a repair without a diagnosis, and the savings it chases are modelled at 11% of total energy costs nationally, with a 5% to 16% spread by climate zone [5].
- **What I'd Watch:** the hour and the pressure, because both have to be right before the picture carries any information.
  - **The spread:** the camera needs a wide gap between inside and outside heat, which is why winter mornings and summer afternoons are the working seasons [2].
  - **The depressurization:** exhaust fans and a dryer pull outside air through the cracks so a leak shows as a streak instead of a guess [2].
  - **The blower-door number:** the test turns the whole house into one figure, under 1,500 for a tight shell and above 4,000 for a leaky one [3].
- **The Catch:** the 10% headline and the modelled average are two statements from one program, the zone spread is wide, and the price band is a government guide's 2025 figure rather than a street price.
