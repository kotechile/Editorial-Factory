<!-- lead -->
U.S. on-highway diesel fell to $6.199 a gallon the week of Oct. 5, down 33 cents in two weeks. [1] And the first shippers running heavy electric trucks on a real freight lane are still paying for something else to make the switch pencil out. Etsy and five other companies are buying certificates that cover the added cost of running those trucks, because at six dollars a gallon the fuel saving alone does not close the gap. [2]

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The number I keep coming back to is not the diesel price. It is that a fuel bill this high still is not the lever that moves freight to electric.

Diesel spent two weeks falling, from $6.529 to $6.199, and that is real relief for anyone buying fuel in bulk. [1] It is also almost beside the point. A year ago the same fuel cost $2.488 — so the pump price is up about 150% even after the recent dip. [1] With freight spot rates running 20% to 28% above their nine-year seasonal averages, carriers are still squeezed. [2]

If high fuel prices sold electric trucks, this would be the moment. Instead, the companies funding them are not leaning on the fuel saving at all. That is the signal worth following.

## By the numbers

- **$6.199 a gallon — Diesel's two-week slide:** U.S. on-highway diesel fell from $6.529 to $6.199 by Oct. 5, yet it still sits $3.711 above a year ago. California pays $8.082. [1]
- **63 trucks — The electric pilot that needs a certificate:** A group including Etsy, Amazon, eBay, Meta and Google is funding 63 heavy battery-electric trucks on the Houston–Dallas lane, set to launch in early 2027. [3]
- **60% — Where the emissions really sit:** Etsy says shipping is its largest source of emissions, at over 60%, and it aims to cut supply-chain emissions per dollar of gross profit by 52% by 2030. [3]
- **$36,000 — The same gap, paid by a port:** The ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach propose paying up to $36,000 per truck per year to claw back the cost of zero-emission drayage by 2035. [4]

## What I'd watch:

I have been watching how the people closest to this are routing around the cost gap rather than through it — and none of them are betting on the pump.

- **The certificate, not the truck, is the product.** These Environmental Attribute Certificates (certificates a company buys to claim an emissions cut it did not physically make itself) let a shipper fund electric freight without owning it. The buyer pays the gap between running an electric truck and running a diesel one. [3]
- **The carrier owns the hard part.** Nevoya, the carrier in the pilot, has to secure the battery-electric trucks, the charging and the renewable power before anyone can buy a certificate. The accounting layer is easy; the depot, the grid connection and the duty cycle are not. [3]
- **Public money is filling the same hole.** The proposed port program pays truckers per terminal visit — up to $36,000 a year each — for exactly the reason the certificates exist: the fuel saving does not cover the extra cost. [4]
- **The infrastructure is still the bottleneck.** The pilot chose Houston–Dallas for cheap Texas power, easy permits and a round trip that fits inside one truck's battery range. [3] That is a narrow set of conditions, not a national rollout.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

The part I keep circling: a certificate is a bridge, not a proof. It says the electric truck costs more to run today, and that someone has volunteered to pay the difference.

That someone is a shipper chasing a target, and targets move. If diesel keeps falling toward last year's $2.488, the gap the certificates cover gets wider, not narrower, and the willingness to pay it gets harder to defend. [1] My read is that these certificates buy a lane of learning — real charging, real duty cycles, real maintenance data — not a cheaper way to move a parcel yet.

I could be wrong, but I read the diesel dip as exactly the wrong direction for this funding model. A cheaper pump makes the electric case harder to sell, not easier. Curious how operators are sizing that risk as they sign new year contracts now. [2]

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Diesel eased to $6.199 a gallon in early October but is still up ~150% year over year, and shippers funding electric trucks are covering the cost gap with certificates rather than relying on fuel savings.
- **Why It Matters:** If fuel price is not the lever, then the carbon-accounting layer is what decides who pays for electric freight — and that changes which teams (finance, sustainability, or logistics) own the last mile's cost.
- **What I'd Watch:** The next signals are about who funds the gap, not the pump price.
  - **The certificate market:** Whether non-electric freight shippers keep paying to fund electric lanes they do not use.
  - **Depot and charging readiness:** Whether carriers like Nevoya can stand up charging fast enough to meet launch dates.
  - **The diesel floor:** Whether further diesel relief widens the gap the certificates have to cover.
- **The Catch:** A certificate proves the electric truck costs more to run today, not less — it is a bridge funded by a shipper target, and targets move.
