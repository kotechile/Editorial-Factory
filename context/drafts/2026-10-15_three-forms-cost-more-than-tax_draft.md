<!-- lead -->
A move abroad opens three US forms, and a missed one costs $10,000. Each form carries its own charge, and not one of them is tax. The account form is the FBAR (Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts). It is due when the combined value of foreign accounts tops $10,000 at any point in the year [1]. A quiet account does not excuse it.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

A move abroad changes where a person pays tax. It also opens a second set of filings, and those filings answer to balances and ownership, not to income.

Three lines run side by side. The account form watches total account value [1]. The asset statement watches what a taxpayer living abroad holds [3]. The company form reports a foreign business the filer owns [4].

The lines ignore profit. An account that crossed $10,000 for one day sits inside the account rule. The IRS (Internal Revenue Service) says the account earning nothing has no effect on whether it must be reported [1]. The filing duty and the tax bill run on two clocks.

What strikes me is where the risk sits. A household can model two countries' brackets to the dollar. It can still trip a filing line that never asks about income.

## By the numbers

- **$10,000 — The account trigger:** The account form is due when the combined value of foreign accounts passed $10,000 at any time in the calendar year [1]. FinCEN, which receives the form, states the same line [2].
- **$200,000 — The asset statement line:** A taxpayer living abroad files Form 8938 once specified foreign assets pass $200,000 on the last day of the year, or $300,000 at any time [3]. A joint return raises those lines to $400,000 and $600,000 [3].
- **$10,000 a year — The company penalty:** A foreign corporation left unreported runs $10,000 for each annual accounting period [4]. After the IRS mails a notice and 90 days pass, the charge grows by $10,000 for each 30-day period, up to $50,000 [5][6].
- **50 percent — The willful ceiling:** A willful miss on the account form lifts the maximum to the greater of $100,000 or half the account [7]. The table applied to penalties assessed on or after January 17, 2025 puts it at $165,353 [8].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The dates and the notice clocks matter as much as the dollar lines. I have been watching how these three filings stack against each other.

- **The extension nobody requests:** The account form is due April 15, after the calendar year reported. The automatic extension runs to October 15, with no application filed [1].
- **The 90-day notice:** The added charge on a missed asset statement or company report starts only after the IRS mails a notice and 90 days pass [5][6]. It then adds $10,000 for each 30-day period, capped at $50,000 [6].
- **Two readings of one ceiling:** The statute caps a non-willful miss on the account form at $10,000 [7]. The adjustment table puts the same ceiling at $16,536 for penalties assessed on or after January 17, 2025 [8].
- **The years that keep charging:** A foreign corporation left off the forms for three years is charged for each of those years [4][6]. The entity penalty is written for each annual accounting period [6].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch:

My read is that these amounts are ceilings and yearly charges, not bills that arrive on their own. The honest limits of the rule are easy to miss.

The asset statement and the company report carry set amounts [5][6]. The account form's $10,000 is a maximum that one indexed table moves, so the two never read alike [7][8]. One filing also does not repair another [5][6].

The part I keep circling is how the biggest bills get built. Our desk keeps one case from an employer that let an engineer work from Spain for about seven months. The Spanish tax agency ruled the employer had a taxable presence there. The bill that followed carried $110,000 in penalties and mandatory contributions. That figure is our field note, not a published statistic. It says what these filings say: the number that hurts comes from a compliance event, not a tax rate.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** A move abroad opens three US filings whose triggers are dollar amounts and ownership, not tax owed. They cover foreign accounts over $10,000, foreign assets over $200,000 for a taxpayer living abroad, and a foreign company the filer owns.
- **Why It Matters:** The penalties are written per form and per year. A missed asset statement or company report runs $10,000, and the account form's ceiling is $10,000 in the statute and $16,536 in the table for penalties assessed on or after January 17, 2025.
- **What I'd Watch:** How the filing dates and the notice clocks run against those dollar lines.
  - **The automatic extension:** The account form is due April 15 and moves to October 15 with no application needed.
  - **The 90-day notice:** The added charge begins only after the IRS mails a notice and 90 days pass, then adds $10,000 for each 30-day period up to $50,000.
  - **The indexed ceiling:** The non-willful account-form maximum is $10,000 by statute and $16,536 under the table applied to penalties assessed on or after January 17, 2025.
- **The Catch:** The set amounts are ceilings and yearly charges, one filing does not stand in for another, and our own field note is that the largest bills come from compliance events rather than tax rates.
