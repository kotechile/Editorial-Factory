<!-- lead -->
On 12 October Colorado's insurance office told carriers what they now owe an owner who asks why a wildfire score moved the price, since a score can lift a bill or end a renewal: the score, the math behind it, the fixes that would change it, and a written answer to an appeal within 30 days [1]. Three days before, Hurricane Isaias came ashore at Destin, Florida. By 6 November, 14,402 of the 68,071 claims it left behind had closed with nothing paid, which is 21% of every claim filed [2]. Colorado now times the first talk. Nobody times the second, and the second turns on the same thing: what an owner can put in front of an adjuster.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The shift:** Colorado now makes a carrier hand over the score and the fixes behind a wildfire price.
- **The number:** 14,402 of 68,071 Isaias claims closed with nothing paid.
- **The risk:** A credit nobody documented is a claim you cannot prove later.

<!-- tension -->

## Colorado timed the price

The rule is HB25-1182, in force since July and signed in May of last year [3]. The notices it requires began going out on 1 October [1]. The state wrote it because a score can set a price and a renewal, and until now the owner had no way to see the math.

The clock is short and it runs both ways. A new applicant's score must arrive about two weeks after a complete application. An appeal has to be answered inside 10 days and decided in writing inside 30, and if the answer runs late the policy keeps covering the house for 10 days past its end date [1]. A state that times all of that has decided a score is a document, not a verdict.

The notice itself is where the leverage sits. It must carry the property's own score, a plain explanation of how the company worked it out, the full range of scores, the features of the house that drove that score, the effect of mitigation, and the right to appeal [1]. A carrier that uses a score to price, write or drop a policy must also post its mitigation discounts on its own site, or say there that it offers none [1]. A year ago a surcharge arrived as a number with no arithmetic attached. That is the part of this rule I would put above the 15%.

The carrier reply is the part that changes a decision. CSAA (an insurer) gives 15% off the wildfire share of a premium to a home that holds a Wildfire Prepared Home certificate at the Enhanced or Essential level, and promises wildfire cover where the home reaches Enhanced and the owner also holds a car policy with it [1]. State Farm will look at homes "even in high wildfire risk areas" when they carry the same certificate [1]. Put those two side by side and the share is the smaller half of the offer. What the certificate buys is a writer's willingness to take the house at all.

## By the numbers

- **15% off the wildfire share:** CSAA's filed discount for a home that holds the certificate [1].
- **10 days:** The carrier's limit to answer a score appeal before the state's clock runs out [1].
- **14,402 Isaias claims:** Closed with nothing paid, of 68,071 filed after the storm [2].

## Florida's gate has no clock

Florida's own storm table reads like the other half of the story [2]. Of the claims in the table, 25,211 had closed with some payment and 14,402 with none, while 20,482 were still open and unpaid; a little over half of the book had closed at all [2]. Homes account for 55,471 of the count, so this is an owner's story before it is anything else [2].

The table prints counts, not causes. The reasons come from a newsroom's read of the same file. Of the closures with no payment, 6,835 involved damage that landed under the deductible, and 5,374 closed over paperwork: a duplicate, a wrong date of loss, an owner who never called back, a claim filed as an inquiry [4]. That last group is what I find hard to square with a market that is otherwise pricing risk house by house. Those claims did not fail on an exclusion. They failed on a file.

The clock is the whole difference. Colorado gives an owner 10 days to be heard and 30 to be answered, and it lends a regulator's weight to both numbers. Florida's table offers no such timetable, and it prints no mean payment, so a closed claim tells you a file ended and nothing about what it held [2]. What I would watch is the 20,482 still open, and whether the share of them that ends with a payment looks like the 25,211 that already did. The same logic runs through the second half of a roof claim, where the withheld money waits on a file and a date: [Your Roof Claim's Hidden Second Check: 12 Months to Collect](/roof-claim-hidden-second-check-12-months/).

## The catch

None of this proves a tidy folder changes an outcome. The storm data has no field for records, so the 5,374 paperwork closures are a class, not a finding about photographs, and some of those claims had nothing to show. Colorado's promise also belongs to one carrier at one certificate level, and its 15% is written for an owner who "may be eligible", subject to that company's rules [1]. A discount is not a right, and a certificate is not a policy.

What I think holds up is narrower and more useful than either headline. A wildfire credit and a claim payment are both calls about proof, and the state has just made the first one legible, timed and open to challenge. That makes the appeal the cheapest practice run on offer: the receipts, the dated photos and the certificate that win a score review are the same papers that answer an adjuster after a storm. The work is half the purchase. The file is the other half, and it is the half the owner keeps.
