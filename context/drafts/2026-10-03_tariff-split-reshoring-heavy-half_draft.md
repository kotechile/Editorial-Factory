<!-- lead -->
On September 28, Washington recommended cutting tariffs on $60 billion of "non-sensitive" goods — toys, household items, holiday decorations [1]. The same week, US Steel broke ground on a $475 million quench-and-tempering line in Alabama [3], and IndustrialSage's tracker pushed announced US manufacturing investment past $2 trillion [4]. The relief and the reshoring are heading to opposite halves of the catalog.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The first real US–China tariff de-escalation drew a line the market has been waiting to see — and the White House's own word, "non-sensitive," is doing the work. Relief went to consumer goods: small appliances, toys, holiday decorations, children's car seats [2]. Everything strategic stayed protected. Steel keeps its Section 232 duties, semiconductors and batteries keep Section 301, and the forced-labor and excess-capacity tariff dockets keep stacking on top [1].

What strikes me is the inversion. The categories Washington walked back are the light-assembly goods that are cheapest and fastest to reshore. The categories it's defending are the ones where reshoring is a decade-long, nine-figure project. My read: capital already got the memo.

## By the numbers

- **$60B — Recommended tariff relief:** roughly $30 billion each way in non-sensitive goods, a US–China Board of Trade recommendation with no reduction amounts or start date attached [1][2].
- **$2.084T — Announced US manufacturing investment:** 251 companies across 43 states, tracked by IndustrialSage as of September 28 [4].
- **67.0% — Semiconductors' share:** $1,395.5 billion of the $2.084 trillion sits in semiconductor and advanced tech; pharma adds another 17.3% ($359.7B) [4].
- **1.2% — Food and consumer goods' share:** just $25.1 billion — the bucket where toys and household goods actually live [4].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

The sourcing teams I'd expect to feel this first are the ones whose reshoring case was built on "tariffs make China uncompetitive." That case now depends on which sensitivity bucket each SKU lands in — and that's an open question, not a price.

- **The recommendation's timeline:** S&P Global's Chris Rogers flagged a real risk of delay — anywhere from a few weeks to the G20 in Miami in December [2]. A recommendation without a date is a planning variable, not a landed cost.
- **Sensitivity classification:** landed-cost models are starting to carry a "will this tariff still exist?" flag, because the relief half and the protected half no longer move together.
- **Where capital is already committed:** the tracker shows the money flowing to the defended half — semis, pharma, steel, tires — which is the opposite direction from the relief [4].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->

## The catch

None of this is settled. The $60 billion is a "consensus on recommendations," not an enacted cut — and Wendy Cutler noted the White House document doesn't even look open to revisions, with no date attached [1]. The bifurcation I'm describing is a direction, not a law. And I could be wrong that the two halves are causally linked: semiconductors and pharma may have been coming home regardless of any tariff split. The honest read is that relief and reshoring are moving in opposite directions, and a decision engine that ignores either is making a bet with one eye closed.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Washington recommended tariff relief on about $60 billion of "non-sensitive" goods — toys, household goods, holiday decorations — while reshoring capital keeps piling into the protected strategic half (semis, pharma, steel, tires).
- **Why It Matters:** the tariff regime is splitting in two, and a reshoring case built on "tariffs make China uncompetitive" now only holds for the goods Washington is actually defending — the hard, slow, expensive half.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The relief's fate:** whether a "consensus on recommendations" becomes an enacted cut — the timeline runs anywhere from a few weeks to the December G20.
  - **Sensitivity buckets:** whether sourcing teams add a "will this tariff survive?" flag to their landed-cost models.
  - **The concentration:** whether semiconductors keep their 67% share of $2.084 trillion while food and consumer goods sit at 1.2%.
- **The Catch:** it's a recommendation, not a law — and the investment concentration may reflect reshoring that was coming anyway, not a response to the tariff split.

I've been following the US–China tariff news all week, and one phrase stopped me: "non-sensitive goods." Washington just recommended relief on $60 billion of them — toys, household items, holiday decorations — with no reduction amount and no start date. The goods staying protected — steel, semiconductors, batteries, tires — are the ones where reshoring is a decade-long, nine-figure project. And the money is following the protected half: semiconductors alone are 67% of that $2 trillion, while food and consumer goods — the toy-and-household-goods bucket — are 1.2%.

My read: the tariff regime just split in two, and a reshoring case built on "tariffs make China uncompetitive" now only works for the heavy half. I could be wrong that the two are causally linked — semis and pharma might have been coming home anyway. But relief and reshoring are moving in opposite directions, and I'd want to know whether sourcing teams are flagging which sensitivity bucket their SKU sits in before they sign the plant.
