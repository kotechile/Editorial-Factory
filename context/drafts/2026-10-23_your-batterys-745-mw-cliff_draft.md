<!-- lead -->
On 6 October, home batteries plugged into California's grid pushed 745 megawatts (MW) into the evening peak. That was a national record, 28% above the 580 MW set on 9 September [1]. Then the money behind it reaches a cliff. The state program that pays for those dispatches closes its season on 31 October, and no new funds are set aside for 2027 [3].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The batteries did their job. Sunrun and Tesla let them run at the request of the California Energy Commission (CEC) and the utilities [1]. The trigger was a price signal: once the California Independent System Operator (CAISO) saw its next-day price pass $200 per megawatt-hour, the dispatch went ahead [1]. Sunrun alone has more than 120,000 home batteries enrolled [1].

What the record measures is a pool, not a home. The Demand Side Grid Support (DSGS) program pays the firm that bundles those batteries — the aggregator — a set sum per kilowatt (kW) of capacity it can show each month. The owner's share comes from somewhere else.

My read starts with the price list. A four-hour resource earns $9.00 per kW-month in May and $19.20 in September, then $10.50 in October, for a season total of $82.80 per kW [2]. A two-hour resource earns $62.10 [2]. Those are the aggregator's numbers, before this season's 30% bonus [2].

Then comes the sentence that decides what reaches a garage. "Storage VPP aggregators shall allocate incentive payments between the storage VPP aggregator and its participants pursuant to the terms and conditions agreed to between the VPP aggregator and participant" [2]. A virtual power plant (VPP) is the pool itself. The state prices one side of the trade and leaves the other side to a contract a homeowner signs once.

That split is why the record and the money are two stories. A battery's rating is what the pool can deliver, and the pool is what gets paid. An owner who reads "745 MW" sees proof of a working machine. The aggregator sees delivered capacity against a rate card it already knows how to price.

The timing widens the gap. The legislature put $27 million into the program in June to carry it through the end of the year [3]. The heat wave that produced the record may have used that up. The session then ended with no new money, leaving 2027 unclear [3]. The statute lets the program run to 2032 [2]. The cheque is written one season at a time.

Enrollment is no easier. The 2026 season is limited to storage aggregators that took part in October 2025, so a homeowner who bought a battery this year has no new door to walk through [2].

Eight days after the record, Pacific Gas and Electric (PG&E) sent next-day notices to about 70,000 customers across 29 counties [4]. A Public Safety Power Shutoff (PSPS) could run from 14 to 17 October. Of those customers, 3,920 are on Medical Baseline [4]. In that week the same battery was a capacity product and a backup appliance. The two jobs pay differently and fail differently.

**By the numbers:**

- **745 MW — One evening's record:** Home batteries delivered 669 MW on 5 October and 745 MW on 6 October, against 580 MW on 9 September and 539 MW in July 2025 [1].
- **$82.80 per kW — Season rate, paid to the aggregator:** The four-hour class, May through October, before the 30% bonus; the two-hour class totals $62.10 [2].
- **120,000 batteries — One aggregator's state fleet:** Sunrun's enrolled count, the number behind the record [1].
- **70,000 customers — The shutoff notice eight days later:** PG&E's 29-county PSPS warning for 14–17 October, with 3,920 on Medical Baseline [4].

<!-- tactical-insight -->

## What I'd watch:

- **The split, in writing.** The rate card is public. The household's share is not. I'm watching what DSGS owners say they receive against that $82.80 ceiling, and whether any aggregator prints the share before enrollment instead of after.
- **The reserve floor.** One live aggregator's terms cap each discharge at two hours, hold a 20% floor on the battery, and skip events during a power outage [10]. Those clauses decide whether an event night leaves a house with stored power. The program leaves them to the contract rather than the rulebook [2].
- **The 2027 cheque.** A program that pays for capacity and then stops leaves the hardware in place and the income gone. I'm watching for an early budget move, a state framework run by the California Public Utilities Commission (CPUC), or nothing at all [3][9].
- **The safety case, kept honest.** The Electric Power Research Institute (EPRI) published failure data for grid-scale batteries on 5 October. The headline is good news: the United States failure rate fell more than 97% from 2016 to 2025 while installs rose 250-fold [5][6]. EPRI also says these units now fail at a rate close to oil-filled substation transformers. Its lead author says the dataset is too small to model [6].

## The catch

The other side of this is stronger than it first looks. A battery bought for outages does not need a capacity payment to earn its keep. A payment program is not the reason to own one. Supporters cite a Brattle Group estimate of about two dollars of system value for every dollar spent. They also argue the October record proves the fleet shows up when called [3][9].

My read is narrower than the headline. The 97% figure covers commercial and grid sites, not the 13.5 kilowatt-hour box in a garage. The safety record quoted for home units belongs to a different group of machines run by different people [5][6]. And the home is the only side of this trade without a printed price. The state prices capacity to the aggregator, the aggregator prices the contract, and the owner pays in cycles.

The cleaner reading is that California proved a pool of home batteries can carry a grid-scale dispatch. It left the payment side half-built. Both things are true in the same season. That is why the next five weeks matter more than the last five.

<!-- tldr -->

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Home batteries in California delivered a record 745 MW into the grid on 6 October. The program that pays for those dispatches closes on 31 October with no new funds set aside for 2027.
- **Why It Matters:** The program pays the aggregator per kW of capacity and leaves the household's share to a private contract. The record tells you about the pool, not about the owner's return.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **The contract split:** The share of the $82.80 per kW season rate that reaches the owner, and whether it is printed before enrollment.
  - **The reserve floor:** Contract terms that cap discharge, hold a minimum charge, and carve out outages — the clauses that protect backup.
  - **The 2027 cheque:** Whether an early budget move or a new CPUC framework replaces the seasonal pool.
- **The Catch:** The 97% failure-rate decline covers grid-scale sites, not home units. The home is also the only side of the trade without a printed price.
