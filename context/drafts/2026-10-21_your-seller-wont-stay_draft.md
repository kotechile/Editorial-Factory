<!-- lead -->
One automated forklift costs $100,000 to $200,000, and Seegrid says its next warehouse model will sell for about half the price of the factory machine it offers today [1][2]. Six days before that report, Symbotic bought its third automation company this year, in a deal it described as a product to fold into a system rather than a service business to inherit [3][4]. The robots are getting cheaper. The seller behind them won't stay the same.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The price is falling for a plain reason. Warehouse labor costs less than factory labor, so the same machine has to clear a lower bar to pay for itself. Labor runs 50% to 65% of warehouse operating costs [2]. That is enough to justify a robot. It is not enough to justify a factory-priced one.

The seller list is shrinking for a plainer reason. Most automated forklift projects are still "small, more like pilots" [2]. Most buyers "start with a handful of trucks" before reaching dozens, if they scale at all [2]. Firms that stay small get bought or leave the market. Symbotic has been buying the parts: forklifts for trailer docks, warehouse software, and now sortation [3][5].

What strikes me is that both stories land on the same invoice, one line above the other. The unit price is the line a buyer can see. It is also the line that keeps improving. The service relationship is the line almost nobody prices, and it is the line being reassigned.

## By the numbers

- **$100,000 to $200,000 — Price per robot:** what one automated forklift system costs upfront, with hardware, software and integration included, per Grand View Research [1].
- **About half — Warehouse model plan:** the price Seegrid says its early-2027 warehouse model will carry against its manufacturing machine [2].
- **2,500-plus — Robots deployed:** Seegrid's fleet across hundreds of facilities and 50-plus customers [2].
- **$5.9B to $14.5B — Market growth:** Grand View's forecast for the autonomous forklift market from 2026 to 2033, a 13.7% compound annual rate [1].

## What I'd watch:

Three details decide whether the cheaper machine is also the cheaper decision.

- **Who signs the service agreement:** Symbotic says cellumation keeps its own name, its Bremen headquarters, its founders and its customers [3][4]. That is a brand arrangement, not a contract term. No release says what happens to an operator's maintenance deal if a later purchase folds cellumation in.
- **Whether the half-price machine ships on time:** the early-2027 date and the half-price target come from Seegrid's chief sales officer, not from a price list [2]. A vendor cutting price to reach a cheaper labor market is also a vendor choosing what to leave out.
- **Whether the pilots become fleets:** a market of 2,500 robots spread across hundreds of facilities is a market of small deployments [2]. What I'd want is the count of sites running dozens of trucks, not the count running a handful.

There is a second reading, and it is friendlier to the buyer than my first. Consolidation can improve service. A larger owner has more field technicians, more spare parts and a longer balance sheet than a startup does. The question for a buyer is not whether the seller stays independent. It is whether the service level survives the sale.

## The catch

The part I keep circling is Cutter & Buck, the strongest case against my worry. It opened a second automated distribution center in Grand Prairie, Texas, this month. The grid holds 50,502 storage bins, runs 32 AutoStore robots and 13 ports, and was built to move 1,256 bins an hour [6][7]. A simulation averaged 1,284 [6][7].

Its first grid went live in Renton, Washington, in July 2023. That one held more than 34,000 bins and 54 robots, and was later expanded by 9,962 bins [6]. The operator stayed with the same platform and the same integrator, because the first project worked. Then it used years of operating data to design the second site differently [6][7]. That is what vendor continuity looks like when it delivers.

The counterweight is the sellers' own arithmetic. Seegrid's chief sales officer estimates that more than 80% of automation-only startups will not survive as standalone companies [2]. "Standalone" is the word that matters, because being bought is a survival route. Fox Robotics, an autonomous forklift maker for trailer docks, took that route in February when Symbotic bought it [3][5]. A buyer who installed a Fox fleet in 2025 now owns a fleet with a new road map. That road map belongs to a company that also sells sortation and storage systems.

Symbotic has not published comparative performance figures for the technology it just bought [5]. That is not a knock on the deal. It is a note on what a buyer can check in year one.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Automated forklifts cost $100,000 to $200,000 a unit. Seegrid says a warehouse version due in early 2027 will carry about half that price. In the same week, Symbotic bought its third automation company of the year, adding sortation to a system that already covers forklifts and warehouse software.
- **Why It Matters:** The price is the part of a payback a buyer can watch fall. The company that services the asset, supplies the parts and writes the software is the part being consolidated. That tie is far harder to renegotiate once the fleet is on the floor.
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether the service tie survives the sale, and whether the cheaper machine arrives as promised.
  - **Service agreement assignment:** who is named as obligated if the vendor is acquired, and what a buyer can claim when the answer changes.
  - **Warehouse model pricing:** whether early 2027 brings a real price list at about half the manufacturing price, or a stripped setup.
  - **Fleet-to-pilot ratio:** whether sites move from a handful of trucks to dozens, which is where a payback either appears or stalls.
- **The Catch:** The half-price plan is a vendor forecast, not a quote. Consolidation cuts both ways, and Cutter & Buck's second grid shows that staying with one vendor can work.
