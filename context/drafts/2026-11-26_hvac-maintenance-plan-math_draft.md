<!-- lead -->
Because labor rates keep climbing, the contractor's HVAC (heating, ventilation and air conditioning) maintenance plan is now a service agreement that pre-pays tune-ups and discounts repairs for less than one unscheduled visit. Sign it and you have bought clean coils, a warranty record and first place in the dispatch queue; skip it and you pay retail each spring and hope your own calendar reminder arrives on time.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
A maintenance agreement is prepaid labor, and it sits under the cost of one visit. This piece gives homeowners the price anchors, the four clauses that decide whether the plan pays, and the honest case for staying unenrolled.

<!-- tension -->
## What you are prepaying
Maintenance pricing has moved one way for years: up, and the climb is labor. A single tune-up visit now carries a flat fee between $75 and $200, depending on the contractor [1]. The national average for a one-off call sits at $275, and most services land between $150 and $350 [2]. Labor drives nearly all of that bill, because hourly service rates run $75 to $150 and the technician's own median wage was $29.33 an hour in 2025 [3]. The visit itself is mostly inspection and cleaning on a schedule the contractor already knows: filter swaps, coil cleaning, drain-line work, refrigerant checks [2].

An agreement restructures that same visit as a subscription. Contractors report that people on the plan get priority scheduling, reduced fees and repair discounts, and some will call you to book so the checkup never slips your mind [1]. Cost guides put the average annual agreement at $75–$150, with a two-visit plan running $150–$300 [1]. Pricing databases reach the same conclusion from the other side: signing up costs more upfront but usually saves money over time, especially on an older system [2]. What I find hard to overstate is how much of the agreement's value is simply the queue — the plan is part work and part standing in line.

## By the numbers
- **$275 average visit:** the national mean for a one-off HVAC maintenance call, per Fixr's 2026 pricing survey [2].
- **$150 a year or less:** what an annual service agreement typically costs, per This Old House [1].
- **$29.33 an hour:** the 2025 median wage of the technician whose labor the plan pre-pays [3].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## Four clauses decide the math
Four things decide whether a renewal sheet is worth your signature.
1. **Count the visits:** a two-visit agreement costs $150 to $300 [1], which only makes sense where your climate truly runs heat and cooling hard.
2. **Price the repair discount:** even a small share on parts — a coil cleaning alone runs up to $350 [2] — is where the contractor's margin hides.
3. **Require a written record:** the plan should issue a service record after each visit, because warranty and home-warranty claims need proof of regular maintenance before they pay out [1].
4. **Run the counter-math:** filters and a can of coil cleaner cost about a tenth of a plan, so the agreement earns its fee only when you will not do that work yourself.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch
The plan's value depends on a behavior, not a price. What I keep coming back to is that enrollment mainly changes whether the visit happens at all. Skip the filter changes and let the outdoor unit collect leaves, and no agreement saves the compressor. Our piece on the [11-degree delta-T that killed a $7,500 compressor quote](/11-degrees-kills-the-compressor-quote/) shows a $180 valve adjustment fixing the problem the agreement was supposed to prevent. If you are the homeowner who books service on time and does the cheap tasks, buy the leanest agreement that includes a written record. If you are not, the plan is still the cheapest way to make the maintenance happen.
