<!-- lead -->
Yarbo (a maker of modular yard robots) will finance its full four-module bundle at $774.92 a month for a year [1]. The landscaping crew it is sold to replace bills a one-acre lawn just $300 to $520 a month [2]. So the vendor's own financing terms put year one of owning above year one of hiring. That result is not a critique of the machine; it is math from two printed prices. Call the monthly payment $775 a month, and the gap is the whole story. A buyer weighing the swap deserves to see that number against the crew's invoice before signing anything.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

Yarbo's checkout and a lawn-care price guide both print a monthly number for the same one-acre yard. The robot's monthly payment is the larger one, and payback waits for year two.

<!-- tension -->
## Why year one loses on paper

The two documents were never meant to meet. Yarbo's storefront, fetched November 21, lists the top bundle at a regular price of $9,299 [1]. That bundle pairs the Y40 Core (the robot's wheeled base unit) with its snow blower, mower and blower modules, and the page finances it at 0% APR (annual percentage rate) — $774.92 a month for a year [1]. MowBook's lawn-care pricing guide, refreshed on November 3, prices a one-acre lawn at $75 to $130 per weekly visit [2]. Four of those visits sum to $300–$520 on the guide's own monthly billing line [2]. I find the collision hard to square: the checkout prints both numbers, and the monthly payment alone states that hiring is cheaper for the first year.

One fairness note before the count hardens. MowBook's monthly figure is mowing-season billing, four weekly visits [2]. The robot's payment runs flat across all twelve months instead. Even granting that mismatch, the robot's payment still tops the crew's whole monthly bill. Yarbo's site is also mid-Black-Friday sale right now, and some product pages print lower sale prices against the same regular prices [1]. I have used the regular ladder throughout; the sale prices narrow the gap but do not close it.

## By the numbers

- **$774.92 a month:** The printed 12-month monthly payment on Yarbo's full bundle at 0% APR [1].
- **$520 monthly:** The top of MowBook's quoted range for a one-acre lawn on monthly billing [2].
- **$7,299 entry bundle:** Yarbo's cheapest ladder rung — the core, snow blower and blower, without the mower [1].

## What I'd watch

The payback story depends on what the machine replaces, and the owner record is mixed on that point. An owner review posted in November 2026, drawn from Yarbo users, reports owners clearing 80 to 90 percent of snow storms [3]. The same review records that the robot does not clear the street-plow berm left at the end of the driveway [3]. In my read, the snow module partly replaces a plow service, not fully — a buyer still pays for the heavy pushes.

The bundle ladder is the other thing worth watching. Yarbo prices the entry bundle at $7,299 and the mower-added tier at $8,199, before the $9,299 top rung [1]. Each step up the ladder adds features and cost. That ladder is built to make the mower module look like the cheap add-on. Yet each rung raises the payment a buyer locks into.

I'd also want to know whether the current sale prices hold through the shipping window: Yarbo prints a sale price on its 4-in-1 bundle page. The Core itself ships "Late Nov – Early Dec 2026"; other items leave in days [1]. A robot financed now may mow nothing until the core lands. Timing, not price, is the open question on the ladder.

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch

My read is that the purchase only pays if two conditions hold at once. The machine has to survive into year two and later, and it has to replace both the mowing contract and the plow visits. Miss either one and the monthly math never closes, because a partial replacement stretches the payback that the 0% APR framing hides. The honest buyer's question is not whether the robot works, but whether one machine can absorb two separate bills.
