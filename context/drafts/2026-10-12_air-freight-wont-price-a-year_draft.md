<!-- lead -->

Air freight stopped pricing a year at a time. Sixty percent of new air capacity contracts effective in the third quarter ran three months or less, against 25% a year earlier, and the share of twelve-month deals fell from 40% to 25% [1]. That number changes the buffer maths for anyone holding safety stock, because the expedite option is what lets a buffer stay thin. Days later, the space to hold that buffer tightened at its fastest rate since March 2022 [2]. Both sides of the trade went off the menu at once.

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The safety-stock decision has always been a comparison of two prices. One is the cost of holding stock. The other is the cost of buying speed when the stock runs short. For years both numbers were stable enough to put in a spreadsheet and defend in a budget review.

The first leg is the one everybody watches. Xeneta's air freight benchmarking data shows spot rates averaging $3.10 a kilogram in September, up 27% on the year, on demand growth of 6% against capacity growth of just 2% [1]. Capacity is the tighter side of that market, and carriers can charge accordingly.

The more telling shift is in the term, not the rate. Three-month agreements alone made up 42% of new contracts, up from 16% a year earlier. Deals longer than twelve months have almost vanished, at 3%. Shippers are asking for floating formulas, a base rate adjusted to the market, rather than a fixed annual number [1].

What strikes me here is that the second leg moved against the planner at the same time, and from the physical side rather than the financial one. The September Logistics Managers' Index (LMI, a diffusion survey of supply chain managers where any reading above 50 means expansion) came in at 70.2, with inventory levels up 6.1 points to 58.9 [2]. The build is running upstream: goods sitting at the wholesale tier read 61.4 against 53.8 downstream, and the release says most of that stock has not reached retailers yet.

The storage market answered that. Warehousing capacity fell 14.2 points to 39.3, a contraction, and the fastest one for that metric since March 2022, when supply chains stopped dead after the invasion of Ukraine [2]. Warehousing prices read 73.5. Aggregate logistics costs hit 246.1 on a 0-to-300 scale where 150 is break-even, the highest since April 2022.

Put the two reads together and the shape of the problem is clear. Stock is being rebuilt at the tier that is furthest from the customer, just as the cheapest place to keep it gets scarce. And the fallback that would justify keeping less of it can no longer be bought on a fixed annual rate [1][2]. A planner who built the safety-stock case on a stable air rate and a stable warehouse rate has lost both inputs, in the same fortnight, without either market making a loud noise about it.

## By the numbers

- **60% — Air deals now three months or less:** New air capacity contracts effective in the third quarter ran three months at most, against 25% in the same quarter a year earlier and 47% in the second quarter of 2026 [1].
- **$3.10 a kilogram — Spot air rate, up 27%:** September air cargo spot rates rose 27% year on year, with demand up 6% against capacity up 2%, lifting the load factor to 62% [1].
- **39.3 — Warehousing capacity, in contraction:** The LMI storage-capacity reading fell 14.2 points, the fastest contraction since March 2022, while inventory costs rose 1.3 points to 79.9 [2].
- **246.1 — Aggregate logistics costs:** That reading sits on a 0-to-300 scale where 150 is break-even, and it is the highest since April 2022. Transportation prices read 92.7 [2].

## What I'd watch:

The teams closest to this are not re-running the safety-stock formula. They are deciding what they can commit to while both inputs float. Here is what I am watching.

- **Contract structure over rate level:** I am watching whether buyers keep chasing a fixed annual air rate that the market has stopped selling, or shift to an index formula with a defined adjustment. Van de Wouw's line is the tell: a one-year fixed rate "doesn't fit the current conditions" [1][3].
- **Where the buffer sits:** The build is showing up upstream, at the wholesale tier, while the pull to retail typically happens in mid-October [2]. If storage stays tight, I would expect pressure to move buffer stock to nodes with cheaper space rather than to cut total units.
- **The fuel rail on the expedite leg:** The published North American air freight fuel surcharge is $0.604 a pound, set from jet fuel at $3.899 a gallon, with freight fuel on diesel at $0.332 a pound [4]. That line is indexed, so it moves with the same market the capacity contracts are floating against.
- **The inbound surge:** The Port of Los Angeles expects containerised imports up roughly 50% to 55% year on year across the last week of September and the first two weeks of October [2]. That volume has to land somewhere, and warehousing utilisation is already at 64.3.

## The catch

My read is that this is a pricing problem, not yet a shortage. The LMI is a survey of what managers expect, not a warehouse lease rate, and a diffusion index above 50 can sit there for months while actual space still clears at a price.

The expedite case can also survive a floating rate. If a team wants speed, an index formula does what a fixed rate did, as long as both sides agree on the index. The real loss is budget certainty, not the option itself.

The part I keep circling is that the two markets did not move together. Air capacity is short because demand outran supply and jet fuel roughly doubled from its pre-conflict level [1][3]. Warehousing tightened because goods arrived. Those are different causes with the same effect on a planner, and either could reverse on its own.

One more caution on the stock side. Business inventories were already 3.8% above a year earlier in July, the strongest monthly build since March, and merchant wholesalers held $958.9 billion of it [5]. That is the balance-sheet shape the buffer shows up in, and it says the refill started before this window, not because of it.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** The two prices behind every safety-stock decision stopped being fixed at the same time. New air capacity contracts were mostly three months or less in the third quarter, and warehousing capacity contracted at its fastest rate since March 2022 [1][2].
- **Why It Matters:** The expedite option is what lets a buffer stay thin, and the storage the buffer sits in is now the scarce input. A planning model fed a fixed annual air rate and a stable warehouse rate is budgeting against numbers the market no longer quotes.
- **What I'd Watch:**
  - **Contract structure:** Whether buyers accept an index formula instead of a fixed annual air rate the market has stopped selling [1].
  - **Buffer location:** Whether stock moves to cheaper nodes rather than shrinking, now that the build is sitting upstream at the wholesale tier [2].
  - **The inbound surge:** Port of Los Angeles imports are forecast up roughly 50% to 55% year on year, and warehousing utilisation is already 64.3 [2].
- **The Catch:** The index is a survey of expectations, not a lease rate, and an index formula restores flexibility even when it costs budget certainty. The two markets tightened for different reasons, so either can loosen on its own [1][2].
