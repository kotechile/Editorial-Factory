Your 49% cut was actually 9%. Helen of Troy, the company behind Hydro Flask and Osprey, was reported this week as having cut inventory 49% from a year earlier, and its own filed results put the figure at $480.3 million against $528.9 million, which is a drop of $48.6 million, or 9.2% [1][4]. The same quarter also booked $26.9 million of tariff refunds, and roughly $23 million of that had been spent again before the period closed [1].

<!-- tension -->

## The big picture:

The refund is the part worth a closer read, because of where it lands. Helen of Troy put it against its cost of goods sold, which pushed gross margin up to 52.2% from 44.2% on sales of $440.9 million [1].

Take the $26.9 million back out and gross profit falls to about $203.2 million, or 46.1% of sales. That is my sum on the company's own figures, not a number it reported, and it leaves a real gain of 1.9 points rather than eight [1].

The company is plain about where the money goes. Its outlook holds about $80.5 million of refunds across three claims phases, and it plans to put 83% to 88% of that back into brand spending, internal projects and inventory composition, for an estimated $10 million to $14 million of pre-tax income [1].

Inventory composition is the phrase to hold onto. Of the $26.9 million booked in the quarter, just $0.8 million cut the value of stock on hand, and $4.3 million was still sitting as a receivable at the close [2].

So the stock did not fall because the refund came in. The refund bought a budget to re-mix the stock, and the stock fell $48.6 million on its own account.

The counter-example sits in the same window and looks nothing like it. Fastenal grew sales 15.5% in the quarter and ended it with stock 3.1% above the year before, which is the ratio gain every planner says they want, and it had no refund credit to help [3].

Now follow the other two legs. Receivables rose 19.9%, payables rose 29.3%, and net trade working capital rose 8.1% [3]. Cash from operations fell to 105.0% of net income from 115.3% a year earlier, and the nine-month figure slipped to 94.8% from 96.2% [3].

Fastenal's own note is that growth with larger customers, who tend to take longer to pay, pulled cash into receivables [3]. The stock gain was real, and the customer's terms took a large share of it back.

That is the clash I keep coming back to. One firm improved its stock figure with a credit it had already promised to spend, and the other improved its stock ratio and still handed the cash to its customers and its suppliers. Neither got free cash off the shelf.

## By the numbers

- **6.1 points — Refund's share of the margin gain:** The $26.9 million credit against $440.9 million of sales, inside an 8.0-point rise in gross margin [1].
- **$0.8 million — Refund that touched stock:** The part of the $26.9 million booked that cut the value of goods on hand, with $4.3 million left as a receivable [2].
- **3.1% — Fastenal's stock growth:** Twelve-month change in stock against 15.5% growth in sales, with receivables up 19.9% and payables up 29.3% [3].
- **94.8% — Cash from operations, nine months:** The share of net income turned into cash, down from 96.2% in the same stretch, even with the stock gains the release credits [3].

## What I'd watch:

The first thing I would ask for is the spending log, because a plan to put 83% to 88% of a refund back into the business is not the same as having spent it. Helen of Troy spent about $23 million of the $26.9 million inside the quarter, which is quick, and the rest of the claims arrive by the end of the fiscal year [1].

- **Where the last phase lands:** The company was told in August 2026 that it could file its third set of claims, and it has not been paid on them yet [2]. The $80.5 million is an estimate, so the share headed for the stock mix is not fixed.
- **The bin count, not the dollar count:** Fastenal ended the quarter with 143,878 weighed vending and scanner sites, up 7.4%, and signings up 6.2% on a daily-sales basis [3]. A rhythm that refills 143,878 sites is a skill; a year-end mix reset is an event.
- **The receivable line in the next filing:** Receivables at Fastenal grew three times faster than stock, and management tied it to the big accounts driving the growth [3]. That is a price cut wearing a sales win's clothes.
- **Whether the refund goes back to margin next quarter:** A credit against cost of goods sold lifts the gross margin line, and the next phase of claims will do the same. The mix question is whether the stock that stays sells faster than the stock that left.

## The catch

My read is that the case rests on how long it lasts, and it cuts both ways. If the mix reset drops dead lines for good, the gain holds, because the write-off changes what the next plan can order [1].

The refund is also real money, and asking how long a gain lasts is not the same as calling it fake. Helen of Troy cut total debt to $672.6 million from $893.2 million and lifted six-month cash from operations to $56.5 million from $47.9 million [1].

Fastenal's numbers need their own caveat. Its stock balance is a twelve-month change, while the 15.5% is a three-month sales change, so the two do not share a base, and stock rose in dollars rather than fell [3].

There is a harder limit under both. Tariff refunds come once, and the firms treating them as a stock budget will find the budget gone in a year, while the refill rhythm they never changed is still setting their days of supply.

## At a glance

- **The Big Shift:** Helen of Troy's stock drop was reported as 49% and is 9.2%, or $48.6 million, in its own filing, in the same quarter that a $26.9 million tariff refund lifted gross margin to 52.2% from 44.2% [1][4]. Fastenal, in the same window, grew sales 15.5% with stock up only 3.1% and still saw cash from operations fall [3].
- **Why It Matters:** Working capital is set across three lines, and lifting one of them with a one-time credit or with a customer's payment terms does not put cash in the bank. The test is whether the gain survives the quarter the credit stops.
- **What I'd Watch:** Whether the refund changes the stock or just the margin, and whether anything changes how often a site refills.
  - **The last claims phase:** The final and largest slice of the refund, still unfiled, which sets how much budget the mix reset really has [2].
  - **Bin sites:** Weighed vending and scanner sites that refill on their own, at 143,878 and growing 7.4%, which is the closest thing to a rhythm a filing will show you [3].
  - **Receivables:** The line where a sales win and a soft payment term look the same until the cash arrives [3].
- **The Catch:** A refund is real cash, and a true mix reset does hold if dead lines stay dropped. The risk is the second year, when the cheque is gone and the refill rhythm is unchanged.
