The same winter now bills a household $686 or $2,977, and the only thing that picks is the fuel the house already burns [1]. On November 10 the National Energy Assistance Directors Association (NEADA), the group of state officials who run federal energy aid, released its winter forecast and priced the season by fuel [1]. A natural-gas household pays $686, exactly as it did last year. A heating-oil household pays $2,977, which is 70.2 percent more than last winter [1]. The average behind those numbers is $1,002, up 7.1 percent, and it is nearly useless to plan with, because it blends bills that sit 4.3 times apart.

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance
NEADA's November 10 forecast prices the same winter by fuel, from $686 for natural gas to $2,977 for oil. For homeowners, the season's cost depends less on what they buy than on which fuel their invoice names.

<!-- tension -->
## How the forecast moved
I read the two NEADA releases side by side, and the swing between them is the story. The September cut put the season at $1,030 per household, up 8.7 percent [3]. The November revision walks the average back to $1,002, yet re-prices one fuel hard [1]. Oil jumps 70.2 percent while gas holds flat and electricity adds just 6.0 percent [1].

The mechanism is supply, not the thermostat. Heating oil tracks global diesel markets, and NEADA names the Strait of Hormuz, the chokepoint for seaborne crude, as the reason it refuses to call the recent decline a trend [1]. The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA), the federal statistics office, shows the same caution in its weekly data: home heating oil slipped 1.5 percent to $6.14 a gallon in the week ending November 2, and still sat about 69 percent above the same week a year earlier [2]. Electricity climbs for a separate reason — rising rates, data-center demand and grid upgrades — and that line is up 32.1 percent since the winter of 2021–22, against 110.1 percent for oil [1]. So the oil household faces a season bill that can pass a major repair, while the electric household's winter costs $69 more than last year's [1]. The Northeast, where most oil-heated homes sit, takes the sharpest regional hit: $1,532 per household, up 16.9 percent [1].

## By the numbers
- **$1,002:** the all-fuel average season bill, up 7.1 percent, blending bills 4.3 times apart [1].
- **$6.14 a gallon:** residential heating oil in the week ending November 2, down 1.5 percent but 69 percent above a year earlier [1].
- **$23 billion:** what households behind on utility payments already owe — one in six of them [1].

## Where the season lands on you
The people closest to the bills are not waiting for December to settle this. NEADA's directors are planning for the bad case, asking Congress for an added $3 billion for the Low Income Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP), the federal bill-help fund [1]. Their reasoning is blunt: states should assume prices stay high all season [1].

For the household, the fuel list orders itself. An oil-heated home now faces a season that rivals a repair in cost, so the cheap acts earn real money: the annual service visit, sealed leaks, a strict thermostat schedule [1]. A gas household's bill is flat, so the savings a heat pump was sold on do not exist on that invoice yet [1]. What I am watching next is the weekly EIA series, because it re-answers the oil question every Monday. If the $6.14 gallon holds through December, the oil forecast stops being a scare number and becomes the budget. ## The catch in the number
What I would not do is treat $2,977 as a price you can fight. It is a projection: a guessed volume of oil at a guessed price. NEADA's own release shows how soft those guesses are, citing prices that fell after its base week [1]. The honest comparison sits on your own meter: last winter's actual gallons at this winter's price. The 2026–27 lesson is narrower than the headline. The fuel mix you inherited decided most of this winter for you, and every other choice on the table is small against it.
