<!-- lead -->
A crack in a foundation wall is a measurement problem before it is a repair problem, and the measurement the guides skip is time. The width a homeowner is told to fear — one-eighth of an inch — is not a standard's number: reinforced concrete under service loads is allowed crack widths from 0.004 to 0.016 inches depending on exposure [3]. The ladder that does decide is BRE Digest 251, the UK Building Research Establishment's classification of crack damage: cracks up to 5 mm (three-sixteenths of an inch) are "aesthetic" and need only redecoration, mid-range widths affect weathertightness, and only the widest class is structural [1][2]. The stakes of getting the call wrong run from $250 to $800 for repairing a single crack [4] to the $5,179 average foundation-repair bill [5].

<!-- tldr -->
## At a glance

This is the decision a homeowner faces the day a crack appears: pay an engineer for a verdict, or measure and watch the crack instead. The width alone settles nothing; direction and change over time do.

<!-- tension -->
## The width alone never was the diagnosis

BRE Digest 251 sorts crack damage into six categories, and the width number changes meaning at each one. Categories 0 through 2 — crack widths up to 5 mm — are "aesthetic" issues that need only redecoration [1]. Category 3, widths of 5–15 mm, affects weathertightness and service pipes and may need brickwork repointed [2]. Category 4, widths of 15–25 mm, is where frames distort and walls lean or bulge; category 5, beyond 25 mm, is the first class the digest calls a stability problem, where beams lose bearing and the structure is generally unsafe [2]. The same digest warns against classifying damage on width alone, because a stair-stepped or offset crack says more than its narrowest point does [2].

Against that ladder, the folklore threshold looks arbitrary. The one-eighth-inch line that repair-company guides quote as "cause for concern" [4] sits far above the 0.004-to-0.016-inch widths the American Concrete Institute's service-load standard treats as acceptable [3]. My read is that the gap explains most of the panic in the first week: a homeowner applies a repair vendor's selling number to a slab and block wall the standard never judged. The vendor number is not a standard — it is a marketing line that happens to rhyme with one.

## By the numbers

- **$800 per crack:** The top of the $250-to-$800 band to repair a crack when the wall is otherwise structurally sound [4].
- **$1,000:** The top of the $300-to-$1,000 band for a structural engineer's report [4].
- **25 mm:** The width at which Digest 251's category 4 ends — frames distorted, walls leaning — and the stability class begins [2].

<!-- tactical-insight -->
## How the people who price this actually call it

The trades that live on these numbers behave like the ladder exists. Cost guides price the decision in bands, not in verdicts: crack repair at $250 to $800 per crack, bowed-wall reinforcement at $5,000 to $15,000, and sinking-foundation work at $4,500 to $20,000 [4]. Those bands only make sense if someone distinguishes a dormant cosmetic crack from an actively bowing wall — which is exactly the distinction the engineer's $300-to-$1,000 report buys [4]. In my read, the report is the cheap option the moment the crack's class is uncertain: it is priced like a fraction of one repair band, and the bands it selects between differ by an order of magnitude.

What the consumer-facing guidance converges on is change over time. This Old House's own decision framing holds that the most important factor is not size alone but whether a crack is stable or changing, especially alongside water intrusion or sticking doors [5]. That is why practitioners date-stamp: a pencil mark at each end of the crack and a re-read weeks later turns a width into a trend. The trend is the datum the ladder's categories were built on — damage that recurs or widens moves up the scale, damage that sits still does not [2].

<!-- nuanced-takeaway -->
## The catch

The ladder is an engineering classification, not a legal one, and it does not travel unchanged to American framing. Digest 251 was written for low-rise brick-and-block buildings; a poured-concrete or block foundation reads its categories sensibly, but a buyer in a US insurance dispute will find that the width a US guide quotes, not the digest, is what an adjuster answers with. The honest framing is that the homeowner's first instrument costs a pencil and a calendar, and the second costs $300 to $1,000 [4] — and nothing in the ladder shortens the waiting. I find that harder to accept than any number in it: the monitoring discipline asks a homeowner to live with an open question, which is exactly why so many pay the report fee instead.
